## Verification status

The blockade, late-November military action, diplomatic escalation and March 1839 treaty are well supported by official Mexican summaries and the treaty text located. The pastry incident’s original wording, Remontel’s occupation, the amount claimed, the complete French demand schedule, the legal status of individual claims, and exact military dates still require primary-source verification.

## Uncertainty register

### 1. Did the pastry incident occur as later told?

AGN confirms a modern archival narrative about a French Tacubaya resident whose pastry shop was looted during a disturbance. S3, S6 and S7 preserve competing versions, including a later account of an 800-peso claim and press inflation to 30,000 pesos. The original Remontel/Gros file and earliest press account have not been inspected. Treat the incident as a later-attested grievance, not a settled eyewitness reconstruction.

### 2. What was Remontel’s business and what happened there?

Sources vary between *fondero* / fonda, restaurant and pastry shop, and between looting, unpaid consumption, or both. Do not call the actors “Mexican soldiers” as a proven fact without the primary source; use “later accounts associate the incident with troops connected to Santa Anna” if the claim must be mentioned at all.

### 3. Which amount is correct?

The SRE history gives 800 pesos; later press-derived accounts give 30,000, while global popular retellings often give 60,000. These may be distinct claims, translations, later exaggerations or conflations. Human review must inspect the original diplomatic file and contemporary newspapers before any number advances to narration.

### 4. What did the 600,000 pesos represent?

The 9 March 1839 convention says 600,000 pesos satisfied French claims for losses suffered before 26 November 1838. It does not list each claimant in the text located. Keep aggregate settlement, accepted pre-existing credits, and disputed indemnity claims separate. Do not call the whole amount a pastry debt.

### 5. Was Mexico legally liable?

S3 reports a Mexican position that foreigners shared the risks of internal disorder and that the state was not responsible for all disturbance losses; French diplomacy invoked international law and reciprocity. The treaty resolves the dispute politically, while some questions were sent to arbitration. The record located does not amount to a neutral legal ruling on each claim.

### 6. What exactly did the ultimatum demand?

S3’s footnote 10 summarises 600,000 pesos, forced-loan exemption, retail commerce, and removal of officials; S4 is the Mexican-printed ultimatum/correspondence volume. The complete scan has not been transcribed. Verify exact amounts, deadlines, officials, legal language and whether the claim list changed between March and late 1838.

### 7. When did the blockade and attack occur?

S3 dates the Veracruz blockade from 16 April 1838 and gives 28 November for the capture of San Juan de Ulúa; S1’s AGN summary refers to 27 November. These may reflect separate stages or source conventions. Check French naval logs, Mexican reports, British dispatches and the treaty record before fixing a day-by-day sequence.

### 8. What was the role of Britain?

S3 describes Pakenham’s late-January arrival, British commercial losses and mediation; S8 points to Foreign Office and Admiralty records. “British-brokered peace” is a useful lead but requires a precise account of whether Britain mediated formally, informally, or mainly applied commercial pressure.

### 9. How did the label spread?

S6 argues that Mexican press satire constructed or magnified the pastry claim, while S1 records the modern official label. The earliest newspaper occurrence and the transition from satire to standard historical name remain unverified. Do not claim that the name was coined by one person or at one date.

### 10. Were there additional human and material costs?

S3 records blockade damage, the capture of Ulúa, Veracruz fighting, expulsion and Santa Anna’s injury, but this pass has not checked casualty rolls, ship reports, property assessments or expulsion records. Avoid exact casualty totals and avoid presenting national victory narratives as neutral outcomes.

## Legal, ethical and editorial risk

No living-person defamation issue is apparent. The principal risks are historical overstatement, turning a negotiated settlement into proof of liability, repeating the 30,000/60,000 figures without provenance, asserting military looting as fact, and reducing Mexican or French civilians to national stereotypes. Human review is mandatory before title, thumbnail, script, archive-image reuse or publication.

## Stop condition

Stop research-to-script handoff until the ultimatum and Mexican reply are read, the Remontel claim is source-checked, the amount discrepancy is explained or explicitly preserved, the aggregate 600,000-peso settlement is separated from individual claims, the French-side records are consulted, and the blockade/Veracruz chronology is verified.
