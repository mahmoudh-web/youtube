## Provisional assessment

The defensible story is narrower and stranger than the popular headline. Britain built a wartime biological-warfare capability, received an institutional go-ahead to produce contaminated cattle feed according to the accessible PBS chronology, and tested related anthrax weapon concepts on Gruinard Island. The cattle-feed plan was never implemented against Germany. The historical consequence that can be documented most confidently is not a German epidemic but the decades-long contamination, review and remediation of the British test site.

The authorisation story is not yet a clean one-person decision. The public record points to a December 1941 memo and January 1942 Defence Committee decision, while released-file reporting frames the work as defensive preparation and possible retaliation. The exact minutes, conditions of use and later cancellation record have not been inspected in this pass. “Churchill’s anthrax plan” is therefore unsafe shorthand unless a human verifies the underlying records and distinguishes the cattle-feed project from separate 1944 anthrax-bomb orders.

Gruinard’s official record is more detailed than the usual “forbidden island” line. Hansard shows that in 1983 the government was still reviewing effectiveness, cost and ecological effects after field trials. In 1987 an independent advisory group judged that the decontamination had worked well but required an agricultural-use demonstration before return to civil ownership. A later UK Parliament answer says the island was declared safe by an independent advisory group before public confirmation in 1990 and was then sold back to the original owner’s estate. The record supports a staged verification process, not a single cleanup date that erased every question.

## What the pack can currently establish

- Porton Down’s biology department and Paul Fildes were central to Britain’s wartime biological-warfare research.
- A public institutional chronology reports a 2 January 1942 Defence Committee go-ahead for production of the cattle-feed plan.
- The plan’s intended target was German agricultural livestock; its operational mechanics and counterfactual human consequences are outside this pack.
- Gruinard hosted anthrax field trials in 1942–43 within the wider programme.
- Britain did not deploy Operation Vegetarian against Germany, and the pack contains no evidence of German exposure to the cattle-feed stockpile.
- A later review reports about five million cakes were produced and destroyed in stages, but exact production/disposal records require human checking.
- A separate 1944 anthrax-bomb order should not be conflated with the cattle-feed plan.
- Dstl dates Gruinard’s main decontamination to 1986; Parliament records continuing advisory review, agricultural-use demonstration and public safety confirmation before/around 1990.
- The exact primary authorisation, cancellation, stockpile and scientific-verification records remain open.

## Editorial interpretation, clearly labelled

Operation Vegetarian is best framed as a story about how a state turns fear of biological attack into a capability, how testing can leave a physical legacy, and how “safe” becomes an evidentiary and political conclusion rather than a magic reset. That interpretation is grounded in the sequence of authorisation leads, non-deployment, decades of contamination review and the later advisory process. It is not a claim that officials in 1942 could predict the full cost or duration of the legacy.

The human editorial value lies in resisting two distortions at once: sensational claims that Britain used anthrax to kill millions, and sanitised claims that Gruinard was simply a technical success. The sources support preparation, testing, secrecy, non-use and a long remediation record. They do not support operational reconstruction, a counterfactual casualty total or a triumphant “war-winning” story.
