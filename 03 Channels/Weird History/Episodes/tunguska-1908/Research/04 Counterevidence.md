## Why “a meteorite hit the ground” is not yet safe wording

| Simplified claim | Counterevidence or qualification | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| “A meteorite struck Siberia and made a crater.” | The field signature is a broad radial/butterfly forestfall; NASA histories report no confirmed crater and no proven macroscopic fragment. Modern models place the main energy release in an airburst around 10 km high. | Reject as unqualified wording. Use “cosmic body entered the atmosphere and exploded.” |
| “No evidence survived.” | Eyewitness reports, seismic/atmospheric records, treefall, burning and possible microparticle evidence survive; the missing evidence is a confirmed large fragment or conventional crater. | Reject “nothing left.” |
| “The asteroid explanation is proven.” | NASA’s historical monograph says microparticles do not decisively discriminate comet from asteroid; Chyba et al. and the NASA workshop favour a stony asteroid based on model fit, not recovered composition. | Use “leading model” with uncertainty. |
| “It was a comet.” | Cometary models were historically influential and remain in the source trail, but the cited Chyba model says appropriate-energy comets disrupt too high; NASA still describes the origin as not certain. | Attribute as a competing model, not consensus. |
| “Lake Cheko is the missing crater.” | Gasperini et al. proposed it, but Collins et al. report no impactor material, un-crater-like morphology and mature trees near the rim, concluding it is highly unlikely to be an impact crater. | Keep as a documented scientific dispute. |
| “The 1908 newspaper eyewitness account proves the trajectory.” | Reports differ by location, time, direction and vocabulary; one early Filimonovo story was partly fictitious, while other reports were remote observations. | Use as witness evidence, not trajectory proof. |
| “Kulik found the crater but failed to recognise it.” | Kulik searched boggy depressions and found no meteorites; the later field pattern supports an airburst interpretation, but the absence of a crater is a research conclusion, not evidence that a particular hole must be the crater. | Do not imply a hidden crater without a source. |
| “The explosion killed no one, so it was harmless.” | The area was sparsely populated, but NASA reports reindeer losses and witnesses blown into the air; harm to people, dwellings and animals is part of the witness record. | Do not call it harmless; exact casualty count is not established here. |
| “The event was a single, clean blast at exactly 7:15.” | Witnesses and reports give around 7:00, 7:15 or later local times from different places; an elongated luminous body and multiple sounds are recorded. | Use “around 7 a.m.” and retain source-specific times. |
| “The butterfly pattern alone proves an asteroid.” | Forest geometry constrains energy, height and direction, but composition is inferred through atmospheric-fragmentation models and material evidence. | Treat geometry as a constraint, not a fingerprint. |
| “The exotic theories are as plausible as the asteroid model.” | NASA’s history lists antimatter, black holes, UFOs and artificial explanations as speculative uses of the event’s uncertainty; the current evidence set supplies no comparable support. | Do not give false balance. |

## Source and perspective problems

- The first reports were remote, multilingual and often telegraphed or repeated; an early newspaper account can preserve real public reaction while still containing invented details.
- Many eyewitness records were collected years or decades later by scientists, officials or folklorists. Their value is real but not equivalent to a contemporaneous instrument trace; memory, translation, interviewer selection and local cultural framing must remain visible.
- Kulik’s expeditions were searching for a large meteorite and at times interpreted depressions through that expectation. His observations of forestfall are central; his hoped-for iron body was not found.
- Later English-language summaries often turn “meteorite,” “bolide,” “impact,” “airburst” and “asteroid” into interchangeable words. They are not interchangeable in this episode.
- “Evenki” is the present spelling used by many institutions, while historical sources may use older Russian ethnonyms. Preserve a respectful modern term and note historical wording only when quoting or analysing a source.
