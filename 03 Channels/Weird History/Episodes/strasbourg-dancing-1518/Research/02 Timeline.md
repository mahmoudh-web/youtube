| Late July / early August | Municipal responses move between supervised or encouraged dancing/music, guild and hospital involvement, pilgrimage, and restriction. The surviving records establish intervention but do not validate every later story about purpose-built stages or paid musicians. | **High for intervention; medium/low for the full theatrical sequence.** [S4](01%20Source%20Index.md), [S5](01%20Source%20Index.md), [S10](01%20Source%20Index.md) |
| 2 August 1518 | A register leaf signed by Sébastien Brant records a ban on public dancing and on kettledrums/drums, reportedly to avoid interfering with recovery. | **High.** Verify the German wording and duration from the original leaf. [S5](01%20Source%20Index.md) |
| 3 August 1518 | A Brant note, as reproduced by Miller, describes the “horrible episode” with sick dancing people as ongoing and records a fine of 30 shillings for dancing until Michaelmas. | **Medium-high for the existence of the note; high caution on translation.** [S11](01%20Source%20Index.md) |

## Chronology guardrails

- Use **mid-July to September 1518** as the defensible outbreak window. Use **14 July** only with an attribution to later chronicle tradition.
- The **25 July letter** is stronger than a later story about a named first dancer, but it does not give a full case count or death toll.
- The episode is described by the city as illness and by later writers as “Saint Vitus dance”; neither label proves an infectious disease.
- “The outbreak ended after the pilgrimage” is a temporal sequence, not proof that the pilgrimage medically cured the condition.
