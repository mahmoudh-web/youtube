## Stop condition

This pack stops at research. No narration, title/thumbnail package, visual plan, archival download, edit, upload or publication decision is authorised by these notes. A human editor must inspect primary records and approve each material claim before downstream work begins.

## Unresolved factual questions

- Does the original Ministry of Health report define the commonly repeated 4,000 estimate as deaths above baseline during the fog, the first three weeks, or a broader period?
- Why does the Ministry statement record 4,703 Greater London deaths for the week ending 13 December while Scott records 2,484 administrative-county deaths for the same week? What are the exact boundaries, registration bases and reporting units?
- Does the Ministry report treat post-fog mortality through February/March 1953 as influenza, pollution, both, or uncertain, and what evidence supports its interpretation?
- Can the original Logan, Wilkins and Ministry tables reconcile daily mortality, weekly mortality, smoke, sulphur dioxide and temperature without mixing sites or baselines?
- Are the Scott sulphur-dioxide values transcribed with the correct decimal placement and units?
- What exact role did domestic fires, power stations, industry, transport, river traffic and imported pollution play in the pollutant mixture?
- Which specific public services stopped or slowed, and which anecdotes about buses, ambulances, theatres, cinemas, livestock, crime or hospital pressure are supported by contemporary records?
- What did the Beaver Committee’s final report say about health, source control, smoke-control areas, fuel substitution, warning systems, costs and implementation order?
- How much did the City of London (Various Powers) Act 1954, the 1956 Act and pre-existing local measures each contribute to the policy transition?
- Does “the Clean Air Act changed everything” overstate a law that focused mainly on smoke and domestic fuel controls while leaving some pollutants and enforcement questions separate?

## Required primary-source checks

1. **Ministry of Health report:** obtain and inspect *Mortality and Morbidity During the London Fog of December 1952*, Reports on Public Health and Medical Subjects No. 95 (1954), including the methods, tables, discussion of influenza, acute mortality and extended mortality.
2. **National Archives record:** inspect the original MH 58/398 item and confirm the transcript wording, geography, week-ending date, 4,703 total and 501 Emergency Bed Service applications.
3. **Contemporary medical papers:** read Scott, Logan and Wilkins in full; record each study’s geography, observation sites, baseline, units, time window and causal language.
4. **Weather record:** locate the original Meteorological Office daily observations and resolve whether 5–8 and 5–9 December describe different phases of the same event.
5. **Beaver Committee:** inspect the 1953 interim and 1954 final reports, including recommendations and any direct reference to the 1952 event’s mortality and source evidence.
6. **Parliament and statute:** inspect the original Hansard debates, the City of London measure and the Clean Air Act’s commencement orders. Keep political rhetoric separate from enacted provisions.
7. **Contemporary press and service records:** inspect 5–15 December 1952 London newspapers, London County Council health/transport records, Emergency Bed Service records and hospital reports before using exact disruption claims.
8. **Later reanalysis:** read Bell and Davis in full and record model choices, influenza controls, pollution measures, lag periods and uncertainty before approving the 12,000 estimate.

## Language and health checks

- “Fog,” “smoke-laden fog,” “smog,” “pollution,” “deaths registered,” “excess deaths,” “deaths attributed to the fog,” “illness,” “hospital applications” and “long-term effects” are not synonyms.
- Use “the Ministry statement recorded,” “Scott’s administrative-county table recorded,” or “Bell and Davis estimated” for the three principal mortality figures.
- Use “associated with,” “consistent with,” “helped drive” and “later analysis argued” where the evidence does not establish exclusive causation.
- Do not diagnose named individuals or assign an individual’s death to a pollutant without a primary medical record and human editor approval.
- Avoid graphic descriptions of respiratory distress, dead animals or bodies. The research note may preserve such evidence as a verification lead without turning it into spectacle.

## Legal, ethical and editorial-risk flags

| Risk | Why it matters | Required control |
|---|---|---|
| Mortality inflation | 2,484, 4,000, 4,703, 6,000 and 12,000 appear under different scopes or methods. | Keep every number tied to geography, time window, baseline and source type. |
| Medical causation | Pollution, cold, pre-existing disease and possible influenza interact in the record. | Separate registered causes, statistical association and individual causation. |
| Source blame | Homes, power stations, industrial furnaces and transport all appear in the source system. | Do not assign sole blame without source-apportionment evidence. |
| Policy overclaim | The smog influenced a longer chain of inquiry, local measures and legislation. | Say “helped drive/influence” and describe the statute’s actual provisions. |
| Historical privacy | Individual medical details and death records may involve descendants and sensitive health information. | Avoid identifiable medical claims unless the record and editorial purpose justify them. |
| Archival rights | Reports, newspapers, photographs and broadcast material have different access and reuse terms. | This pack records leads only; rights review is a later human gate. |
