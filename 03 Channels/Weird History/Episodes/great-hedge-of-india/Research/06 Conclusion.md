## Provisional assessment

**Yes, Britain’s Indian administration maintained a very long Inland Customs Line that used living thorn hedge sections; no, the located evidence does not support saying that a continuous 2,500-mile living hedge ran across India.** The most defensible description is a changing customs system whose physical infrastructure varied by year and terrain: live plants in some stretches, dry thorn backing, stone walls, ditches, earthen banks, natural obstacles, roads and guard posts elsewhere.

The 2,500-mile figure is useful as a scale marker for the wider line in a later official synthesis, but the primary 1867–68 report gives 1,817 miles for a named Imperial Line excluding other sections, while Batten’s 1873 memorandum gives 1,200 miles for a specific Rajputana/Central India portion. Those are not contradictions to be averaged away; they describe different scopes and moments.

## What the pack can currently establish

- The line’s administrative purpose was to stop differently taxed or untaxed salt from entering British territory and to collect duty on salt and, in some areas, sugar.
- The 1867–68 report records substantial live-hedge gaps and reliance on dry hedge, young plantings, stone walls and scarped earth in at least Hissar; the later Imperial Gazetteer describes cactus and masonry/ditches elsewhere.
- The 1873 Batten memorandum supplies unusually concrete figures for one portion: about 1,200 miles, £105,728 cost in 1872–73, 8,578 officers and men, and more than £900,000 salt duty plus £142,000 sugar duty as reported.
- Statutes made the system more than a landscape curiosity: officials could regulate crossings, search people and goods, seize untaxed salt and conveyances, and destroy unauthorized salt works.
- The system changed as railways, Sambhar Lake control, tax equalization and production-point excise altered the economics of the line; the wider line was reported abandoned in 1879 except an Indus remnant.
- The salt tax generated major revenue and was defended as a replacement for many transit duties, but parliamentary critics described it as a food tax falling on poor consumers and protested regional increases after famine.
- The current evidence does not yet measure household impact consistently. It supports a careful account of price differences, fiscal extraction, enforcement powers and contested consumption effects—not a universal income percentage or quantified health outcome.

## Strongest provisional wording

> In nineteenth-century India, the British did maintain a customs line thousands of miles long to control salt. Parts of it became a living thorn hedge—but the line was a shifting hybrid of plants, dry brush, walls, ditches and checkpoints, and the famous 2,500-mile figure should not be mistaken for one continuous hedge.

This wording still requires human verification against the report images, Acts, abolition notices and local/regional evidence. It is not script copy approved for publication.
