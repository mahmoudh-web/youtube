## Purpose

This folder holds the source-led research pack for the provisional episode “The Cuyahoga River Fire: How a Burning River Changed Environmental Politics.” It investigates what happened on June 22, 1969, why that relatively small fire eclipsed earlier river fires, and how much the event actually influenced federal environmental policy. It is a research pack, not a script or production brief.

## Responsibilities

- Preserve a traceable source index, timeline, claim ledger, counterevidence record, uncertainty/human-verification note and provisional conclusion.
- Separate the physical event, the media transformation, and the later policy history.
- Distinguish fact, attributed claim, interpretation and uncertainty; never turn a symbolic association into sole causation.
- Track image/date-caption risk, especially the frequent use of 1952 fire photographs to represent the 1969 event.
- Stop at human research verification before narration, packaging, visual generation, editing, QC or publication.

## Key Decisions

- Use `cuyahoga-river-fire-1969` as the episode folder slug under the authoritative Weird History channel folder.
- Correct the issue premise’s June 29 date to June 22, 1969. June 29 appears in a legacy EPA timeline entry, but the National Park Service, EPA Journal, Time, Case Western Reserve University and the contemporary Congressional Record all identify June 22.
- Treat the 1969 fire as the last and most famous recorded Cuyahoga fire, not the first or largest. EPA records identify at least 13 recorded fires between 1868 and 1969; the 1952 fire caused much greater property damage.
- Treat the fire’s policy role as a catalyst/symbol within a larger chain that included pre-existing water-pollution law, the Santa Barbara oil spill, Lake Erie activism, Earth Day, the EPA reorganization and the 1972 Clean Water Act.
- Treat “the river burned” as shorthand for a fire on oil- and debris-covered water; the liquid river itself did not burn.
- Treat all newspaper scans, photographs, video and magazine pages as research references until a human verifies captions, provenance, copyright and permissions.

## Constraints and invariants

- Access dates in this pack are 2026-09-14.
- Do not claim that the Cuyahoga fire alone created the EPA or Clean Water Act. Official sources use contributory language; the legal record shows the EPA was established through Reorganization Plan No. 3 of 1970 and the Clean Water Act was the 1972 amendments to the Federal Water Pollution Control Act.
- The immediate ignition source is not fully settled in the located record. Use “a likely spark/flare from a passing train” only with attribution; do not state an eyewitness-proven mechanism.
- Do not use a 1952 fire photograph as a 1969 image. Time’s 1969 article used a photograph from the larger 1952 fire, and no verified photograph of the actual 1969 flames has been located in this pack.
- Damage figures differ by source: the CWRU entry gives $45,000 to one bridge plus $5,000 to another; EPA Journal summarizes $50,000; later Congressional remarks give a higher estimate. Preserve the source and avoid false precision.
- “No one was hurt” is not a cleared claim here; the located record concerns a short fire and property damage, not a comprehensive injury audit.
- No script, title/thumbnail package, visual brief, edit plan or production asset is authorized by this pack.
