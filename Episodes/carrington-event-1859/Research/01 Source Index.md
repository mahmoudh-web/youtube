## Research question

What do the contemporary solar, observatory, telegraph and newspaper records actually document about the September 1859 storm, and which claims belong only to later models of what a comparable storm could do to modern infrastructure?

## Conflict and episode promise

The event is strongly documented as a rare solar optical outburst, a severe geomagnetic disturbance, extraordinarily widespread aurorae and disruptive currents on 1859 telegraph lines. The unresolved editorial boundary is between those records and the later “the internet would collapse” analogy. The pack keeps observed effects, contemporary interpretation and modern scenario modelling in separate lanes.

## Source-quality key

- **A — primary record or near-contemporary scientific publication:** original observation, observatory paper, contemporary newspaper, or a scientific compilation that quotes named telegraph/observatory reports.
- **B — archive or official data gateway:** institutional catalogue, digitised historical record or government source that preserves or explains the primary evidence.
- **C — modern scholarly synthesis or model:** useful for reconstruction and uncertainty, but not a direct 1859 observation.
- **D — popular retelling:** orientation only; never the sole support for a material claim.

## Evidence-handling rules

1. Say **solar flare**, **geomagnetic storm**, **aurora**, **induced current** and **CME** only at the level supported by the cited source. The contemporary record documents the solar light, magnetic/telegraph effects and aurorae; “induced current” and CME are later physical interpretations of that record.
2. Attribute telegraph fires, shocks and battery-free operation to the named reports compiled by Loomis or the identified newspaper. Do not imply every telegraph office caught fire.
3. Keep the 28 August disturbance, the 1 September solar flash/observatory crochet and the 2 September major storm as distinct episodes within a broader late-August/early-September sequence.
4. Treat “the internet would collapse,” a global blackout, exact repair times and trillion-dollar losses as modern counterfactuals. They require a current model and infrastructure assumptions; they are not observations from 1859.
5. Inspect scans and original locators for the telegraph quotations, especially the Boston–Portland exchange, before approving verbatim narration.
6. No source in this index authorises reuse of scans, photographs, diagrams or newspaper images. Rights, attribution and any visual treatment remain outside this research pack.

## Primary-source retrieval queue

1. Inspect Carrington’s and Hodgson’s original PDF pages and diagram; confirm the time transcription and the exact printed wording.
2. Read Stewart’s three plates and the Kew timing convention; reconcile the 11:20 a.m. 1 September short disturbance with the 2 September storm start.
3. Retrieve the original telegraph reports cited by Loomis (especially Prescott and the Boston, Pittsburgh, Washington, Norway and European accounts) and record whether each is an operator report, superintendent report or later newspaper retelling.
4. Inspect the New York Times and Springfield *Weekly Republican* page images; transcribe only what the scans support and preserve newspaper attribution.
5. Compare the BGS digitised magnetograms with the historical scans before using any converted nT/degree values.
6. Read the full National Academies and Lloyd’s/AER reports before using any cost, population, duration or transformer-damage estimate.
