## Stop condition

This is a source-led research pack, not a script. No narration, title, thumbnail, visual treatment or production asset should be built until a human editor verifies the original report pages, the measurement language, the model qualifiers and the source-mechanism uncertainty below.

## Required primary-source checks

- Inspect the page images, map and plates in [Miller's USGS Professional Paper 354-C](https://dggs.alaska.gov/webpubs/usgs/p/text/p0354c.pdf), not only the OCR text. Confirm the printed page references, mean-sea-level datum, altimeter procedure and the exact wording of the 1,740-ft and 1,720-ft conclusions.
- Verify the 1959 [Tocher and Miller USGS record](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/field-observations-effects-alaska-earthquake-10-july-1958) against the article/DOI before quoting the “more than 1700 feet” formulation. Keep its 10 July title distinct from 9 July local time.
- Treat Ulrich and Swanson as recorded eyewitness accounts. Verify whether a later retelling is quoting the original account, an interview, or a secondary paraphrase.
- Check the cited studies behind [Franco et al. 2020](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/20/2255/2020/) before turning any modeled amplitude, flow depth or run-up output into narration.

## Mandatory terminology check

The research pack uses these terms deliberately:

- **Run-up elevation:** maximum land elevation reached by water. The historically reported maximum is about 1,720 ft / 524 m a.s.l. at the trimline.
- **Wave height/amplitude:** vertical scale of the water wave in the bay or at impact. Later studies model values roughly in the 94–162 m range for several approaches, with other model-specific outputs.
- **Flow depth:** water thickness at a particular location during inundation; the 2020 model reports 4–10 m at the point where the 524 m a.s.l. run-up was reached.
- **Trimline:** field evidence marking the upper boundary of near-total forest destruction; it is a proxy for destructive reach/run-up, not a photograph of the wave crest at that elevation.

Do not write “the wave was 1,720 feet high,” “a 1,720-foot wall crossed the bay,” or “a 1,720-foot wave hit the open ocean.” The approved evidence-bounded wording should say **run-up elevation** or **water reached an elevation of about 1,720 ft above mean sea level**.

## Measurement and interpretation risks

- **1,800 vs 1,720:** the first is an early altimeter reading; the second is the later, more precise maximum trimline measurement. They are not rival direct wave heights.
- **1,740 vs 1,720:** Miller's summary describes a surge to about 1,740 ft and a forest trimline at about 1,720 ft. Keep the quantities distinct and attribute the interpretation.
- **Eyewitness vs survey:** boat survivors establish sequence, direction and local violence; they did not measure the head-of-bay maximum.
- **Model output vs observation:** physical and numerical models test plausibility. Their amplitude, run-up and flow-depth outputs depend on source geometry, bathymetry, topography, roughness, grid resolution and slide assumptions.
- **Single vs double slide:** the rapid rockslide explanation is well supported, but Ward and Day's possible second submarine slide remains a competing hypothesis for the outer-bay evidence.
- **Sensational superlatives:** use “highest documented run-up” only after the human editor checks the scope and wording of the cited source. Avoid implying that the event was an open-ocean tsunami of the same height.

## Visual and rights checks

- If using the 1958 aerial photographs, map plates or field photographs, verify the source institution, rights status, attribution and whether a license permits publication.
- Do not create faux archival footage or imply that a modern simulation is original 1958 footage.
- Any diagram should label **run-up elevation**, **wave height/amplitude**, **flow depth** and **mean sea level** separately.
- A visual of a vertical 1,720-ft wall of water is misleading unless clearly marked as an intentionally non-scale misconception being corrected.
