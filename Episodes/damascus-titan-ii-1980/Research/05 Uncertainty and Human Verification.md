## Core uncertainties

### 1. What was the precise maintenance error?

The public summaries agree that a socket was dropped and damaged the first-stage fuel tank. They differ on the tool’s weight, its fall distance and the exact maintenance step. The original Eighth Air Force mishap report and witness statements must be checked before naming the airman’s equipment choice or assigning personal blame.

### 2. What ignited the vapours?

The NNSA/DOE summaries establish ignition of accumulated fuel vapours, but the accessible sources do not establish a final ignition source with sufficient precision. The Washington Post reported that an exhaust fan did not activate; other later reconstructions may identify a spark or sequence. Do not state a fan, electrical fault or specific mechanical trigger as settled until the board report, diagrams and command log are read.

### 3. How long did detection and response take?

The record gives a 6:47 p.m. vapour indication, a 24-minute detection delay in the NNSA summary, evacuation milestones, and a 2:13 a.m. emergency-team entry. It also gives 2:50 a.m. or just-before-3 a.m. ignition. These are compatible at broad scale but not yet reconciled minute by minute. Preserve a range and identify the source for every time.

### 4. What did the warhead’s safety features prove?

The warhead was recovered essentially intact and official summaries report no radioactive contamination. That supports the outcome, not a general claim that all nuclear weapons were safe in accidents or that this warhead could not have been damaged under any other conditions. Do not quote yield, safety-switch design or hypothetical blast/fallout effects without a separate primary-source review.

### 5. Did age cause the accident?

The Titan II was an aging liquid-fuel system and Congress had already discussed leaks, injuries and warning systems. Hans Mark’s reported briefing said the missile’s age was not the immediate cause. The evidence currently supports age as context and a maintenance/design/response question, not as a settled single cause.

### 6. What did the Air Force investigation conclude?

An Eighth Air Force Missile Investigation Board report was produced in December 1980, but the report itself has not been inspected in this pack. Schlosser’s source notes identify copies in the Dan Glickman papers at Wichita State University and at the Titan Missile Museum and Jacksonville Museum of Military History. These locations are research leads, not verified quotations.

## Legal, ethical and public-understanding sensitivity

- **Nuclear-war misinformation:** “Minutes from detonation,” “almost destroyed Arkansas” and exact counterfactual casualty figures are not supported by the current public record. Keep the conventional explosion and nuclear-warhead presence separate.
- **Individual fairness:** The dropped-tool sequence involves named service members and injured responders. Use names only after primary-record verification; do not convert a reported error into a moral judgment about character or competence.
- **Living participants and families:** Some responders, witnesses and relatives may still be living. Avoid unnecessary medical detail, speculation about long-term illness and blame not established by records.
- **Government secrecy:** “Neither confirm nor deny” communications about weapon presence should not be reframed as proof of a cover-up or radioactive release.
- **Classified/redacted records:** Redactions mark an incomplete record. They do not justify filling gaps with the most dramatic scenario.
- **Archival rights:** Government status, public access and copyright are separate questions. Every scan, photograph, map, broadcast clip and transcript needs a rights check.
