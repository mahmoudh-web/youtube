## Purpose

This folder is the source-led research area for the nineteenth-century Inland Customs Line in British India, provisionally titled “The Great Hedge of India: Did Britain Build a Thorn Customs Wall?” It is a research pack, not a script or production brief.

## Responsibilities

- Preserve a traceable source index, timeline, claim ledger, counterevidence record, uncertainty/legal-sensitivity note and research conclusion.
- Separate the customs line as an administrative barrier from the living hedge as one changing component of it.
- Keep uncertainty visible around maximum length, continuity, materials, labour and the social effects of the salt tax.
- Stop for explicit human verification before narration, packaging or production work.

## Key Decisions

- Use `great-hedge-of-india` as the episode folder slug under the authoritative Weird History channel folder.
- Treat “2,500 miles” as a reported length for the wider customs line at a particular point, not as proof of a continuous living hedge. The strongest primary report located here gives 1,817 miles for the Imperial Line in 1867–68 and describes only part of that as a satisfactory live hedge.
- Use the 1867–68 Imperial Customs report and the 1873 Batten memorandum as the core primary records; use the Imperial Gazetteer, Hansard and modern scholarship to triangulate and expose disagreement.
- The authorized six-file Research sync uses `01 Source Index.md` through `06 Conclusion.md`, with the exact fifth file named `05 Uncertainty and Legal Sensitivity.md`.

## Constraints and Invariants

- Access dates in this pack are 2026-09-14.
- Do not say the entire 2,500-mile line was a continuous, living, thorn hedge. The primary report records gaps, young plants, dry hedge backing, stone walls and earthen barriers.
- Do not describe the salt tax’s effect as a single measured percentage of household income unless a specific contemporary dataset is verified. Preserve the regional variation and the disagreement between fiscal defenders and critics.
- Colonial administrative language such as “smuggling,” “Native States,” “subjects,” “oppression” and “barbarous” must be attributed or contextualized. Do not infer consent from British or princely-state administrative documents.
- Local labour, household consumption, prosecutions, resistance and health effects are under-documented in the located sources and require specialist human review.
