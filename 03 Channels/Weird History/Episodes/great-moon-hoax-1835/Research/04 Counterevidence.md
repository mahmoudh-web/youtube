## Why the simple “newspaper fooled everyone” story is incomplete

The serial is documented and its lunar observations were fabricated, but the reception story is more complicated than a universal mass deception. The evidence includes reprints, credulous readers, sceptical readers, satire, commercial self-promotion, later memoir and institutional interpretation. The episode should explain the machinery of trust while resisting both “everyone believed it” and “everyone instantly knew it was a joke.”

| Simplified claim | Counterevidence / qualification | Current handling |
|---|---|---|
| “The whole world believed in bat-men.” | Reprints show attention and reach, but surviving evidence also includes sceptical newspapers, rival attacks, parody and later disputes. | Say the story travelled widely and produced documented credulity, scepticism and entertainment; do not quantify universal belief. |
| “The Sun reported that Herschel had found life on the Moon.” | That is the newspaper’s framing, not an observation by Herschel. | Use “*The Sun* claimed/presented a report attributed to Herschel.” |
| “John Herschel discovered the bat-men.” | Herschel’s real Cape work is documented in observatory records and the 1847 results; the bat-men belong to the fabricated serial. | Keep the real Cape expedition as a control record beside every description of the hoax. |
| “The story came from the Edinburgh Journal of Science.” | The series used a supposedly authoritative but false source frame; catalogue and later histories describe the source as a fabricated supplement. | Say “the paper attributed it to a supposed supplement”; verify the journal’s exact publication history. |
| “Richard Adams Locke definitely wrote every word alone.” | Locke is the strongest attribution, but later accounts discuss technical material, possible influences and competing Nicollet/Poe theories. | Say “the serial is generally attributed to Locke, who later claimed authorship”; do not turn attribution into a settled production-room transcript. |
| “Locke intended a hoax to deceive readers.” | Locke later said he intended satire and was surprised by the seriousness of the reception. | Attribute the intention claim to Locke; treat motive as contested or partly unknowable. |
| “Locke intended only harmless satire.” | Regardless of intent, the paper presented fiction with scientific authority and used a real person’s reputation. | Separate claimed intent from observed effect and editorial responsibility. |
| “The circulation jumped to exactly 19,360 because of the hoax.” | The number appears in later histories and may derive from the paper’s own claims; an audited circulation series has not been located. | Use only as an attributed reported figure after checking contemporary circulation evidence. |
| “Yale was fooled.” | The Yale delegation anecdote is reported by later histories, but the delegation’s purpose, participants and degree of belief need primary documentation. | Say “later accounts report a Yale visit seeking the supposed source,” pending verification. |
| “A missionary expedition to the Moon was actually organised.” | The Springfield fund is a later-reported anecdote tied to Harriet Martineau; its documentary basis and seriousness are unresolved. | Keep it in the research queue, not in settled narration. |
| “The hoax was the first fake news story.” | Newspaper hoaxes and fabricated news predate 1835; “first” depends on definition and surviving evidence. | Call it an early, influential example of fabricated newspaper science or a case study in media trust. |
| “The six articles ran six consecutive days.” | Common summaries say six-part series, but the known dates are 25–29 and 31 August, with a gap on Sunday. | Use “six installments from 25–31 August, excluding Sunday” only after original issue verification. |
| “The Sun confessed on 16 September.” | The paper’s editorial, as quoted by O’Brien, uses evasive language about authenticity and refuses to confess until English or Scottish papers corroborate it. | Describe an equivocal acknowledgement/response, not a clean retraction, until the original issue is read. |
| “The story was exposed when Grant was discovered to be fictional.” | Grant’s fictitious status is part of later explanation; the exposure process involved rival newspapers, correspondence, source checking and the paper’s own posture. | Present several verification routes rather than one reveal moment. |
| “The illustrations prove people believed the report.” | A print proves production and circulation of an image, not the maker’s or viewers’ private belief. | Treat S3/S14 as evidence of visual reception and reuse, with belief left open. |
| “The bat-men were Victorian aliens.” | They were literary inventions in a newspaper fabrication, later reprinted and illustrated. | Use “the paper’s invented bat-men” or “the fictional *Vespertilio-homo*.” |
| “The real telescope could see anything described in the serial.” | Herschel’s actual 20-foot reflector and Cape results document serious astronomy, not resolved lunar flora and zoology. | Do not reproduce the hoax’s magnification or lens claims as engineering fact. |
| “The story worked because nineteenth-century readers were ignorant.” | The source frame borrowed specialist authority, and contemporaries included both credulous and sceptical readers; belief is not reducible to intelligence. | Analyse source accessibility, authority and publication practices rather than mocking readers. |
| “The hoax caused the modern news industry.” | It is a useful case in penny-press economics and verification debates, but causal claims about journalism require broader scholarship. | Label media-history conclusions as interpretation and keep them proportionate to the evidence. |

## Source-bias and absence ledger

- **The serial and 1859 reprint:** indispensable for what was printed, but the 1859 compilation may regularise text and adds retrospective framing. It cannot establish what every reader believed.
- **The *Sun*’s 16 September editorial:** primary evidence of the paper’s response, but self-interested and rhetorically evasive; read the full issue before summarising it.
- **Later *Sun* histories and O’Brien (1918):** preserve valuable quotations, anecdotes and internal tradition, but are decades removed and may celebrate the paper’s commercial success.
- **Library and museum essays:** strong guides to objects and context, but their statements about circulation and public belief are secondary summaries.
- **Herschel records:** authoritative control evidence for his actual work and later reaction, but private letters quoted by modern institutions still need original-document verification.
- **Absent or unresolved evidence:** a complete run of original *Sun* issue scans with stable page locators; audited circulation records; original Yale records; local Springfield missionary records; full text of Herschel’s letters; and a systematic map of contemporary reprints and corrections.

## Required counterevidence search

1. Compare the 1859 text with original *Sun* issues for 25–29 and 31 August 1835, noting changes, omissions and typographic evidence.
2. Retrieve and transcribe the full 16 September 1835 editorial; distinguish what the paper admitted, implied, denied or deferred.
3. Locate contemporary *Journal of Commerce*, *Herald*, *New-Yorker*, *New York Times* and other reports that believed, doubted, mocked or reprinted the story.
4. Verify the Yale delegation and Springfield missionary anecdote from institutional or contemporary records; remove them if they remain only later folklore.
5. Inspect Herschel’s 1836–37 correspondence and 1847 Cape results; record exact wording and the difference between public and private response.
6. Test the Locke-only authorship narrative against the 1835–40 record and later scholarly debate; preserve uncertainty about intent and assistance.
