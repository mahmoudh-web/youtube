## Research question and promise

How did a deadly confrontation over a poplar tree become a high-risk demonstration of force, and why did the response stop short of a direct attack on North Korean personnel or facilities? The answer must distinguish what happened from what U.S. officials hoped the display would communicate and what later historians infer about de-escalation.

## Source-quality key

- **A** — primary record, treaty/armistice material, official archive, or contemporaneous government document.
- **B** — official institutional history or declassified retrospective with a clear provenance.
- **C** — reputable scholarly or professional retrospective used for triangulation.
- **D** — tertiary or partisan account used only to expose competing narratives or generate leads.

## Key entities and voices

- United Nations Command / U.S. Forces Korea: operational command, JSA security and the formal protest channel.
- Capt. Arthur G. Bonifas and 1st Lt. Mark T. Barrett: the two U.S. Army officers killed on 18 August; confirm ranks and posthumous rank references before publication.
- Korean Service Corps / ROK laborers and KATUSA personnel: work party and security participants whose experience should not disappear behind the U.S.–DPRK state narrative.
- Korean People’s Army guards and command: attackers and the source of the North Korean “self-defence” account; individual responsibility is not fully established in the located record.
- Gen. Richard G. Stilwell: CINCUNC and author of the operational response/protest channel.
- President Gerald Ford, Secretary Henry Kissinger, the Washington Special Actions Group and Joint Chiefs: U.S. policy deliberation, force deployment and restraint decisions.
- President Park Chung-hee, Minister Suh and Gen. Ro: ROK consent, pressure for a forceful but non-firearms response, and alliance politics.
- Gen. Victor S. Vierra / Task Force Vierra and the 2nd Infantry Division / Task Force Brady: operational formations named in later U.S. Army histories; exact command relationships require order-of-battle verification.

## Research-source opportunities (not production assets)

- Ford Library Box 10, “Korea — Operation Paul Bunyan”: retrieve the full scan and any opened companion files, especially the JCS assessment, operation plan and situation reports.
- U.S. Army Center of Military History, 2nd Infantry Division and JSA records: verify unit composition, ranks, timings and after-action language.
- U.N. Security Council / Military Armistice Commission records: inspect the original 18 August protest, 21 August message exchange and 6 September supplement.
- National Archives of Korea and Korean-language records: check the official Korean account, later archival provenance and transliteration.
- FBIS Daily Reports and the North Korean broadcast record: compare the 18, 21 and 23 August accounts rather than relying on a translated excerpt.

## Source gaps

The located pack does not yet contain the complete operation order, full after-action report, contemporaneous medical/casualty records, all JSA rules in force on 18 August, or a bilingual review of the Korean and North Korean records. The exact total force, duration and causal reason for the absence of a KPA attack remain verification items.
