## Verification status

This is a source-led research pack, not a script approval. The central chronology and federal settlement are well supported by institutional and statutory sources, but the casualty wording, force totals, exact survey incidents and compensation shorthand require a human verification pass.

## Mandatory uncertainty to preserve

### “War” label

The event is widely and officially called the Toledo War, but the located evidence more securely shows a boundary dispute with rival surveys, arrests, legal penalties, militia mobilisation and a stand-off than a conventional war. Use “so-called Toledo War,” “the Toledo boundary crisis” or “the dispute later remembered as the Toledo War” where a neutral description is needed. If the familiar label is used in a title, the episode should qualify it early.

### Casualty

The best-supported lead is that Michigan Deputy Sheriff Joseph Wood was stabbed by Ohio partisan Two Stickney during an attempted arrest, probably on 15 July 1835. Institutional sources describe the wound as minor/nonfatal and call it the only injury or bloodshed. A Library of Congress text says no blood was shed, and the underlying affidavit, indictment and medical record are not yet in hand. Until those are checked, the safe wording is “a reported nonfatal stabbing, often described as the dispute’s only injury.” Do not say “one person died,” “the only casualty” without qualification, or “bloodless.”

### Compensation / “trade” claim

The federal record supports a conditional admission and boundary settlement. The 15 June 1836 statute establishes Ohio’s northern boundary, defines Michigan’s new boundary and requires assent; the 26 January 1837 statute admits Michigan after that assent. Later Michigan and Ohio histories describe the resulting Upper Peninsula inclusion as compensation or an exchange. The statutes do not use that word, do not describe a cash payment, and do not erase Michigan’s earlier claim to an eastern part of the peninsula. Use “later histories describe most of the present Upper Peninsula as compensation for relinquishing the Toledo claim” rather than “Congress paid Michigan for Toledo.”

## Other open questions

- What exactly did the 1787 Ordinance, Ohio’s 1802 constitution and the 1805 Michigan Territory act say, and how did the surviving maps represent those words?
- What are the exact dates, endpoints and legal status of the Harris and Fulton surveys?
- What did the Pains and Penalties Act actually criminalise, and what penalties did it provide?
- Did Phillips Corners involve shots exchanged, warning fire, or only a contested report? What do the contemporary reports say?
- How many people were actually present in each mobilisation, as opposed to available on a census or authorised by a budget?
- What did Jackson’s commissioners recommend, and why did Jackson remove Mason?
- Were the September 1836 and December 1836 conventions procedurally and representatively comparable? How did Michiganians describe the “Frostbitten Convention” at the time?
- Which eastern Upper Peninsula lands were already within Michigan’s 1835 proposed boundaries, and what exact area did the 1836 boundary add?
- What treaty and Indigenous-government records are necessary to describe the land and its communities responsibly?

## Legal, ethical and editorial risk

No living-person defamation issue is apparent. The main risks are historical overstatement, partisan state-memory framing, false precision around injury and acreage, and erasing Indigenous title and local agency. Human editorial review is required before a title, thumbnail, script, archive-image reuse or publication decision.

## Stop condition

Stop research-to-script handoff until the Wood record has been checked, the “war” label is explicitly qualified, the statutory settlement has been read against later “compensation” language, the exact Upper Peninsula comparison is mapped, and the Indigenous/treaty context has been sourced.
