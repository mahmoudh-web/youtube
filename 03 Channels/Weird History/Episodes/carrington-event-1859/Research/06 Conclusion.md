## Provisional assessment

The source record supports a powerful late-August/early-September 1859 space-weather sequence. On 1 September, Richard Carrington and Richard Hodgson independently recorded a short-lived brilliant solar outburst. Kew Observatory preserved a near-simultaneous short magnetic disturbance and a larger storm beginning on 2 September. Contemporary auroral reports reached unusually low latitudes, while telegraph records describe line-specific currents that interrupted communications, shocked operators, produced sparks and heat, ignited paper or wood in some cases, and on selected lines allowed messages to continue after batteries were disconnected.

The core historical claim is therefore narrower—and stronger—than the popular apocalypse story: the 1859 storm visibly transformed the sky and measurably disturbed the era’s long telegraph conductors. The evidence does not show that every telegraph station burned, that all communication stopped, or that a modern internet existed to be “knocked out.”

## What the pack can currently establish

- Carrington’s and Hodgson’s solar observations are primary evidence of a rare optical outburst, not a direct measurement of a CME.
- Kew’s photographic observatory record documents a short disturbance near the solar observation and a separate, larger 2 September magnetic storm.
- Loomis’s near-contemporary scientific compilations preserve named reports from telegraph operators, superintendents, observatories and newspapers across North America and Europe.
- The telegraph effects were heterogeneous: some lines were unusable or intermittent, some produced sparks or heat, some reports describe fires or shocks, and some operators reported useful current after disconnecting batteries.
- The sequence is best described as a set of linked late-August/early-September disturbances, while “Carrington Event” is the later umbrella label.
- Modern CME/ICME language, exact storm indices and flare classes belong to later reconstruction. They should be attributed, not voiced as if observed in 1859.
- Modern systems are vulnerable through different pathways: geomagnetically induced currents in grids, ionospheric effects on HF radio and GPS/GNSS, satellite radiation/drag and aviation/radio impacts.
- Lloyd’s/AER’s 20–40 million people, 16 days–1–2 years and $0.6–2.6T figures are outputs of a 2013 North American model scenario. They are not historical observations, global totals or a guaranteed forecast.

## Safest research wording

> In 1859, astronomers saw an extraordinary flash on the Sun; less than two days later, aurorae spread far from the poles and the era’s telegraph lines began carrying currents they were never designed to handle. Some operators were shocked, some equipment sparked or burned, and a few lines could work after their batteries were disconnected. The records show a severe solar-terrestrial storm—not a literal internet outage. What a comparable storm could do today is a separate question for modern infrastructure models.

## Editorial interpretation, clearly labelled

The most defensible episode tension is the gap between what the Sun and telegraph operators actually recorded and what later audiences imagine when they hear “Carrington Event.” In 1859 the technology was crude but electrically exposed; the telegraph lines became instruments of the storm as well as casualties of it. Today’s hazard is broader, but it is not a simple scale-up: the outcome would depend on storm magnetic structure, geography, grid design, satellite hardening, radio conditions, warning time and mitigation. “The internet would collapse” is therefore a dramatic analogy, not a conclusion supported by the historical record.

This paragraph is an interpretation of the indexed evidence, not a quotation from a witness or a model report.
