## Counterevidence register

### “Edison electrocuted Topsy to defeat AC” versus the documented corporate chain

The popular version has a real basis for association: the film carried the Edison name, the copyright claimant was Thomas A. Edison, and a local “Edison” electric plant supplied power. But the names refer to different corporate roles. The Edison Papers says the Brooklyn utility acted at Luna Park’s request, Edison played no role in directing that utility in 1903, and Edison’s extant correspondence does not mention Topsy. The film company’s credit proves production/distribution, not that the inventor personally selected the subject or ordered the shoot.

### “No Edison involvement” versus a real Edison business connection

The rebuttal should not overcorrect into “Edison had nothing to do with it” without qualification. Edison was president of the Edison Manufacturing Company, the company’s name appeared in the film/copyright record, and his earlier laboratory work helped create the cultural and technical association between electrocution and Edison. The supportable formulation is: personal direction or attendance is unproven and contradicted by the located record; company-level association is documented.

### “The War of the Currents was still happening” versus the 1892 transition

Edison did campaign against AC in the late 1880s and early 1890s, and earlier animal experiments were used in that dispute. Rutgers dates his exit from the lighting business to the 1892 GE merger. Topsy’s execution in 1903 occurred more than a decade later. A continuity of cultural imagery is possible; a continuous 1903 commercial campaign is not established.

### “The execution was a humane alternative” versus an exploitative spectacle

The SPCA/ASPCA intervention is described in later institutional histories as objecting to hanging and approving a combination of methods. That may explain the period’s choice, but it does not make the event ethically neutral. The same record documents public attention, press coverage, a film camera and a sensational title. Use “period officials described/accepted it as more humane,” not “it was humane.”

### “Topsy killed three keepers” versus one securely located 1902 report

Many modern accounts repeat three deaths and a lit-cigarette incident. The BPL research note identifies one 1902 *Brooklyn Daily Eagle* report and explicitly says other killings cannot be confirmed directly by multiple sources. The ledger therefore treats one reported death as established newspaper content and the larger count as disputed. The final episode should not use “three” without a checked, independent source chain.

### “The film shows an instant death” versus what the image proves

The Edison catalogue says the elephant falls “dead,” and the newspaper says death was quick. The surviving transfer continues to show the body after the collapse; scholarship notes that the footage does not independently prove the exact physiological moment of death. Do not narrate “instantaneous” as an observed medical fact.

### “ASPCA records prove the method” versus the archive gap

The NC State item is a genuine ASPCA archival object, but the public page exposes metadata and warns that OCR is automatic and uncorrected. A no-hit search for “Topsy” is not proof that the annual report is silent, and a report’s silence would not prove no private correspondence existed. Page-image review and related file requests remain required.

- Original *New York Times*, *Commercial Advertiser*, *New York Sun* and *New York Press* pages, especially for AC wording, voltage, method sequence, crowd size and Edison naming.
- ASPCA 1903 annual report page images and any 1902–03 correspondence/docket concerning Topsy or elephant executions.
