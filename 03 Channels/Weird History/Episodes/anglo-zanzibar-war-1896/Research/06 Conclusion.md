## Provisional assessment

The evidence supports a British-directed bombardment of the Sultan’s palace on 27 August 1896 after a succession crisis. It does not support treating “38 minutes” as a universally recorded stopwatch result. The strongest compact record located is Rear-Admiral Harry Rawson’s Admiralty telegram: he says he opened fire at 09:00 and ceased at 09:40. Other contemporary and later accounts say 09:02–09:40, 09:37, 09:48, 50 minutes or about an hour.

The best current research wording is therefore: **the strongest compact account puts the armed exchange at about forty minutes, while other records range to roughly an hour; it is conventionally remembered as the shortest war in history.** That sentence still needs a human check against the original dispatches and logs.

## What the pack can currently establish

- Hamad bin Thuwaini died on 25 August 1896; contemporaries suspected poisoning, but that allegation is not established here.
- Khalid bin Barghash occupied the palace and claimed the Sultanate during the succession crisis.
- The crisis occurred within a British protectorate relationship shaped by the 1890 Great Britain–Zanzibar and Anglo-German agreements.
- British authorities preferred Hamoud/Hamud and issued an ultimatum requiring Khalid to lower the flag, surrender and leave the palace.
- Rawson’s account says firing began at 09:00, the Sultan’s corvette was sunk, shore guns were silenced, and firing ceased at 09:40.
- Khalid left the palace and took refuge with the German Consul; Hamoud was proclaimed Sultan.
- British sources report heavy Zanzibari losses, commonly about 500 killed or wounded, but the primary casualty register and the killed/wounded split remain open.
- The familiar record label is a later/public reception frame built from a very short firing interval, not a complete description of the political crisis.

## Editorial interpretation, clearly labelled

The record suggests that the short duration was a consequence of extreme firepower imbalance and an already constrained political relationship, not evidence that the event was harmless or trivial. The “shortest war” frame is useful as a reception-history question, but it can conceal that the bombardment was an act of imperial coercion and that Zanzibari losses were substantial by the available estimates. This is an interpretation from the treaty, dispatch and casualty evidence—not a quotation from a single actor.
