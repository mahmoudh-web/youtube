## Historical legal and ethical risks

### Administrative power versus lived effect

The 1843 and 1875 Acts establish powers to destroy unauthorized salt works, seize salt and conveyances, apprehend people, restrict crossings and search persons and goods. Those powers show the coercive capacity of the system; they do not, without prosecution registers or testimony, quantify how often each power was used or how individual people experienced it.

### “Tax impact” is not one number

The records show different rates, salt sources, provincial systems, transit duties and supply routes. Parliamentary speakers disagree over whether equalization reduced the burden overall, and the located evidence lacks a verified district-by-district household budget series. Use “regressive,” “burdened poor consumers” or “reduced consumption” only with a cited region, speaker or study and an explicit caveat.

### Colonial and princely-state terminology

“Native States,” “subjects,” “smuggling,” “illicit salt,” “barbarous,” “oppression” and “civilization” occur in colonial records or later interpretation. Preserve quotation/attribution and explain who used the term. Negotiation with a princely state does not establish popular consent; British control does not erase the agency of state officials, salt producers, carriers or consumers.

### Labour and coercion

The reports make maintenance, patrol and transport visible but do not yet establish a single labour regime. Do not call the work “forced labour,” “indentured” or “voluntary” without a source specific to the operation, district and period. Search wage rolls, contracts, district reports and local histories before making a claim about workers.

### Map and image sensitivity

The Avery/Moxham map lead and O’Carroll fieldwork are useful visual routes, but a modern redrawn map is not a nineteenth-century survey. Verify original map provenance, scale, legend, copyright and whether a surviving embankment is actually connected to the customs line. No image is approved for reuse by this note.

## Claim-risk triage

| Claim or wording | Classification | Safe current handling | Human check required |
|---|---|---|---|
| “Britain built a 2,500-mile hedge across India.” | Misleading shorthand | Say that the wider Inland Customs Line is described as nearly 2,500 miles in a later official synthesis and incorporated live hedge sections plus other barriers. | Reconcile S1, S2, S3 and S11 by year/component. |
| “The entire hedge was continuous and living.” | Contradicted by primary snapshot | Do not use. S2 explicitly describes gaps and dry/stone alternatives. | Inspect full report maps/tables for other divisions. |
| “The Great Hedge was built in one year/by one man.” | Oversimplification | Describe a multi-decade, changing administrative system; Hume’s role was important in strengthening sections, not proof of sole authorship. | Retrieve the early line reports and Moxham pages. |
| “The tax made people starve” or caused a quantified health crisis. | Causal/health claim | State only the tax rates, price/consumption arguments and available evidence; no causal health claim yet. | Retrieve medical, famine, district and consumption records; consult a specialist. |
| “The tax was harmless because it replaced transit duties.” | One-sided official rationale | Present the replacement argument alongside critics’ food-tax/famine objections and regional price evidence. | Verify whether and where transit duties actually fell. |
| “Everyone crossing was searched” or “people could not cross.” | Overbroad operational claim | Say the law authorized regulated crossings and searches; do not infer universal enforcement. | Retrieve rules, guard-post returns and prosecution records. |
| “The line divided India.” | Geographically imprecise | Say it crossed or separated specified British and princely territories and changed over time. | Use contemporary maps and political boundaries, not only modern maps. |
| “Local people built it” / “Britain forced villagers to build it.” | Under-evidenced labour claim | Say labour and maintenance were substantial, while the current set does not identify a single labour regime. | Find wage, contract, district and oral-history evidence. |
| “The line disappeared in 1879.” | Needs qualification | Say the wider line was abandoned in 1879 except an Indus portion according to S11; hedge maintenance and local remnants need separate checks. | Locate the abolition notice and last remnant records. |

## Mandatory human-verification gate

Before any material claim advances, a human must:

1. Inspect the page images and tables in S1/S2, especially the 1867–68 introduction and Hissar Appendix II, and record exact pagination.
2. Verify every QDL transcription in Mss Eur F126/74, including the 1,200-mile length, £105,728 cost, 8,578 staff, revenue figures and 250-mile Bombay sections.
3. Retrieve the official 1843 and 1875 Acts and confirm legal sections, penalties, search powers and territorial scope.
4. Retrieve the 1874 partial-abandonment and 1879 abolition notices; distinguish line length, live-hedge length and any Indus remnant.
5. Retrieve Plowden’s report and later salt-administration records for price, production, seizure, prosecution and consumption data.
6. Check the sources behind the 2,500-mile, 2,504-mile, 1,817-mile, 1,450-mile and 1,200-mile figures; do not blend them into one measurement.
7. Obtain regional/local-language or specialist review covering Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Central India, Bengal/Orissa, princely-state revenue and labour.
8. Obtain rights/provenance checks for maps, photographs, scans and any surviving landscape trace before visual use.

Until these checks are complete, this pack remains in **Research** and must not advance to scriptwriting, packaging, visual generation, editing, QC or publication.
