## Historical and source sensitivity

- Bologna and Modena are living cities with active civic identities. Attribute medieval actions to named communes, rulers, commanders, or chroniclers; do not turn the episode into a present-day insult toward residents.
- “War of the Bucket” is a later label. The Italian *secchia rapita* means a seized/stolen bucket; modern English “rape” can imply sexual violence and should not be used without a clear historical-language explanation.
- “Guelph” and “Ghibelline” are useful period labels but do not describe every local alliance, economic interest, exile network, or territorial dispute. Avoid presenting them as modern political parties.
- The episode involved real military violence, deaths, prisoners, damaged settlements, and political humiliation. Do not let the comic object erase the human cost or describe the conflict as harmlessly silly.
- Current heritage pages and commemorations are reception evidence. They preserve how Modena and Bologna remember the episode, not necessarily what a 1325 witness wrote.

## Source and translation risks

### Retrospective chronicles

The Modena chronicle witness used here is a later, transmitted civic record. Griffoni’s *Memoriale* was compiled around 1411, also retrospectively. Both are valuable, but neither should be presented as an eyewitness transcript. A specialist must inspect the critical editions, manuscript relationships, and exact wording of the “Conflictus Zapolini” passage.

### Silent or missing evidence

The Modena 1325 entry records the defeat and pursuit but not the bucket. That is a meaningful limitation, not proof that no bucket was taken. Conversely, a later heritage statement that the bucket was taken is not a primary document proving timing or causation.

### Literary deformation

Tassoni’s poem is explicitly mock-heroic and its annotations acknowledge chronological rearrangement. “The poem says” and “the war record says” must remain separate. The poem may preserve local traditions while also exaggerating, combining, and satirising them.

### Numbers and tactical detail

Online retellings often repeat very large army and casualty numbers. The source pack does not treat those totals as ready for narration. They require a human to compare the critical Griffoni edition, Modena chronicle witnesses, and modern military-history scholarship. Medieval chroniclers’ numbers are not automatically censuses.

### Italian and Latin translation

Have an Italian medievalist check:

- *per cagion della quale è fama che nascesse tal guerra*;
- *Nell’anno 1325 i Bolognesi furono disfatti e messi in fuga dai Modenesi presso Zappolino*;
- *pallium* / *palio* and the context of the red cloth in the Modena chronicle;
- the exact wording and dating of Griffoni’s “Conflictus Zapolini.”

Do not silently translate *fama* (“reputation,” “it is said,” “it is reputed”) into a factual causal assertion.

## Legal and ethical sensitivity

- Defamation risk is low because the principal actors are long dead, but avoid inventing motives for Passerino, Azzone, the Bolognese commune, or later chroniclers.
- Avoid alleging “theft” as a legally established act by named individuals unless the source supports it; “later tradition reports that a bucket was taken as a trophy” is safer.
- Do not use the story to make claims about modern Italian regional character, civic dishonesty, or national politics.
- Respect cultural-property uncertainty. Do not state that the displayed Modena bucket is authenticated, stolen property, or legally claimable by Bologna without an object-history and legal review.
- If later work discusses the title’s English translation, flag the possible sexual-violence misunderstanding and prefer a historically accurate explanation of *rapita*.

## Human-verification stop condition

**Stop the research-to-script handoff until a human reviewer has completed all of the following:**

1. Read the exact 1325 entries in the critical editions of Griffoni and the relevant Modena chronicle witness; record edition, page, manuscript, and translation details.
2. Verify whether Griffoni gives 15 November, another date, or a calendar convention that explains the modern date.
3. Check the documentary record for the Monteveglio episode, the 1326 peace, prisoners, border terms, and the identities/roles of Passerino and Azzone.
4. Compare all medieval or early-modern references to the bucket and determine whether any source demonstrably places the taking before the conflict, during the battle, or after the victory.
5. Ask a medieval Italian historian to review the translations of *fama*, *pallium/palio*, and the bucket passages.
6. Ask a collections specialist or Modena archivist to verify the current object’s provenance, custody history, and whether “original” is an institutional claim or a documented authentication.
7. Remove or qualify every army-size, casualty, commander, and tactical claim that lacks a critical-edition locator.
8. Confirm that any future narration distinguishes established fact, later report, literary invention, and working interpretation.

Until those checks are complete, this pack is **not narration-ready** and must not advance to scriptwriting, creative development, visual generation, editing, QC, or publication.
