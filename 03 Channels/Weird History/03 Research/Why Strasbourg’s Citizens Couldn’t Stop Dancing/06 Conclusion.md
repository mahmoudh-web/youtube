## Narrowest defensible conclusion

In summer 1518, Strasbourg's magistrates documented and responded to an unusual episode in which people danced compulsively or abnormally for weeks. The surviving municipal records establish a commission, medical consultation, a planned pilgrimage to Saint Guy at Hohlenstein above Saverne, guild/civic involvement, and a 2 August ban on public dancing and drums. The episode then eased by late summer or September.

The popular story adds a named first dancer, several hundred participants, stages, paid musicians, and people dancing to death. Those details come from layered chronicle and later medical traditions whose dates, counts and wording do not align. A precise scale and death toll cannot be recovered from the sources used here. The name “Frau Troffea” is not secure in the direct municipal record.

The cause remains open. Strasbourg physicians in 1518 described a natural illness linked to astral conjunction and heat, with hot blood in the Brant tradition. Later interpretations propose ergotism, stress-related mass psychogenic illness, conversion-like symptoms, religious trance or other models. None is demonstrated by the surviving evidence, and a modern diagnosis should not be projected backward as a solved answer.

## Confidence summary

- **High:** the 1518 Strasbourg episode occurred; city authorities intervened; 23/25 July records and the 2 August ban exist; pilgrimage and public-dance restrictions were part of the response; the episode declined by September.
- **Medium:** broad early counts around 34–50; the role of guilds and supervised treatment; later chronicle reports of more than 400; the precise sequence of early municipal measures.
- **Low / unresolved:** 14 July as the exact start; “Frau Troffea” as an identified person; stages and paid musicians as a complete factual sequence; any exact death toll; “15 deaths per day”; ergotism; mass psychogenic illness; voluntary ritual versus involuntary illness.
