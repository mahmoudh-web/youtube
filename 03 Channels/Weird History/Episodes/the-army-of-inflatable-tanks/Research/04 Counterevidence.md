## Counterevidence Register

| Narrative pressure | Counterevidence / limitation | Editorial consequence |
|---|---|---|
| “Every major Ghost Army operation succeeded.” | Toomey assesses Brittany as marginal; Brest’s surrender objective failed; Kodak is expressly difficult to evaluate. | Use operation-level grades only as provisional research shorthand, never as a universal verdict. |
| “The Germans were completely fooled at the Rhine.” | The captured overlay supports a false picture of some formations, but Eldridge reports another German LXXXVI Corps estimate that retained useful information and argues the overlay has been overused as proof. | Say German records show mislocation or mistaken expectations in particular respects, not total blindness. |
| “Ghost Army caused the Rhine crossing’s surprise.” | German Army Group H/Lüttwitz reports cited by Eldridge also identify increased bombing and artillery preparation as cues of an imminent attack. | Attribute surprise to a combination of deception, operational security, Allied force movements, and other battlefield signals. |
| “Lives saved can be counted.” | Public estimates conflict: 10,000–40,000, about 25,000, or unspecified “thousands.” No baseline, model, or counterfactual causal method was located. | Do not state a precise number. Report only attributed estimates with a low-confidence warning. |
| “Dummy equipment was harmless.” | At Brest, the official account says five real 709th tanks were destroyed after communication/coordination failure. | Include the friendly-risk lesson if the operation is discussed; do not sensationalize. |
| “The official history is an enemy record.” | Fox’s history is a U.S. postwar official history, mirrored by GALP; German overlay and POW evidence are mediated through U.S. reports. | Label provenance at every use. A captured document is not the same as a direct, fully contextualized German assessment. |
| “Veteran memory settles disputed details.” | Rebh’s recollection conflicts with the official-history St-Lô claim and was recorded decades after the events. | Use oral history as valuable perspective and a lead to corroboration, not as sole proof. |

## Source-Bias Audit

- **Fox official history:** unusually valuable proximity to the unit, but retrospective, institutional, and potentially inclined to preserve a coherent success narrative.
- **GALP web pages:** accessible and richly sourced, but transcriptions/curated summaries require comparison with NARA originals.
- **U.S. G-2 and after-action reports:** operationally useful and often contemporaneous, but written by the side evaluating its own deception and may infer enemy belief from partial evidence.
- **Captured German material:** potentially the strongest test of effect, but provenance, translation, date, echelon, and what the document did not know all matter.
- **Veteran interviews:** preserve method and lived experience; memory, interview framing, and elapsed time can alter precision.
- **Toomey and Eldridge:** valuable analysis, but neither replaces inspection of the primary records they cite.

## Negative Evidence to Preserve

1. The wider Brest surrender objective failed even though some fire and force movements supported the tactical ruse.
2. Brittany did not cause the German withdrawal hoped for by the deception planners.
3. Kodak has no located outcome measure strong enough for a success claim.
4. Viersen evidence is internally non-uniform: one captured overlay and POW reports support misdirection, while another German estimate and other battlefield cues complicate the picture.
5. No located source proves the number of lives saved.

## Safe Interpretive Synthesis

The evidence is strongest for bounded effects: drawing fire away from real units, shifting apparent force allocation, sustaining a fictitious formation, and misdirecting some German expectations about the Rhine crossing. It is not strong enough for a universal success story, a single-cause explanation of Allied outcomes, or a precise casualty/lives-saved calculation.
