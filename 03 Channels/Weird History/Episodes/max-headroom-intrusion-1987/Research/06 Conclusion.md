## Provisional assessment

The central event is well documented: on 22 November 1987, a masked performer interrupted WGN-TV’s news for roughly half a minute and later interrupted WTTW’s *Doctor Who* broadcast for roughly 90 seconds. Contemporary reporting says WGN restored its signal by changing the frequency of a studio-to-transmitter link. WTTW’s later engineering account says the operation required sophisticated technical knowledge. Together, these sources establish an unusually deliberate broadcast intrusion and a meaningful contrast between the stations’ recoveries.

They do not answer the title question. The located public record does not identify the performer(s), prove an insider role, establish the exact equipment or RF route, or prove why the broadcast was made. Contemporary and later sources report an FCC investigation, but no public FCC technical finding was located; a 2022 FCC FOIA request is marked “No Responsive Documents.” FBI FOIA logs show later requests for records, not the contents or existence of a 1987 Bureau investigation. The safest current promise is therefore: **the surviving broadcasts and station accounts show how the intrusion worked at a broad level, but the public record still does not identify who sent it or why.**

## What the pack can currently establish

- Two related-looking interruptions occurred on Chicago television on 22 November 1987.
- WGN’s interruption occurred during its 9 p.m. news, in the sports segment, and lasted about 25–30 seconds according to differing contemporary and later accounts.
- WGN’s normal signal returned after an engineer changed the frequency of the studio-to-transmitter link, according to the contemporary *Chicago Tribune* account.
- WTTW’s later station retrospective places the second interruption during *Doctor Who* and describes about 90 seconds of masked, garbled, obscene parody before the normal programme returned.
- Contemporary reporting described microwave links between station studios and downtown transmitters and quoted engineers about the need for a strong interfering signal.
- A WTTW engineer later assessed the required knowledge as potentially combining broadcast, satellite and amateur-radio expertise. This is attribution, not a suspect identification.
- The FCC was reported to be investigating and treating the matter seriously. The complete FCC investigative record or technical conclusion was not located here.
- Official FBI FOIA logs list Max Headroom-related records requests opened in 2017, 2018 and 2023. Those entries are records-request metadata, not evidence that the FBI solved or publicly identified the culprit.
- A public FCC FOIA request from 2022 is marked “No Responsive Documents.” That result is bounded to the request and does not prove historical records never existed.
- The identity, number of participants, motive, equipment, launch location, exact RF path, legal limitations and archive rights remain unresolved.

## Editorial interpretation, clearly labelled

The strongest interpretation is about infrastructure and institutional vulnerability: the first interruption exposed a control path that WGN could counter quickly, while the second remained long enough to become a memorable broadcast artefact. The content appears media-literate and deliberately parodic, but its references cannot establish a motive. The case endured because the audiovisual evidence is vivid while the technical and investigative records needed to identify its source are incomplete or unavailable.

That interpretation is derived from the sequence, station accounts and contemporary engineering descriptions. It is not proof of an insider, a particular geographic launch point, a coordinated plan beyond the two interruptions, or a hidden message.
