## Why counterevidence matters

The barrage is not in doubt; the target is. The accessible record contains incompatible official statements, divided witnesses, and later explanations that do not all describe the same stage of the event. The research question should therefore be framed as “how did an unconfirmed target generate a real military response?” rather than “which dramatic object was definitely there?”

## Hypothesis matrix

| Hypothesis | Evidence in its favour | Counterevidence / limitation | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Japanese or other enemy aircraft attacked Los Angeles | Radar and visual reports were treated seriously; Stimson reported as many as fifteen aircraft; the Army history allowed one to five unidentified planes. | No bombs, no confirmed or shot-down aircraft, no recovered wreckage in the located sources; Knox rejected the raid; Japan’s involvement is not established by the sources in this pack. | Possible in contemporary official reporting; not established. |
| A false alarm with no aircraft | Knox called it a false alarm; WDC acknowledged conflicting reports and no aircraft shot down; 1949 inquiry recollection says about half the witnesses saw none; official Army history says the Navy rejected the firing. | The Army retained one-to-five unidentified planes; the radar contact and some witness reports remain unexplained; “no aircraft confirmed” is not identical to “no aircraft present.” | Strong for the absence of a confirmed attack; not a complete causal reconstruction. |
| A weather balloon initiated a chain reaction | Official Army history says the first shot was aimed at a probable meteorological balloon; Droke’s 1949 account identifies a meteorological balloon and describes perceived targets multiplying after firing; later Smithsonian synthesis calls a stray balloon likeliest. | The 1949 timing (about 1 a.m.) conflicts with the official history’s first-shot timing shortly after 3 a.m.; the actual balloon launch/recovery and radar correlation are not in the pack. | Leading later explanation; requires primary verification. |
| Searchlights, shell bursts, and wartime expectation multiplied perceived targets | Droke describes firing, searchlights, and imagination creating targets; the Space Force booklet records searchlights and shellfire spreading across the coast; the event followed Pearl Harbor and the Ellwood shelling. | This explains visual escalation better than the initial radar report; no instrument records or visibility analysis were inspected. | Plausible mechanism, not proven whole-event account. |
| Extraterrestrial craft / modern UFO event | Later popular culture uses the photograph and mystery framing; some later accounts reinterpret “unidentified” as “UFO.” | No located 1942 document uses extraterrestrial language; contemporary categories were enemy aircraft, balloon, airplane, or unknown; the image’s alteration history is unresolved. | Later cultural interpretation; not evidence. |

## Official disagreement is itself evidence

- **Western Defense Command, 25 February:** no bombs, no planes shot down, conflicting reports, investigation continuing (S4).
- **Navy Secretary Frank Knox:** no planes found; false alarm (S5).
- **Army Secretary Henry Stimson:** as many as fifteen aircraft, possibly commercial planes operated by enemy agents; investigation continuing (S5).
- **Later official Army history:** one to five unidentified planes may have touched off the event; Navy concluded there was no excuse for the firing (S1/S2).

These should be shown as a sequence of official positions, not merged into a single “government explanation.”

## Witness and image counterevidence

- Droke’s 1949 recollection reports roughly equal witness disagreement and even different operators looking through scopes on the same instrument reporting different results (S6, PDF p. 5, lines 119–129).
- Edward Bennett’s later recollection describes three high planes, but the booklet does not supply an independent observer, flight log, or contemporaneous statement that corroborates the count (S3, PDF pp. 4–5, lines 113–120).
- The Times photograph is evidence that a dramatic image was published. It is not, by itself, evidence of the object’s identity, position, number of objects, or unaltered appearance. The Times archive article’s note about period retouching makes the negative/print trail a required check (S9).

## Negative findings that must not be overstated

- The pack has not found an original radar plot, command-room log, firing log, coroner file, or full Western Defense Command after-action report. This means “not located in this pass,” not “does not exist.”
- No source inspected here proves that Japanese aircraft were absent from every part of the Southern California sky; the safe claim is that no enemy aircraft was confirmed or shot down in the located record.
- No source inspected here proves that a balloon was the only object involved; the safe claim is that a balloon/war-nerves chain reaction is the leading later explanation.
