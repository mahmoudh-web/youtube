## Popular version being tested

The familiar story says that on 14 July 1518 a woman called Frau Troffea began dancing in a Strasbourg street, hundreds joined her, the city hired musicians and built stages to make the sufferers dance the illness away, many died, and the outbreak was finally cured by a pilgrimage to Saint Guy. The archive supports a strange public-health episode and municipal intervention, but not every part of this sequence at equal confidence.

## What survives the simplification

### 1. The event is not only a later legend

The magistrate's 25 July letter (ADBR 12 J 1495), the 23 July municipal materials (AMS III 200/15), and the 2 August Brant-signed order (AMS 1 MR3, f° 72r) are direct archival anchors. They show that officials handled an unusual illness, consulted physicians, sent some affected people toward Saint Guy at Saverne, involved civic structures, and restricted public dancing and instruments. See the [Source Index](01%20Source%20Index.md).

The safe core is therefore: **Strasbourg experienced a documented episode of compulsive or abnormal dancing in summer 1518, and its magistrates responded as if it were both a health problem and a threat to public order.**

### 2. The archive is small but meaningful

The Archives de Strasbourg says little survives in the relevant **1 MR3** and **III 200/15** holdings. Its current summary mentions inquiries of relatives, Saverne, guild involvement, and the 2 August ban. This is not a large case file or a day-by-day register. Sparse records explain why later chroniclers have supplied so much of the story. See [S6 in the Source Index](01%20Source%20Index.md).

### 3. “Frau Troffea” is a retrospective identity

The direct municipal letter says the episode began with a woman but does not name her. Clementz explicitly warns that “Frau Troffea” is not present in the only authentic document that attributes the beginning to a woman. The name enters through later Paracelsian tradition and its publication history. Use “an unnamed woman in the 25 July letter” for the secure version; use “Frau Troffea” only as a later attribution. See [S2–S3 in the Source Index](01%20Source%20Index.md).

### 4. Scale is a layered set of reports, not a census

The material gives several non-equivalent numbers: roughly 34 within a few days, about 50 in one early account, and more than 400 within four weeks in the later Imlin chronicle. Clementz notes the disagreement and warns that “affected” figures should not be treated as precise epidemiological counts. Use ranges and source labels, not “400 people definitely danced.” See [S8 and S12 in the Source Index](01%20Source%20Index.md).

### 5. Deaths are the most dangerous vivid claim

Later accounts and modern retellings report 15 deaths per day or people dancing to death. Clementz states that the exact number who died cannot be determined and that some historians say no document certifies deaths from exhaustion. That is not proof that nobody died; it is proof that a confident death toll is unavailable. The pack therefore records deaths as **unresolved**, not as zero and not as “hundreds.” See C11–C12 in the [Claim Ledger](03%20Claim%20Ledger.md).

### 6. The municipal response did change

The 25 July letter describes a natural illness according to the physicians and the 2 August order bans public dancing and drums because they supposedly interfere with recovery. Brant/Miller material and later chronicles describe music, supervised dancing, halls or market spaces, then restriction and pilgrimage. The general movement from attempted management to restriction is plausible; the most theatrical version—purpose-built stages, paid musicians, and a mass public spectacle—is less secure and depends heavily on later Specklin/Wencker traditions. See S5, S7, S10 and S11 in the [Source Index](01%20Source%20Index.md).

## Cause: competing explanations, no solved diagnosis

### Contemporary medical explanation

The 25 July letter reports that doctors called the condition natural, linked to astral conjunction and heat. Brant's later extract describes hot blood and dietary/instrument advice. This establishes what authorities thought, not what caused the movements. See S3, S7 and S12 in the [Source Index](01%20Source%20Index.md).

### Ergotism

Ergot poisoning is a plausible-sounding modern theory because ergot can produce neurological and psychiatric symptoms. Clementz argues it fits poorly with the reported pattern, spontaneous decline and absence of a reported ergotism syndrome in Strasbourg. Marillier and Godard still discuss it as a possible etiology while concluding that the precise cause is unknown. The evidence cannot confirm or rule it out with laboratory certainty. See S12 and S15 in the [Source Index](01%20Source%20Index.md).

### Stress / mass psychogenic illness / conversion

Waller's influential interpretation connects the episode to severe stress and culturally available beliefs; modern medical writers discuss mass psychogenic illness or conversion-like symptoms. Miller and Gotman caution, in different ways, against treating historical bodies as transparent examples of a modern diagnosis. These are interpretive models, not settled facts. See S11, S13 and S14 in the [Source Index](01%20Source%20Index.md).

### Religious-cultural trance

The name “Saint Vitus dance,” pilgrimage, music and ritual healing belong to a regional religious culture in which dance could be both symptom and remedy. This makes belief and social expectation historically relevant. It does not prove that the episode was voluntary ritual, nor that “trance” is a diagnosis. See S1, S11 and S12 in the [Source Index](01%20Source%20Index.md).

## Working verdict

The defensible story is narrower and more interesting than the standard one: a real Strasbourg episode prompted unusually detailed municipal action, but the surviving record is too sparse and the later chronicles too layered to support a precise scale, death toll, named patient zero, or single cause. The unresolved gap is part of the historical subject and must remain visible.
