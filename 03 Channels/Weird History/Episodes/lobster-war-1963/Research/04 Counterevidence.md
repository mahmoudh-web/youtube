## 1. “War” versus crisis and armed persuasion

The title is historically recognisable, but the located record does not show a formal declaration, exchange of fire or naval battle. The Brazilian Navy’s own *Amazônia Azul* account says the press labelled the episode a war but that it “did not pass beyond armed persuasion” and credits naval presence with helping bring about the French withdrawal (S3, p. 29). A later legal synthesis likewise describes the first response as shows of force followed by diplomacy, not combat (S10, PDF pp. 9–13).

Safe treatment: “Lobster War” as an ironic/media label; “naval confrontation,” “coercive diplomacy” or “armed persuasion” as the factual description. Human review should decide whether “sent warships” is acceptable packaging language without implying that war occurred.

## 2. The withdrawal date is not one settled date

Two source clusters describe different endpoints:

- The official Brazilian Navy institutional page dates French withdrawal from the disputed area to **24 February 1963** (S3, p. 29).
- The detailed maritime retrospective places *Paul Goffeny* and four fishing vessels leaving the Atol das Rocas area on **10 March**, after France announced on 8 March that the boats would return and the sloop would be recalled (S18, lines 155–165; S10, PDF p. 13).
- BNDigital states only that the French withdrew in **March 1963** (S5).

These may refer to different movements: an initial displacement, the removal of *Tartu*, a change of mission, or final departure of the remaining vessels. Do not “correct” one source to fit another until naval logs and diplomatic traffic are inspected.

## 3. “Clemenceau was sent” versus “Tartu was detached”

The retrospective Brazilian account lists a French task force that departed Toulon, with *Clemenceau* and *De Grasse*, but says *Tartu* later took a separate route to Brazil and that reports of *De Grasse* accompanying it were unconfirmed (S18, lines 52–63). The 1974 legal synthesis records reports that *Clemenceau* and *De Grasse* might sail from Dakar but also records French denial and the presence of Brazilian ships near Recife (S10, PDF pp. 11–12). The official Navy summary focuses on *Tartu*.

Safe treatment: state that *Tartu* was sent/detached to protect French fishing interests; describe the larger force as a nearby or reported capability only after verifying French operational records. Do not portray an aircraft-carrier standoff that the sources do not establish.

## 4. The legal issue was broader than biology

The repeated “swim or jump?” framing is useful only if it is tied to the treaty category. Article 2(4) of the 1958 Continental Shelf Convention defined sedentary species by immobility or constant physical contact with the seabed/subsoil (S8; S1, pp. 72–74). Brazil’s legal opinion argued that crustaceans belonged within the resource category and that the conference rejected a proposed crustacean exclusion (S1, pp. 73–74). France’s position, as reproduced in the legal record and later scholarship, treated the lobster’s ability to swim as removing it from sedentary resources and placing it with high-seas fisheries (S1, pp. 70–71; S10, pp. 14–15).

The dispute therefore concerned who could regulate and profit from a resource under unsettled law, not a scientific contest whose winner automatically determined sovereignty. The legal opinions also record that neither country had signed the 1958 Convention, making the customary-law and domestic-decree questions central.

## 5. “Brazil won” versus the 1964 compromise

Brazil’s naval presence helped produce a French withdrawal from the immediate 1963 operating area, but the later settlement reported by Messick allowed 26 French vessels to fish for up to five years subject to a return/tribute arrangement (S10, PDF pp. 20–22). That is not a clean legal victory for either state. The agreement’s exact parties, zones, quota/profit formula and implementation remain to be verified from the original text.

Safe treatment: the crisis ended operationally in 1963 and was later regularised through diplomacy in 1964; avoid “Brazil won” unless a human reviewer approves a clearly attributed political reading.

## 6. Local fishing interests and industrial fishing are not interchangeable

Brazilian legal advice invoked northeastern jangadeiros and the region’s economic and social development (S1, pp. 70–71 and 152). Later legal scholarship describes apprehension about more modern French boats and declining Breton catches (S10, pp. 14–18). These are evidence of competing economic stakes, not proof that every Brazilian fisher or every Breton fisher held the same position. Obtain Brazilian fisheries statistics, association records and French armateur/crew sources before making claims about livelihoods, depletion or exploitation.

## 7. Cold War context is not a causal explanation

The dispute occurred during the Cold War, and the 2021 Escola de Guerra Naval dissertation deliberately reads it alongside the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis (S11). That makes the period relevant to the language of alliance, naval readiness and escalation, but it does not establish that Washington, Moscow, NATO or the Cuban crisis directed events. A 1974 synthesis repeats a contemporary suggestion that U.S. buyers had an economic interest in Brazilian lobster and did not want French competition (S10, PDF pp. 16–17); this is a reported allegation/interpretation, not verified evidence of U.S. intervention.

Safe treatment: use “Cold War-era coercive diplomacy” as an analytical frame only when clearly marked as interpretation. Do not say the crisis was a proxy confrontation or that the United States backed Brazil without primary diplomatic records.

## 8. The famous quotation has an attribution problem

Later Brazilian Navy writing attributes the kangaroo analogy to Paulo Moreira da Silva (S18, lines 36–38; S19, p. 96). A later maritime article also preserves competing attribution problems around other famous phrases from the crisis (S18, lines 46–50). The pack has not located the original 1963 minutes, press transcript or the cited 2004 memoir/article that would establish exact wording, speaker, language, audience and date.

Safe treatment: paraphrase the legal positions in the ledger; if the analogy is retained at all, label it a later-attributed anecdote and verify the primary chain first.

## 9. “Illegal fishing” is a position, not an automatic legal finding

Brazilian Navy and later Brazilian accounts call the French activity unauthorised or illegal under Brazilian law (S2; S18). French diplomatic records described seizures beyond territorial waters as legally objectionable and sought arbitration (S1/S7). The disagreement itself is central evidence. Use “Brazil treated the fishing as unauthorised” and “France protested/seeking arbitration,” not “France illegally stole Brazilian lobsters,” unless a competent legal record supports that exact wording.

## 10. Source-bias map

| Source cluster | What it does well | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Brazilian Itamaraty opinions | Reproduces legal texts, notes and Brazil’s reasoning | A neutral legal outcome or the complete French position |
| Brazilian Navy institutional histories | Identifies operations, ships and institutional interpretation | Full French operational intent, independent chronology or neutral assessment of readiness |
| French diplomatic/parliamentary records | Shows French official framing and archive trail | The truth of the French legal claim or complete Brazilian response |
| Contemporary press | Captures public rhetoric, timing and reported movements | Complete logs, neutral casualty/position data or legal adjudication |
| Later legal scholarship | Clarifies the legal category and dispute-resolution context | Direct proof for every date or quotation in a noisy retrospective |
| Later maritime memoir/research accounts | Supplies ship names, operational detail and quotation leads | A substitute for primary logs, exact speech provenance or a simple winner |

## Research implication

The strongest counterevidence does not erase the episode. It narrows it: a real resource and jurisdiction dispute escalated through seizures, diplomatic notes and naval positioning, but the located sources support a negotiated crisis more securely than a conventional war narrative.
