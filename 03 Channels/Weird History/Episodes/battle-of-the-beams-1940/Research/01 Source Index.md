## Research question

How did German radio beams guide bombers toward British targets in 1940, how did Britain discover and interfere with them, and what does the wartime record justify calling a “battle”?

## Working answer and scope

The located record supports an intelligence-and-engineering contest rather than a single battle. British signals intelligence and technical examination led R. V. Jones and colleagues to a June 1940 airborne search that detected a Knickebein signal. Britain then organised No. 80 Wing and developed several countermeasures: interference with the beam, false or spoof transmissions, meaconing of navigation beacons, and attacks or defensive measures associated with predicted raids. German systems evolved from Knickebein to the more precise X-Gerät and remotely controlled Y-Gerät, while British code names and later accounts do not always align.

The pack focuses on 1940 and the Coventry test case, with 1941 follow-through included only where it clarifies the technical contest. It does not claim that jamming alone determined bombing outcomes, and it does not treat later memoirs or simplified diagrams as a complete operational record.

## Source-quality key

- **A — primary/contemporary or official record:** wartime Air Ministry material, official history, archive transcript, operational record lead or institutional collection record.
- **B — authoritative institutional synthesis:** RAF Museum or NSA historical account that identifies sources and preserves operational context.
- **C — later specialist analysis:** RAF Historical Society paper using official raid reports, technical accounts and post-war interviews; valuable but interpretive.
- **D — access/transcription mirror:** readable reproduction of a source whose original catalogue item or scan still needs checking.

## Source-handling rules

1. Say “radio-navigation beam” rather than “radar beam” unless quoting a source.
2. Distinguish German system names from British code words: Knickebein, X-Gerät and Y-Gerät are not interchangeable, and “Ruffian” is not stable across the located accounts.
3. Attribute later interpretations such as “jamming caused distrust” or “weather mattered more than the beams.”
4. Treat the June 1940 search flight as a documented confirmation in the official narrative, while retaining the intelligence and classification trail behind it.
5. Treat archive catalogue entries as routes to verification, never as proof of the contents of uninspected files.
6. No visual asset is approved for production by this research pack.

## Visual opportunities (research leads only)

- **Conceptual beam diagram:** a clearly labelled reconstruction showing dot/dash overlap, a Knickebein cross-beam and the distinct X-Gerät multi-cross-beam logic. Base it on S3/S7, mark it “conceptual,” and do not present the diagram as an archival photograph or exact station geometry.
- **June 1940 intelligence chain:** a map/timeline connecting the Y Service intercept, technical examination, Jones’s hypothesis and the Anson search. Use only cleared map data; do not expose sensitive source detail beyond the public record.
- **Equipment and operators:** archival photographs or scans of receivers, transmitters, No. 80 Wing/TRE work and 1940 aircraft, subject to TNA/RAF Museum/other rights-holder clearance and accurate captions.
- **Cold Water operational plan:** a document-inspired treatment of AIR 2/5238 showing the categories of countermeasure, with any reproduction, transcription or audio use cleared by The National Archives.
- **Coventry case study:** a non-gratuitous map/timeline of KGr 100, the BROMIDE position and the raid context, with civilian-harm framing and no claim that one jammer determined the outcome.
- **Code-name comparison card:** a source-attributed table for Knickebein, X-Gerät, Y-Gerät, Ruffian, Aspirin and Bromide that visibly marks unresolved mappings rather than implying certainty.

These are storyboarding leads, not approved assets. Human review must record source provenance, rights/licence, caption accuracy, sensitivity treatment and whether a visual makes a disputed technical claim look settled.
