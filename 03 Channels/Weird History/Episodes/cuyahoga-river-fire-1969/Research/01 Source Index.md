## Research question

What happened in the June 22, 1969 Cuyahoga River fire, why did this fire become nationally famous when earlier fires did not, and how much can be responsibly attributed to it in the creation of the EPA and passage of the Clean Water Act?

## Working answer and scope

The located record supports a bounded answer: the fire was a brief, relatively small industrial-waterway fire near railroad bridges, but it arrived amid growing concern about Lake Erie, pollution, environmental health and the limits of fragmented regulation. Cleveland mayor Carl Stokes and environmental reporter Betty Klaric helped turn the local incident into a public argument; Time’s August 1 article supplied national reach, using a dramatic photograph from the 1952 fire rather than a verified image of the 1969 flames. The fire helped crystallize public concern, but it did not single-handedly create the EPA or Clean Water Act. Existing water-pollution law, the Santa Barbara spill, Earth Day, congressional work and executive reorganization were also part of the chain.

This pack corrects the episode brief’s June 29 date to **June 22, 1969**. A legacy EPA timeline has a June 29 heading, but its own surrounding history and the stronger contemporary/institutional record identify June 22.

## Source-quality key

- **A — primary/legal/custodial record:** contemporary Congressional Record, statute or executive reorganization plan, original magazine page, archival catalogue or official agency record.
- **B — official historical synthesis:** National Park Service, EPA or Ohio EPA historical material.
- **C — academic/institutional synthesis:** Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland State University or public-media fact checking that identifies source limitations.
- **D — later secondary account:** useful for reception history and triangulation; not sufficient alone for a disputed material claim.
