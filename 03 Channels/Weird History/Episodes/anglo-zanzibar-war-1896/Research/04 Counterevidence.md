## Why the “38 minutes” is not a single uncontested measurement

The slogan depends on which event starts and stops the clock:

| Evidence | Duration / wording | Challenge to a clean 38-minute claim |
|---|---|---|
| Rawson telegram | Fire opened at 09:00; ceased at 09:40 | Officially reported firing interval is 40 minutes. |
| Later naval history | Cease-fire at 09:37 | A source based on despatches and the *Zanzibar Gazette* gives a different endpoint. |
| Bluejackets reconstruction (S17) | Ship fire at 09:02; barrage stopped at 09:48 | Depending on which endpoint is accepted, the interval is 46 minutes. |
| Western Times / Foreign Office message | Firing “continued for an hour” | A same-day report uses a rounded one-hour description. |
| Daily News, St John’s | Bombardment lasted 50 minutes | Delayed reporting gave a fifth interval. |
| Historical Association | 38, 40 or 43 minutes | The public-facing historical summary itself preserves disagreement. |

The safe research conclusion is not that one source is automatically “wrong.” The sources may measure: an order to fire, the first observed discharge, the end of naval firing, the fall of the flag, or the end of resistance. The original logs and dispatches are needed to determine whether the differences are rounding, transmission, or genuinely different endpoints.

## Casualty and damage counterevidence

| Claim commonly repeated | Counterevidence / qualification | Research status |
|---|---|---|
| “Only one person was hurt.” | Rawson’s reproduced telegram names one seriously wounded *Thrush* petty officer; Clowes says one seaman; the Bluejackets reconstruction says two British casualties; the Zanzibari loss is reported as about 500 killed or wounded. | Do not reduce the event to a bloodless British anecdote. Verify returns. |
| “500 people were killed.” | Clowes and later summaries say about 500 killed or wounded; early reports said losses were unknown; some later accounts turn the estimate into a death toll. | Use “about 500 killed or wounded, according to later naval histories,” pending primary returns. |
| “The palace was destroyed and nothing else mattered.” | Contemporary reports describe the palace, old Custom House, harbour craft, streets and surrounding buildings as damaged or burning; a civilian witness described danger to the English Club. | Verify site names and sequence against photographs, maps and dispatches. |
| “The British captured the Sultan.” | Rawson says Khalid surrendered to the German Consul; other reports say he escaped to or took refuge in the German Consulate. | Safer wording: he left the palace and obtained German protection. |

## Counterevidence to simplified political framing

### “It was simply an irrational local coup”

The succession crisis happened inside a protectorate relationship shaped by the 1890 British–German agreement and a separate Great Britain–Zanzibar agreement on protection and succession. The British preference for a successor was not an afterthought. The exact 14 June clause still needs a human reading, but the treaty context makes a purely spontaneous “local coup versus neutral Britain” framing incomplete.

### “Britain annexed Zanzibar immediately”

The 1890 agreement and the 31 August 1896 official notice reproduced by The Bluejackets (S17) distinguish a protectorate from direct annexation. The later British-backed Sultan remained nominal ruler. Do not collapse protectorate, informal empire, and colony into one legal status; verify both texts before using this as a legal conclusion.

### “Khalid’s death or guilt is known”

The located contemporary material reports suspicion that Hamad was poisoned, but no verified forensic or judicial finding was found. Khalid’s motive, German support and responsibility for the death remain separate questions.

### “The shortest war label explains the whole event”

It describes a tiny slice of a longer succession and imperial crisis. The label has reception value, but it can obscure the prior reduction of Zanzibar’s diplomatic room and the human cost of the bombardment.

## Perspective and source bias

- Rawson’s report is an official British military account written after the action; it is essential but interested in presenting the operation as controlled and successful.
- British and international newspapers depended on telegraphic summaries and sometimes used colonial vocabulary (“usurper,” “rebels,” “enemy”) without explaining Zanzibari political positions.
- The *Zanzibar Gazette* and Foreign Office files may preserve local administrative detail, but access and language need checking rather than assuming that British records represent all Zanzibari voices.
- The surviving photograph catalogues document physical damage but cannot, by themselves, establish who was present, what happened to bodies, or the exact timing.

## Required counterevidence search

1. Compare Rawson’s dispatch with the *St George*, *Thrush*, *Racoon/Raccoon*, *Sparrow* and *Philomel* logs or reports of proceedings.
2. Retrieve the 2 September 1896 *Zanzibar Gazette* supplement and any Zanzibar Government casualty, hospital or burial record.
3. Search FO 107/54 and FO 107/57 for Cave’s account, the succession agreement, German protection of Khalid and the appointment of Hamoud.
4. Check whether “43 minutes” derives from a specific primary report, and record that source rather than repeating the number as folklore.
