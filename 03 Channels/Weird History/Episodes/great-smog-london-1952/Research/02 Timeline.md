## Chronology

| Date | Event / evidence | Confidence and caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Late Nov–4 Dec 1952 | Cold weather and coal demand create conditions in which domestic fires, power stations and industrial furnaces emit smoke into a city already accustomed to winter fog. The Met Office describes the preceding cold spell, anticyclone and later inversion; the parliamentary POSTnote describes coal smoke and SO₂ as the main historical pollutants. | High for the general setup; exact fuel volumes and source shares need the original technical reports. |
| 5 Dec 1952 | The smoke-laden fog begins across much of Metropolitan London. The National Archives transcript records the five-day period as 5–9 December. Scott’s contemporary analysis says the intense fog persisted from 5 through 8 December, while the Met Office says the wider episode ran to 9 December. | High for 5 December onset; “fog” and “episode” may have different end definitions. |
| 5 Dec, daytime/night | The Met Office describes a relatively less dense, smoky fog during the day that thickened after nightfall; visibility fell to a few metres in places. The inversion prevented normal vertical mixing, keeping smoke and gases near ground level. | High for mechanism and broad chronology; exact street-by-street visibility needs contemporary records. |
| 6–8 Dec 1952 | Fog and pollution persist. Scott reports the highest London County Hall averages over the weekend ending 10 a.m. Monday 8 December: smoke 4.46 mg/m³ and sulphur dioxide reported as 1.339 ppm in the table/transcript. Wilkins’s abstract reports 12-site averages reaching about 1.6 mg/m³ smoke and 0.7 ppm SO₂, roughly 5–6 times normal. | High that measurements rose sharply; units and the Scott decimal formatting require inspection of the original tables before quotation. |
| 6–8 Dec 1952 | Health services receive increased demand. The Ministry of Health statement later records 501 Emergency Bed Service applications for respiratory disease, with most patients aged 45 and over. | High for the recorded service statistic; this is not a count of all illness. |
| 6–8 Dec 1952 | The Met Office and later official summaries describe travel and public life as heavily disrupted. Exact claims about specific routes, theatres, cinemas, livestock and crime require contemporary newspaper or service records. | Broad disruption: high. Specific anecdotes: open verification item. |
| 9 Dec 1952 | Weather changes and the fog clears. The Met Office calls 9 December the clearing day; the National Archives still defines the smoke-laden period as 5–9 December. | High for clearing by 9 December. The mortality response continues after the air clears. |
| Week ended 13 Dec 1952 | The National Archives transcript of the 19 December Ministry statement gives 4,703 deaths registered in Greater London, more than double the previous two weeks. Scott’s paper gives 2,484 for the administrative county of London for the same week; the datasets must remain separate until their boundary/definition difference is resolved. | High that both figures are recorded; unresolved scope difference. |
| 19 Dec 1952 | Ministry of Health statement says investigations include deaths by age and cause, pathologists’ reports, lung chemistry in fatal cases, and chemical study of fog and smoke. | High; primary statement reproduced by The National Archives. |
| Jan–Mar 1953 | The Ministry report catalogue and later studies track mortality and morbidity beyond the acute fog. Bell and Davis later analyse December 1952–February 1953 and estimate roughly 12,000 excess deaths, rejecting influenza as a complete explanation for the persistence. | High for study period and later estimate; causal interpretation remains a later epidemiological model, not a contemporaneous count. |
| 27 Jan 1953 | In Parliament, the Minister says no interdepartmental committee has investigated the weather conditions and refers to existing action on atmospheric pollution. | High for parliamentary record; illustrates that the response was still being contested. |
| 19 Mar 1953 | Minister of Health reports that a detailed study of hospitals, vital statistics and other sources is underway and says matters reasonably attributable to the fog will be included. | High; primary parliamentary record. |
| 8 May 1953 | Parliamentary debate cites the scale of December mortality and sickness and announces a government decision to appoint a comprehensive committee on air pollution. The debate includes claims and estimates that must not be treated as final statistics. | High for political pressure and committee decision; individual figures in debate vary. |
| 21 Jul 1953 | Committee on Air Pollution is announced under Sir Hugh Beaver. Terms of reference cover the nature, causes and effects of air pollution, existing preventive measures, practicable measures and recommendations. | High; primary Hansard record. |
| 2 Dec 1953 | Government statement says the five-day smog was exceptional and reports interim measures: Met Office warnings for serious fog, public advice, domestic smokeless fuel, and smoke minimisation by industry and other large fuel users. | High for the statement; exact implementation and effectiveness need subsequent records. |
| 25 Nov 1954 | The final Beaver report, Cmd. 9322, is published. Hansard describes its recommendations as a comprehensive national clean-air policy; the Wellcome catalogue identifies the 80-page HMSO report. | High for publication; inspect the report before attributing detailed recommendations or cost estimates. |
| 5 Jul 1954 | The City of London (Various Powers) Act receives Royal Assent and is later described by Parliament as prohibiting smoke emissions in the City. | High for date and legal existence; it is a local measure, not the UK-wide 1956 Act. |
| 4 Feb 1955 | Commons debates the Beaver report and describes the 1952 event as the reason the committee was established; the debate distinguishes the immediate 4,000 figure from later additional deaths. | High for parliamentary framing; speeches are not final epidemiology. |
| 24 Apr–30 May 1956 | Parliament debates the Clean Air Bill. Lords debate says the smog aroused public opinion and that the Bill largely incorporated Beaver recommendations. | High for legislative history; do not infer that every measure was solely caused by the 1952 episode. |
| 5 Jul 1956 | The Clean Air Act 1956 receives Royal Assent. Its text includes restrictions on dark smoke, furnace emissions, grit and dust, smoke-control areas, domestic fuel provisions and a Clean Air Council. | High; primary statute. |

## Chronology controls

- Use **5–9 December** for the public event window, with a note that Scott’s medical analysis treats the dense fog as 5–8 December.
- Use the post-event week ending 13 December to explain why death registrations peaked after visibility improved.
- Never write “4,703 people died in the smog” without saying that this is the Greater London registered-deaths total for the week ended 13 December in the Ministry statement.
- Never write “2,484 people died in the smog” without the administrative-county scope and Scott’s table context.
- Keep the 12,000 Bell–Davis estimate in a separate long-term/extended-effects section.
