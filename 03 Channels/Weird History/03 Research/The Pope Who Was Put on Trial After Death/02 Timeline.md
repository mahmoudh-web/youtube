## Timeline with evidence status

| Date | Event | Evidence and confidence |
|---|---|---|
| c. 866–867 | Formosus, then bishop of Porto, participates in the Roman mission to Bulgaria. Khan Boris later asks for Formosus as archbishop. | Monroe, *Trials*, Ch. 2 (S10), around pp. 40–57; **high for the mission, medium for exact year and later interpretation**. This explains why “ambition” became a later accusation but does not prove it. |
| 876–878 | Formosus falls into conflict with Pope John VIII and leaves Rome for West Francia. The surviving conciliar/letter evidence concerns his deposition or condemnation alongside other Roman officials; the exact legal sequence is difficult to reconstruct. | MGH *Concilia* 5, pp. 53–54 as transcribed/discussed in S10 around pp. 92–94; **medium-high**. Do not compress this into a simple “permanent excommunication”: Marinus later reverses the situation. |
| 11 Aug–mid-Sep 878 | Synod of Troyes meets in West Francia; John VIII’s earlier measures against Lambert and Adalbert are confirmed. Formosus is present in the surrounding exile/rehabilitation story. | S10 around pp. 93–94 and MGH *Concilia* 5 contents; **medium-high** for the synod/date, lower for every Formosus-related detail. |
| 882–884 | Marinus I absolves Formosus from the oath imposed under John VIII and restores him to Porto. | S10, pp. 5–6; **medium-high**. The point matters because Formosus was not an unchangingly condemned figure between 878 and 891. |
| 6 Oct 891 | Formosus becomes pope, leaving Porto for the Roman see. | Holy See’s official record S1; S10 around p. 129; **high for the regnal date, medium-high for the legal framing of the transfer**. |
| 893–895 | The standard later explanation says Formosus secretly invited Arnulf of Carinthia to intervene in Italy and promised him the imperial crown, thereby betraying Lambert. | The *Annales Fuldenses* report the invitation claim for 893; discussed in S10 around pp. 145–148; **medium for the report, low for the motive as a proven fact**. Monroe notes that the evidence is more complicated. |
| 896 | Arnulf captures Rome; Formosus receives him and crowns him emperor at St Peter’s. | Regino, *Chronicon*, S5, Anno 896; *Annales Fuldenses* as quoted in S10; **high for capture/coronation**. S5 does not say Formosus invited Arnulf. |
| 4 Apr 896 | Formosus dies at Rome on Easter, according to the Fulda continuation; the Holy See gives 4 April 896 as the end of his pontificate. | S1 and S3; **high for the date used in the pack**. Medieval annalists’ calendar conventions and wording should still be checked against the critical edition. |
| late 896 / early 897 | Stephen VI becomes pope after Boniface VI’s very short pontificate. A Roman council convened under Stephen subjects Formosus’s dead body to a posthumous judgment. | S2, S3, S4, S9, S10; **high for the core event, uncertain for the exact day/month**. The surviving direct acts of this council are not securely extant as an independent set. |
| same proceedings | The body is removed from its grave and treated as deposed; the Fulda record says an advocate answered for Formosus and that he was buried outside the customary papal burial place. Other annals add the throne, deacon, dragging, mutilation, and river. | S3 is **high** for the sparse version. S4 and Liudprand in S9/S10 are **medium for the broader ritual, lower for individual dramatic details**. |
| spring–summer 897 | The body is reportedly removed again and placed in the Tiber; the attribution differs. Auxilius says Stephen ordered the second removal with weights; the Ravenna material attributes the Tiber violation to people who violated the tomb; other annals combine the episodes. | S9/S10, Ch. 5, around pp. 187–210; **medium for a second removal, low-medium for who ordered it**. This is a key unresolved point. |
| by June–Aug 897 | Stephen is dethroned and imprisoned, then strangled. | Herimannus and Stephen’s later epitaph as quoted in S10 around pp. 161–162; **medium-high for imprisonment/death, medium for the exact causal link to popular outrage**. |
| Dec 897 | Theodore II becomes pope for roughly twenty days. He is reported to restore Formosus’s ordinations and recover/rebury the body at St Peter’s. | S10 around pp. 162–163; the existence and role of a distinct Roman synod are debated; **medium**. |
| Jan–May 898 | John IX succeeds Theodore and the Ravenna synod meets in 898 with Lambert present. Its purpose includes rehabilitation of Formosus and review of the earlier proceedings. | S6, S7, S8, S9, S10; **high for a Ravenna synod and Formosus’s rehabilitation, medium for exact date and the boundaries of each textual section**. |
| later tenth/early eleventh century transmission | The Ravenna dossier survives in differing manuscript versions. Older editions treated parts as separate Roman/Ravenna councils; modern cataloguing and MGH editing revise that picture. | S2, S6, S7, S8, S10; **high for the transmission problem**. This is why “John IX held two clearly documented synods” should not be stated without qualification. |
| c. 920–972 / c. 960s | Liudprand of Cremona writes the *Antapodosis*, roughly sixty years after the event; it becomes the fullest and most memorable narrative but confuses Stephen VI with Sergius III. | S9/S10, Ch. 1, pp. 1–3 and source discussion around pp. 27–28; **high for the relative chronology and source criticism**. |
| 904–911 | Sergius III’s later anti-Formosan policy reopens the ordination dispute in the polemical record. | S12, S14, and S10 around pp. 210–216; **medium-high for renewed controversy, requiring care not to merge it with the 897 trial**. |

## Chronology guardrails

- The event is commonly dated to **January 897**, but the evidence permits a broader **late 896/early 897** window; the pack uses the broader date unless a source specifically says January.
- The Tiber episode may be a second removal after the initial reburial, but the surviving capitula do not make the sequence fully explicit.
- “Theodore’s Roman synod” and “John IX’s Roman synod” are not interchangeable labels. Current source criticism points securely to the Ravenna material while questioning whether a distinct Roman council of John IX is evidenced by surviving contemporary records.
