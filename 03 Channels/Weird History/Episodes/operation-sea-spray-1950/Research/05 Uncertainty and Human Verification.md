## Unresolved factual questions

1. What is the original scan and classification history of Special Report 142? Confirm title page, abstract, maps, appendices, redactions and the report's stated release dates.
2. Was the event formally called “Operation Sea-Spray” in a 1950/1951 record, or is that a later label? Use the formal report title unless a primary record confirms the code name.
3. Which agency sponsored, approved and operated the test? Reconcile the Camp Detrick report, Army programme tables, Navy vessel account and 1977 witness statement.
4. What ship was used, and what was its exact route? The later press account says a Navy auxiliary mine-layer; the primary ship log is not located.
5. Did six trials occur on six days, or does the 20–27 September interval include non-release days? Reconcile the report abstract, trial table and later six-day descriptions.
6. What exact organisms and fluorescent tracer were used on each trial? Confirm whether the report's BG/SM/fluorescent-particle wording applies to all six or only the programme summary.
7. What did the report mean by “success,” and what was the blocked fourth conclusion? Do not reconstruct redacted text from later summaries.
8. What were the exact 43 station locations, measured viable counts and meteorological conditions? Use the primary tables rather than later coverage maps.
9. Are the 23-mile, 117-square-mile and 800,000-person figures comparable? They may refer to different trials, metrics or report sections. Do not treat them as one settled exposure estimate.
10. What were the exact onset dates, diagnoses, treatments and outcomes for the eleven Stanford patients? Obtain the full Wheat paper and any surviving hospital/medical records.
11. Were the Stanford isolates retained, and can any be compared to the 8 UK test strain? The later review says they were not archived; verify from the primary medical and laboratory records.
12. What did the 1977 Farmer et al. CDC comparison actually test, how many isolates were included and what confidence limits did it have? Read the full Lancet article.
13. What did the 1950 medical and military advisers know about *Serratia* pathogenicity and prior human disease? Locate the 1946 Paine paper, Army safety memoranda and contemporaneous literature.
14. Did the military alert Stanford Hospital, San Francisco health authorities or any other public-health body before or after the release? The located report/hearing do not answer this conclusively.
15. What exactly did the district court find about strain identity, route and Nevin's death? Obtain the full findings, exhibits and trial transcript; do not infer from the appellate summary.
16. How should the Ninth Circuit outcome be described? It is a discretionary-function/jurisdiction ruling with a dissent discussing the causation finding, not a general scientific exoneration.
17. Is there evidence for later Bay Area infections, microbial persistence or ecological change? Treat the claim as hypothesis until strain-level and longitudinal evidence exists.

## Language and naming controls

- Say “biological simulant” and “simulated biological-warfare vulnerability test” unless the primary record requires stronger wording.
- Say “Navy vessel in an Army/Camp Detrick-supported programme” until agency responsibility is verified.
- Say “the report records six trials from 20–27 September” rather than “the Navy sprayed the city for six days” as an unqualified fact.
- Say “eleven Stanford patients were reported with *Serratia* infections” and “one died of bacterial endocarditis”; do not add that the release caused either outcome.
- Use “temporally associated,” “alleged,” “the Army argued,” “the medical paper reported” and “not established” precisely.
- Do not call the later message/report a “cover-up” unless a source directly supports that characterization; “not publicly disclosed at the time” is safer.
- Do not use “poisoned,” “bioweapon attack,” “mass human experiment,” “killed” or “one of the largest” as narrator voice without human legal/editorial approval.

## Legal, ethical, medical and editorial flags

- This concerns unwitting civilian exposure to live organisms, an unusual hospital cluster and a death. Avoid graphic medical detail and sensational “own citizens” framing.
- The Nevin family's causal allegation must remain clearly labelled. Do not identify the U.S. government as legally liable; the Ninth Circuit ruling turned on the FTCA exemption and jurisdictional posture.
- Avoid implying that later pathogenicity proves historical negligence or that official belief in safety proves no risk.
- The subject may be misrepresented as evidence for present-day biological-conspiracy claims. Keep the source ledger visible and reject unsupported ecological or population-health claims.
- Do not include actionable aerosolisation, cultivation, dosage or release instructions. Archive only high-level historical facts.
- Rights, provenance and sensitivity review would be separate future gates for any archival image or footage; none is created here.
