## Legal and ethical sensitivity

### Sovereignty and colonial framing

The dispute is often narrated as a quirky quarrel between two nation-states. That frame can erase the Coast Salish peoples whose homeland, labour, resources, and political rights predated both claims. The episode should state clearly that the 1846 treaty and 1872 arbitration were Anglo-American instruments; neither is evidence that Indigenous title was morally or universally extinguished.

### Racialised and colonial language

The documents use nineteenth-century terms for Indigenous people and settlers. Reproduce such language only when the exact wording matters, with attribution and context. Do not repeat the period claim that HBC or Indigenous people were responsible for “raids” without documentary proof; the NPS administrative history identifies that belief as unsupported or contested.

### Individuals and reputational risk

The principal actors are historical figures, so ordinary living-person defamation risk is low. The higher risk is misattributing threats, bad faith, or deliberate provocation to Cutlar, Griffin, Dallas, Hubbs, Douglas, or Harney when the accounts conflict. Use “claimed,” “reported,” “the record says,” and “the evidence does not establish” where appropriate.

### Casualty and harm language

“The only casualty was a pig” is a memorable institutional shorthand for the absence of a U.S.–British battle death. It should not be used to imply no human harm, displacement, coercive jurisdiction, Indigenous dispossession, or economic loss occurred. The episode needs a human review of this wording before publication.

### Visual/legal review flags

No creative or production materials are being drafted in this research pack. If later visual work uses maps, historical photographs, treaty scans, or military images, verify rights and captions independently. Do not use a modern map to imply the pre-1872 boundary was settled.

## Open questions

1. What do the original 15 June 1859 HBC Bellevue Journal entry and Griffin-to-Douglas letter say in full, and how do they compare with Cutlar's affidavit?
2. What exactly did Harney's 11 July petition, 16 July orders, and later letters instruct Pickett to do? Which parts were rescinded or disapproved?
3. What do British Admiralty and Foreign Office records say about Hornby, Baynes, Douglas, and the proposed landing of Marines?
4. What were the exact terms and legal status of the Scott–Douglas joint-occupation agreement?
5. What reasoning, maps, and translations appear in the 1872 German arbitration record, beyond the U.S. president's summary?
6. How did the occupation affect Coast Salish communities, HBC workers including Hawaiian shepherds, Black Pioneers, women, and non-military residents?
7. Which force totals and “only casualty” formulations survive direct muster, medical, and post records rather than later park interpretation?
