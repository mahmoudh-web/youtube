## Central conflict

The popular version is a clean ecological morality tale: malaria spraying killed insects, poisoned the food chain, killed cats, caused a rat invasion, and forced WHO and the RAF to parachute replacement cats into Borneo. The located evidence supports a narrower account: a real malaria programme existed; rats and cats were linked in a 1959 North Borneo report; a small RAF drop to Bario is documented through a reproduced flight record; but the causal chain between those facts is not demonstrated.

## Evidence supporting a real Bario cat delivery

- The reproduced No. 48 Squadron Operations Record Book says a Beverley flew from Changi to Bario on 13 March 1960 and carried out a “unique drop” (S5). It lists 7,000 pounds of stores, including over 20 cats, and records a message saying the cats were safe.
- The contemporary newspaper trail is independent of the later Internet legend: the *Straits Times* sought 30 “flying cats” on 9 March, the *Singapore Free Press* published “It Rained Cats in Sarawak” on 17 March, the *Straits Times* followed with “Those Cats Go in Bang on Target” on 22 March, and a further *Straits Times* report appeared on 18 April (S7–S10).
- The 2005 Association of Former WHO Staff newsletter says the WHO Library received the RAF record page after staff had questioned whether the story was real (S11). That supports the existence of a source trail, although the newsletter is not the operational record itself.
- A later National Library Board Singapore retrospective reports 23 cats, a Bario drop and the follow-up message about the cats’ safety while linking readers to the newspaper archive (S6). It is useful corroboration and source discovery, not an original record.

The safest interim statement is therefore: **a March 1960 RAF supply drop to Bario that included more than 20 cats is strongly supported by a reproduced RAF record and contemporary newspaper references; the original records still require inspection.**

## DDT and cat deaths: support and limits

### What supports a connection

- The 1959 North Borneo annual report explicitly says field rats were a greater menace than usual partly because antimalarial spraying accidentally killed many cats (S3). This is a contemporaneous official statement, but it is about North Borneo/Sabah.
- O’Shaughnessy’s review records cat deaths after indoor residual spraying in several locations and explains that direct contact with DDT residue and grooming was one proposed pathway (S4). It also records WHO representatives’ conflicting public statements about domestic-animal deaths.
- Harrisson’s later account says cats died after eating contaminated cockroaches and links the rat increase to the loss of cats in the Kelabit Highlands (S4, pp. 4–5). This is an important named account, not a verified toxicology record.

### What weakens the neat causal claim

- The 1956 WHO Sarawak report focuses on malaria vectors, parasite rates and the operational difficulty of spraying the Baram/Tinjar valleys; it does not report a cat die-off, rat crisis or cat delivery (S1).
- The North Borneo report cannot be transplanted into Sarawak. A Sabah observation is relevant comparative evidence, not proof that Bario’s cats died for the same reason.
- O’Shaughnessy concludes that the alleged DDT biomagnification chain in the cat story was never verified (S4). The review traces that idea through Harrisson, Conway and later popular versions rather than to a Sarawak toxicology investigation.
- The story’s chemical labels vary: some versions say DDT, others dieldrin or another insecticide. S4 records that dieldrin was used in Sarawak but soon discontinued, and that North Borneo’s cat story may have involved dieldrin. The chemical must remain source-specific.
- The idea that cats ate poisoned lizards/geckos or cockroaches is a proposed pathway, not a measured exposure chain. Do not present it as a food-web finding.

## Source-provenance conflict

The strongest evidence for the drop is not a single public RAF release but a chain: an RAF ORB page reproduced/cited by a peer-reviewed article, a 2005 WHO-staff newsletter reporting that page, and contemporary NLB newspaper records. This is enough to treat a small Bario drop as highly plausible, but not enough to claim that every vivid detail in later accounts is true. The original ORB page and newspaper scans are the next verification gate.

## What would change the working answer

The conclusion should be revised if the original ORB does not contain the quoted Bario entry; if the newspaper scans identify a different location or date; if Sarawak malaria files show that the cats, rat infestation and spraying were directly linked; if a contemporaneous toxicology or veterinary record identifies the insecticide and exposure route; or if Harrisson’s original text explains his 1965 date as a separate event. A primary record showing WHO authorization would also change the agency-attribution wording.
