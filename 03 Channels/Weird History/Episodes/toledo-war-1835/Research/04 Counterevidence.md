## Counterevidence and competing readings

### 1. “War” versus boundary crisis

Michigan and Ohio institutional pages use “Toledo War,” “Border War” or “war,” and the sources document militia mobilisation, arrests and at least one armed confrontation. That supports using the familiar label as a historical-memory object. It does not establish a formal interstate war or sustained battlefield campaign. A Library of Congress reproduction describes the episode as an “Ohio and Michigan war” but says no blood was shed, while Ohio and Michigan institutional pages identify Wood’s stabbing as the only bloodshed or injury. The safe description is “a boundary dispute that escalated into armed mobilisation and coercive enforcement, later remembered as the Toledo War.”

### 2. Casualty and death claims

- Ohio Memory, Ohio History Connection, Michiganology and the 1869 W. V. Way volume converge on Joseph Wood being stabbed during an attempted arrest of Two Stickney.
- Ohio History Connection calls it a minor flesh wound; Michiganology says Wood recovered; Way says he was reported in a dying condition but was actually very little hurt.
- A Library of Congress text says “No blood was shed,” which conflicts with the institutional Wood account and may reflect a different definition or a later compressed narrative.
- No source indexed in this pass establishes a death. That is a research finding, not proof that no death occurred.

**Human verification:** locate Lyman Hurd’s affidavit, the Monroe County indictment, any court disposition and a contemporary medical/coroner record. Record the exact date, weapon description, wound location, survival and legal venue. Until then, use “reported nonfatal stabbing” or “best-supported injury lead,” not “the only casualty” as an unqualified fact.

### 3. Phillips Corners and “shots fired”

Later accounts describe a confrontation around Ohio surveyors re-marking the Harris line. Some summaries use “battle,” “shots exchanged” or “fired over their heads,” while others focus on capture and withdrawal. The event should not be staged as a battle with confirmed combat losses until the contemporary Ohio and Michigan reports are compared. A safe provisional line is “a reported armed encounter around the survey, with no confirmed injury in the sources currently indexed.”

### 4. Force-size inflation

The Michigan DMVA page gives a 250-person volunteer group; other summaries refer to hundreds or roughly 1,000 Michigan militiamen, while Ohio accounts describe different numbers at different moments. These may refer to separate deployments, volunteers, militia available on paper or forces actually present. No single total should be used. The ledger therefore uses “militia mobilisation” and sends muster rolls/censuses to retrieval.

### 5. “Michigan got the Upper Peninsula for Toledo”

This is a useful narrative shorthand but not a complete legal description. The 15 June 1836 Act sets Ohio’s northern boundary and describes Michigan’s boundary through Lake Superior, Montreal River, the Menominee and Lake Michigan; it makes admission conditional on a convention’s assent. The 26 January 1837 Act admits Michigan after the December assent. Neither statute calls the Upper Peninsula “compensation,” promises money, or says Ohio paid Michigan.

Modern institutional sources describe the result as compensation, an exchange, more than 9,000 square miles, four-fifths or the western three-quarters. Michigan’s earlier territorial/statehood boundaries already included an eastern portion of the present Upper Peninsula. The safe wording is “the federal settlement transferred most of the present Upper Peninsula into Michigan’s state boundaries while resolving the Toledo claim and statehood condition; later histories call that compensation.”

### 6. “Winner” claims

Ohio retained the disputed strip and Michigan accepted the federal boundary to secure statehood. Michigan gained a much larger northern land area, whose timber and mineral value became clearer later. This makes “Ohio won” and “Michigan won” both incomplete depending on the measure. Keep the outcomes separate: legal/de facto control of Toledo, federal admission, land area and later economic hindsight.

### 7. Jackson and partisan explanations

Michigan’s official history records Jackson’s intervention and Mason’s removal. Some later accounts attribute the outcome to Ohio’s congressional power or electoral politics. This pack does not yet establish a single motive for Jackson, Congress or either governor. Do not state that Jackson “favoured Ohio for votes” without presidential correspondence, congressional debate and a source that makes the claim explicitly.

### 8. The land was not an empty prize

The present pack is focused on the boundary crisis and does not yet provide sufficient treaty, Indigenous-government or local-community evidence for the legal and human history of the land. The episode must not treat the Toledo Strip or Upper Peninsula as unoccupied scenery. Add primary treaties, Indigenous-history scholarship and local records before narration.

## Source-bias controls

- Michigan government and Michiganology sources foreground statehood and the Upper Peninsula’s later value.
- Ohio History Connection and Ohio Memory sources foreground Ohio’s claim, Wood’s stabbing and the state’s eventual retention of Toledo.
- The 1869 Way volume is close enough to preserve documentary leads but is a retrospective local history with selection and partisan-memory risks.
- Maps show competing lines and geography; they do not by themselves decide the legal question.
- Federal statutes control what Congress enacted, not whether the settlement was morally, politically or economically fair.
