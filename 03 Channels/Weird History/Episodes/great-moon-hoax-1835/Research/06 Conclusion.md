## Provisional assessment

The evidence supports a six-part serial published by New York’s *Sun* from 25 August through 31 August 1835, excluding Sunday, presenting invented lunar discoveries as a report connected to the real astronomer John Herschel’s Cape of Good Hope work. The text described lunar geography, vegetation, animals, architecture and winged humanoids called *Vespertilio-homo*. The surviving 1859 compilation preserves the serial; Library of Congress and university-library records preserve the illustration and reception context.

The safest current promise is: **a penny newspaper borrowed a real astronomer, a real telescope expedition and the voice of science to sell an impossible Moon—then left historians to untangle what readers believed, how the story travelled and how evasively the paper backed away.** The episode’s subject is not whether bat-men existed. It is how a fabricated report acquired authority and why its afterlife is harder to measure than the legend suggests.

## What the pack can currently establish

- *The Sun* published six installments beginning 25 August 1835 and concluding 31 August, under a headline attributing the material to Sir John Herschel at the Cape of Good Hope.
- The serial claimed to be from a supplement to the *Edinburgh Journal of Science* and used the supposed Dr Andrew Grant as a reporting persona.
- The articles described fictional lunar landscapes, plants, animals, temples and winged humanoids; these are contents of a fabrication, not historical observations.
- John Herschel really conducted a major astronomical survey near Cape Town from 1834 to 1838, leaving archive and published-observation records that can be used as a factual control.
- Richard Adams Locke is the strongest historical attribution for the serial, and later accounts preserve his explanation that he intended satire; authorship and intent should remain attributed until the earliest records are checked.
- The story was reprinted, illustrated, debated and translated, demonstrating substantial reach; the distribution of belief among readers is not measurable from the current source set.
- The *Sun* addressed the authenticity controversy on 16 September 1835, but the available later quotation presents an equivocal response rather than a simple, immediate retraction.
- The circulation figure, Yale delegation, Springfield missionary story and “fooled the world” framing remain reported or popularised claims requiring primary verification.

## Editorial interpretation, clearly labelled

The Great Moon Hoax worked by making fiction look like a report: it attached extraordinary claims to a living scientific reputation, a real expedition and technical details that many readers could not quickly test. Its historical interest lies in the gap between reach and belief. Reprints, illustrations and arguments show that the story moved, but they do not yield a clean percentage of believers or justify “the whole world.” The *Sun*’s later evasiveness is part of the story: exposure did not arrive as one universally accepted reveal, and the paper’s own account should be treated as evidence with an incentive to minimise fault.

This interpretation is drawn from the serial text, LOC and Clements Library context, Herschel archive/observatory records and O’Brien’s later history of the paper. It is an analysis of the evidence, not a quotation from one 1835 participant.
