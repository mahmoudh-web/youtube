## Competing accounts of the initial shooting

### Bulgarian version

The British dispatch’s published extract says the Bulgarian account was that a Greek sentry from post 69 crossed the frontier, shot at a Bulgarian sentry at post 1, was shot and then a fusillade followed when comrades tried to recover the body (S6, pp. 55–62). A later Bulgarian archival narrative similarly says the Greek sentry entered Bulgarian territory and fired first (S8). These are coherent claims, but they are claims transmitted by interested or source-dependent parties.

### Greek version

The Greek verbal-note account reproduced by the Bulgarian Telegraph Agency says Bulgarian troops attacked Greek post 69, killed the Greek sentry, and later shot Captain Vassiliadis while he approached with a white flag (S7). Contemporary newspapers also carried the Greek version of two Greek deaths and a Bulgarian white flag/misunderstanding explanation (S12). These reports establish the existence of a Greek case, not its truth.

### What the Commission may change

The Commission was created to investigate the origin of the incident and the facts surrounding it. The official report and Annex 815 must be read before claiming that it determined who fired first, whether the white flag was seen, or how the dog story entered the record.

## Dates and endpoints are not interchangeable

- The League report catalog describes incidents from 19–22 October.
- The official League case summary anchors Bulgaria’s appeal to 22 October and calls the matter the Demir-Kapu incident.
- Some later accounts use 18 October for the first crossing or dog story.
- Greek withdrawal was ordered and observed on 28–29 October; that is not the date of the Commission’s responsibility finding.

The safe timeline is therefore: incident beginning on/around 19 October; appeal on 22 October; Briand intervention on 23 October; Council withdrawal mechanism on 26 October; Commission on 29 October; report dated 28 November; Council conclusions adopted 14 December.

## “Petrich was captured” versus “the League stopped the offensive”

Contemporary press reports used dramatic language about Petrich being captured, while the League’s official retrospective account says an intended Greek offensive was countermanded before the 24 October attack and that withdrawal was then observed. The primary military reports must settle which frontier posts, villages or town areas were occupied and when. Until then, use “Greek forces occupied Bulgarian frontier districts” only with the cited source and avoid a definitive town-capture sequence.

## “£45,000” versus “30 million leva”

Later English-language retellings commonly state a £45,000 payment. The Bulgarian Telegraph Agency’s reproduction of a Commission summary gives 30 million leva, split into 10 million for government/material and moral losses and 20 million for private losses (S7). Time’s 14 December 1925 report also gives 20 million plus 10 million leva (S7/S9 leads). The relationship between the currencies, the exact adopted amount and the payment record must be checked in Official Journal pp. 172–177 and follow-up documents. Do not silently convert or merge the figures.

## Casualty and “war” inflation

The located sources use different descriptions of casualties and scope. A border shooting, a prolonged fusillade, local civilian losses and military occupation are not the same as a formal interstate war. Do not use a single casualty number or “shortest war” label without a source-specific locator and endpoint definition.

## Source-bias map

- **League records:** strongest for procedure, decisions and the language adopted by the Council; not automatically neutral about every factual allegation.
- **Greek notes and press:** preserve the Greek account of a Bulgarian attack and Captain Vassiliadis’ death; may frame Greek action as defensive or compensatory.
- **Bulgarian notes and later official narratives:** preserve the Greek-crossed-border account and foreground Bulgarian territorial violation; may minimise Bulgarian responsibility for the first shots.
- **British dispatches:** useful external diplomatic observation, but dependent on Bulgarian officials, correspondents and limited field access.
- **Contemporary newspapers:** show circulation of competing stories and dramatic claims, not a verified incident log.
- **Later legal scholarship:** identifies exact Official Journal pages and preserves the Council conclusion; it remains secondary until the primary scan is read.

## Required counterevidence search

1. Compare the Commission report’s factual findings with the 27 October Council statements by the Greek and Bulgarian representatives.
2. Locate British, French and Italian military-attaché reports on the withdrawal and any field description of Demir-Kapu/Petrich.
3. Locate Greek and Bulgarian military casualty returns, Captain Vassiliadis’ record and any civilian damage lists.
4. Trace the earliest printed appearance of the dog story: contemporary newspaper, memoir, later history or oral tradition.
5. Check the original 14 December decision and payment/implementation records before using “£45,000,” “30 million leva,” “paid” or “settlement.”
