## Reading guide

Dates and actions are separated from interpretation. “Contemporary record” means the event or official position appears in a document produced during the dispute; “later account” means a retrospective source such as the 1924 Iowa article or the 1876 autobiography. Exact dates for the bee-tree cutting and some Gregory encounters remain unresolved.

## Chronology

| Date | Event / record | Classification | Evidence and caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1816 | John C. Sullivan surveys the Osage cession boundary: north from the Missouri/Kansas confluence, then east toward the Des Moines River. The marked line later becomes the Sullivan or Old Indian Boundary Line. | Established fact | S1 pp. 2–3; S2 pp. 2–4; S7 pp. 339–340. The survey’s irregularity and the reason for the deviation need the field notes, not just later summaries. |
| 1820 | Congress’s Missouri admission act describes the northern boundary by a parallel through the “rapids of the river Des Moines” and reference to the Indian boundary line. | Established legal text | National Archives’ [Missouri Compromise transcription](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/missouri-compromise); S13. The phrase is the legal ambiguity later litigated. |
| 1821 | Missouri enters the Union. The Sullivan line is treated by federal land administration and Missouri as its practical northern boundary for years. | Established broad fact | S7 pp. 340–341; S10 U.S. Reports pp. 673–674. “Accepted” should be unpacked by institution: state, federal survey and land-sale practice were not necessarily identical. |
| 1824 | Treaty creating the Half-Breed Tract refers to a continuation of Missouri’s northern boundary toward the Mississippi. | Reported historical fact | S7 p. 340. Retrieve the treaty text and Indigenous-party context before using this as proof of state title. |
| 1834–1838 | The land north of Missouri passes through federal territorial administrations; Iowa Territory is organised on 4 July 1838. | Established broad fact | [State Historical Society of Iowa, Iowa Territory unit](https://history.iowa.gov/learn/k-12-education/professional-learning/iowa-history-course/unit-2-iowa-territory), paras. 53–54; S10. The territorial boundary inherited the unresolved question. |
| 1837 | Missouri commissions J. C. Brown to resurvey the northern boundary, locating “rapids” in the Des Moines River rather than the Mississippi rapids associated with the older reading. The resulting Brown line sits roughly 9 miles north of Sullivan’s line at the east end and 13 miles north at the west end, creating an area estimated at more than 2,600 square miles. | Established survey history with approximate figures | S6; S7 pp. 340–341; S2 pp. 4–9. The exact line and area must be checked against Brown’s field notes and map. |
| Late 1838 | Missouri legislature passes an act defining or recognising the Brown line as the state’s northern boundary. | Established legal action | S1, printed document A (15 Dec 1838 Clark County act) and document B (the 1839 boundary act); S7 pp. 341–342. The federal message contains a printer’s/date discrepancy: its note gives 11 Feb 1839, while Boggs’s proclamation refers to 16 Feb. Do not flatten this without checking the Missouri Laws volume. |
| 18 Jun 1838 / Jan 1839 | Congress authorises a federal survey of Iowa’s southern boundary. Commissioner Albert M. Lea reports in January 1839 that four lines could be argued from the legal and survey evidence; Missouri does not appoint or maintain a cooperating commissioner. | Established administrative record | S2 pp. 1–9; S1 Lucas reply. “Four lines” is Lea’s technical framing, not proof that each had equal legal status. |
| 8 Jul 1839 | Van Buren County commissioners write to Governor Robert Lucas alleging Missouri officials have assessed property and probed residents’ views on slavery, and ask for executive guidance. | Contemporary allegation / official complaint | S1 printed p. 3, document C. This is an Iowa-side report; it establishes what the commissioners alleged, not that every allegation was independently proven. |
| 29 Jul 1839 | Lucas issues a proclamation ordering Iowa officials to resist foreign jurisdiction and enforce Iowa/U.S. authority; it invokes Iowa’s organic act and a law against exercising foreign official functions. | Established contemporary official action | S1 printed pp. 4–5, document D. It establishes the threat of criminal process and fines/imprisonment under the cited law, not a completed military action. |
| 23 Aug 1839 | Missouri Governor Lilburn W. Boggs issues a counter-proclamation claiming the Brown line and ordering Missouri civil/military officers to enforce Missouri law, call county power and, if needed, militia. | Established contemporary official action / partisan legal claim | S1 printed pp. 6–8, document E. Boggs’s statement is evidence of Missouri’s position and instructions, not a neutral boundary determination. |
| 25 Sep 1839 | Lucas replies that Iowa accepts Congress’s eventual decision but will continue jurisdiction to the Sullivan line; he directs U.S. and Iowa civil officers to prosecute encroachments and urges calm. | Established contemporary official action / partisan legal claim | S1 printed pp. 9–17, document F. His assertion that Sullivan’s line had been continuously recognised is a claim supported by some federal land records but should be checked against the complete record. |
| Mid-Oct 1839 | Uriah S. Gregory, sheriff of Clark County, Missouri, enters the disputed area to collect taxes and fails/withdraws after resistance. | Established in later account; likely contemporary event | S7 pp. 342–343; S1’s later-document context; S15. Exact date and whether this was the first attempt require the sheriff’s correspondence or court record. |
| 5 Nov 1839 | Lucas’s second annual message says the boundary dispute might ultimately lead to an “effusion of blood.” | Contemporary official warning | S7 p. 344. This supports threatened violence, not actual injury. |
| Nov 1839, exact day unresolved | Later accounts say one or more bee trees in the disputed area were cut by a Missourian, with honey taken or damage done; the culprit escaped south. Counts vary between three and four trees. | Later-attested story; unresolved primary record | S4–S8. S7 reports a Palmyra Whig poem published 26 Dec 1839; inspect the newspaper and court file. The 1839 federal message S1 does not mention this incident. |
| 20 Nov 1839 | Gregory returns to collect taxes and is arrested by Henry Heffleman, Van Buren County sheriff, for acting under disputed authority. | Established in later historical synthesis; date specifically reported | S7 p. 345; S8; S15 gives a different sequence/date framing. Retrieve the arrest record, charge and release conditions. |
| 23 Nov 1839 | Clark County court meets and orders Generals David Willock and O. H. Allen to muster Missouri militia to support civil authority. | Contemporary action as reported by later source | S7 p. 345. The court order itself is not yet in hand; find it in Missouri county/state records. |
| 4 Dec 1839 | Clark County court appoints a committee to confer with Iowa legislators and seek a friendly settlement or joint interim control. | Later account of a contemporary local action | S7 p. 348. Confirm the court minutes and the precise proposed division of civil/criminal jurisdiction. |
| 6 Dec 1839 | Lucas orders Iowa militia commanders to muster if necessary to support territorial civil authority; U.S. Deputy Marshal G. A. Hendry prepares a posse. | Contemporary action as reported by later source | S7 p. 346; compare S1’s insistence on civil process. “Iowa militia” and “posse comitatus” should not be treated as identical institutions. |
| 7 Dec 1839 | The Iowa Palimpsest account places the Lewis County Missouri regiment on the march toward the disputed area, poorly supplied, and says more than 2,000 Missouri men began gathering. | Later account of mobilisation | S7 p. 345. This is a mobilisation estimate, not a verified field strength. Other sources give about 800 Missouri troops or 600–2,500. |
| Dec 1839 | Iowa force is described by S7 as roughly 1,200 nominal men, with fewer than 1,100 non-commissioned men and about 500 reaching the Farmington camp; later recollections give about 600 Iowa troops. | Conflicting mobilisation estimates | S7 pp. 348–349; S15. Preserve both as source-attributed figures until muster rolls are found. |
| 9–14 Dec 1839 | Peace committees press for suspension of hostile actions pending federal settlement. On 12 Dec the Clark County court orders Missouri militia to disband; on 14 Dec the Iowa Territorial Assembly supports a federal solution. | Established broad endpoint, dates from later synthesis | S7 pp. 348–349; S15. Confirm the legislative resolution and court order. The demobilisation ends the crisis, not the underlying boundary case. |
| 24 Dec 1839 | President Martin Van Buren transmits Lucas’s documents to the U.S. Senate, citing intense excitement over the competing jurisdictions and the need for congressional attention. | Contemporary federal record | S1 printed p. 1. This is the clearest contemporary federal acknowledgement of the crisis’s seriousness. |
| 6 Apr 1849 | In *Missouri v. Iowa*, the Supreme Court holds the practical Sullivan line to be the boundary. | Established legal resolution | S10, U.S. Reports 48 U.S. 660. Read the complete opinion before summarising the holding; “Iowa won” is an editorial shorthand, not the court’s whole reasoning. |
| 1850–3 Jan 1851 | Court-appointed commissioners resurvey and remark the line; the 1851 report records Sullivan witness trees and iron monuments. | Established physical-demarcation process | S11, paras. 26–80. S7 p. 350 describes the Court’s acceptance in 1851. Distinguish the 1849 legal decision from the 1851 completion/acceptance of marking. |

## Timeline conclusions

1. The contemporary record makes the boundary and jurisdiction crisis real: official acts, proclamations, arrest threats and a federal message survive.
2. The dramatic bee-tree anecdote is not in the central 1839 federal message located here. Its existence is supported by later institutional and historical accounts, plus a reported contemporary newspaper poem, but the original newspaper/court record remains to be inspected.
3. The crisis escalated through civil process and militia orders but ended through local negotiation and federal referral before a verified battle. That supports “border mobilisation” or “armed stand-off” more safely than “war.”
