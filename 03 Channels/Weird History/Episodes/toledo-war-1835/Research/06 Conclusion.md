## Provisional finding

The strongest evidence supports an episode about how a technical boundary dispute became a statehood crisis. Ohio and Michigan Territory inherited competing legal readings of Great Lakes geography and boundary language. When each side backed its claim with local government, survey crews, arrests, legal penalties and militia, the confrontation acquired the retrospective name “Toledo War.” The sources currently support armed mobilisation and coercive enforcement, not a conventional war with a settled battle narrative.

The casualty story should remain narrow: the best-supported lead is a nonfatal stabbing of Michigan Deputy Sheriff Joseph Wood by Ohio partisan Two Stickney during an attempted arrest. Multiple institutional sources call this the only injury or bloodshed, while another located text says no blood was shed and the primary affidavit/court/medical record has not yet been checked. That is enough for a research lead, not an unqualified “sole casualty” claim.

The ending is also more complicated than a simple land trade. Congress’s 15 June 1836 act established Ohio’s northern boundary, defined Michigan’s boundary and conditioned admission on assent. A second convention assented in December, and Congress admitted Michigan on 26 January 1837. Later histories describe the inclusion of most of the present Upper Peninsula as compensation or an exchange for relinquishing Toledo; the statutes do not use that word or establish a cash payment, and Michigan’s earlier proposal already included part of the eastern peninsula.

## Safe episode promise

How did a line on a map produce arrests, rival militias and a statehood crisis—and why is “Michigan traded Toledo for the Upper Peninsula” a useful but incomplete summary?

## Strongest narrative spine (research only)

1. The map problem: two boundary readings converged on the mouth of the Maumee and a growing Toledo.
2. The enforcement problem: both governments tried to make their jurisdiction real through laws, surveys, arrests and armed presence.
3. The casualty problem: Wood’s stabbing is vivid but the safe record is narrower than popular retellings.
4. The federal problem: Congress used Michigan’s admission and revised borders to settle the dispute.
5. The memory problem: Ohio retained Toledo, Michigan gained statehood and most of the present Upper Peninsula, and later “winner” stories depend on what outcome is measured.

This is an editorial scaffold, not narration. No sentence should move to script without a claim-ledger row.
