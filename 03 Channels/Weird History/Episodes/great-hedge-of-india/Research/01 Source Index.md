## Research question

What did the Inland Customs Line actually look like over time, how continuous was its living hedge, and what can the surviving records safely establish about the tax burden and social effects on people who produced, carried or consumed salt?

## Conflict and episode promise

The memorable claim is that Britain built a 2,500-mile thorn hedge across India. The source-critical question is narrower and stranger: a customs boundary of changing length used several kinds of barriers, while a live hedge occupied only some sections and existed alongside dry brush, stone, ditch-and-bank works, rivers, roads and guard posts. The fiscal system made an everyday necessity taxable, but the evidence for household impact is uneven and often written by officials defending or administering the tax.

## Source-quality key

- **A — primary/official:** contemporary government report, statute, parliamentary debate, archival manuscript or contemporaneous administrative record.
- **B — institutional/archival synthesis:** archive catalogue or official historical synthesis that identifies and contextualizes primary material.
- **C — scholarly/curatorial interpretation:** academic or curatorial work used to frame significance, not to replace the underlying record.
- **D — later popular synthesis:** useful for reception history or leads only; not sufficient alone for material claims.

## What the source set currently supports

- A long-lived Inland Customs Line existed, but its measured length depends on which component and year is meant.
- The line was a hybrid barrier. Primary evidence supports live plants in some sections, dry hedge backing, stone walls, scarped earth, ditches, rivers and administrative guard posts in others.
- The 2,500-mile figure is defensible as a later official description of the wider customs line at a particular stage; it is not defensible as the length of one continuous living hedge.
- The salt duty produced major revenue and was defended as a substitute for many transit duties, but contemporaries also described it as a food tax burdening poor consumers and reported enforcement against illicit salt.
- The available record is much better at measuring administrative cost, revenue and infrastructure than at reconstructing individual household budgets, women’s work, local growers’ experiences, or the health effect of reduced consumption.
