## Provisional Answer

The best-supported answer is not that inflatable tanks won a war by themselves. The 23rd Headquarters Special Troops produced identifiable effects when visual, sonic, radio, and human-signature deception supported a real Allied operation, but the effects varied by objective and were never uniformly measured.

| Operation | Provisional finding | Evidence boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Task Force Mason | Localized success: the Army account says fake artillery was shelled while the real 980th moved relatively unscathed. | Verify the operation file; current evidence is an official retrospective summary. |
| Brittany | Marginal or mixed. | Toomey says German forces did not withdraw from Mortain; do not call it a clear success. |
| Brest | Tactical partial success, strategic failure. | Apparent German fire/force shifts support the ruse, but the surrender objective failed and friendly tanks were lost after miscommunication. |
| Bettembourg | Credible operational effect. | Official accounts report a fictitious 6th Armored presence, German force reaction, and an eight-day hold; verify the cited AARs. |
| Kodak | Unresolved. | Radio deception was used, but the official history says the pace of the Bulge made the result difficult to appraise. |
| Viersen | Strongest case for enemy mislocation and altered expectations. | Captured overlay and reported POW evidence support the claim, while other German records and battlefield cues rule out “completely fooled” or “sole cause.” |

## Interpretation

The evidence supports a narrower and more durable story: deception could make a small force look like a larger formation, redirect fire, preserve a thinly held position, or move an enemy’s expected attack axis. It worked as an operational instrument, not as magic. German intelligence was uneven, echelon-specific, and responsive to multiple signals. The strongest claim the pack can currently support is that some Ghost Army operations materially assisted Allied objectives; it cannot support a universal success rate, a “fooled Hitler” line, or an exact lives-saved total.

## Lives-Saved Claim

Later accounts cite totals from “thousands” to 10,000–40,000 or about 25,000. These figures are inconsistent and no source located here supplies a transparent counterfactual method. The safe research conclusion is: operational reports describe reduced exposure, tactical protection, or surprise, and commanders believed the work saved lives; the exact number is not established.
