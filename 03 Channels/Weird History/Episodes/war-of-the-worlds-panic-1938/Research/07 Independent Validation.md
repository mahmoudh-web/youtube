## Executive verdict

**PASS WITH CORRECTIONS; HUMAN VERIFICATION REQUIRED.** The pack’s central conclusion is supportable as a provisional research finding: genuine fear and action are documented, while the size of a nationwide “mass panic” is not established by one compatible measurement. The pack also correctly warns against turning headlines, self-selected correspondence or Cantril’s estimates into a national census.

It is not ready for narration or production. The decisive unresolved items are the original FCC scans and attachments, the full Cantril methods and calculations, the original follow-up survey instruments, the complete newspaper articles, and the three University of Michigan item records. The accessible evidence also reveals a possible **1,349 versus 1,344** Michigan-corpus count discrepancy that must be reconciled before either number is stated as settled.

The validation verdict is not episode approval. The human editor remains the final verifier and publication gate.

## Chronology, names, numbers and wording controls

- **Date and programme:** 30 October 1938, CBS, Mercury Theatre on the Air, Orson Welles, Howard Koch and H. G. Wells are supported by S1 indexed metadata, S2 lines 20–21 and S16 lines 79–80.
- **Opening and closing:** S2 lines 20–21, 278 and 364–369 show repeated identification. The exact number of announcements and their timing still require the audio/script check.
- **Fictional geography:** Grovers Mill, Princeton, Trenton and New Jersey are programme content, not evidence that a real event occurred. S2 lines 78–85 and S16 lines 94–107 support the broadcast’s fictional setting and form.
- **Trenton:** “About 2,000 calls in two hours” is a contemporaneous report by Paul Morton as summarized by S4 lines 10–11, not a verified independent call log. The receipt date is 1 November 1938; the letter date is 31 October.
- **Audience numbers:** Keep Cantril’s “at least six million heard” and “at least one million frightened or disturbed” as attributed estimates. Do not merge them with the LOC 5,000-household/approximately 2% result or NARA’s 40–50% late-tuning result until the original surveys are retrieved and their universes reconciled.
- **Michigan corpus:** The pack’s 1,349 figure is supported by the 2024 project result, but the 2026 project account says 1,344. The collection may distinguish preserved letters from transcribed or catalogued items, but that explanation is not established here.
- **Letters:** The NARA page directly verifies Yaukey’s letter and quote at S3 lines 72–74; the U-M Sereda, Moore and George transcriptions remain blocked. Do not quote the latter three until scans and metadata are checked.
- **Newspaper wording:** S14 verifies the *New York Times* headline as metadata. It does not verify the headline’s population claim, article anecdotes or any reported injury/death.
- **Historical context:** S3 lines 64–65 supports the Munich/breaking-news context and plausible mechanisms, but the context is not proof of causation for every listener.
- **Welles motive:** Public statements are evidence of what Welles said, not of private intention. S16 lines 81, 91–93 and 109–113 should remain attributed.
- **Rights:** S1’s permission note concerns the radio play’s use in the IU item; S10 collection search results and collection metadata warn that some letters may be copyright-protected. Archival access is not production clearance.

## Source-quality and coverage assessment

### Strong or useful sources

- S1 is a high-value archival recording record, with unusually important provenance disclosure. The actual file was not listened to in this pass, so no timestamped quotation is independently cleared.
- S2 is useful for line-level programme content but is an editorial transcript. It should not replace the audio or Howard Koch manuscript for exact wording.
- S3–S4 are authoritative National Archives presentations and provide direct paths to the FCC correspondence. They are strong for what the archive says and for contemporary letter excerpts, but they remain summaries unless the underlying scans are read.
- S8 is the essential original study. JSTOR’s accessible excerpts verify its headline estimates and differentiated-reaction language, but the unavailable methods, tables and appendices are material limitations.
- S9 is peer-reviewed and directly accessible at the article/abstract level. Its page-specific historiographical claims require a full-PDF read before narration.
- S14 is a reliable archive record for the existence, date and headline of the *New York Times* page. It is not a substitute for the complete newspaper page/article.
- S16 is a useful institutional feature for context and for keeping public words separate from later opinions; it is not primary evidence of population scale.

### Coverage gaps and source weaknesses

1. The original Paul Morton and J. V. Yaukey FCC scans, attachments and any police/city records remain unchecked.
2. The IU audio and an archival Howard Koch script have not been compared, so exact timestamps, announcement count and quoted wording remain open.
3. Cantril’s sample recruitment, calculations, categories and appendices remain unavailable; “heard,” “frightened,” “disturbed,” “believed” and “acted” must not be treated as synonyms.
4. The CBS/AIPO late-tuning reports and the LOC-described 5,000-household survey have not been reconciled. The figures may describe different samples, dates or questions.
5. The complete 31 October *New York Times* article and at least two additional regional newspaper issues were not inspected. Headline framing cannot validate reported injury, death, flight or traffic anecdotes.
6. U-M item-level pages are blocked in this environment, preventing independent checking of Sereda, Moore and George’s names, locations, dates and quotations.
7. The U-M letter count is inconsistent across project records (1,349 in 2024; 1,344 in a 2026 project account). The denominator must be defined before use.
8. The source pack’s claim that a 2025 U-M announcement introduced the corpus was not independently located. Accessible records show a 2020 U-M report about the transcription project and a 2024 project-results announcement; the timeline date should be checked.
9. S17’s preservation entry was not independently retrieved, but it is non-load-bearing and should not be used to establish reaction scale.

## Unsupported, overstated or contradictory items

1. Do not state “America,” “the nation,” “millions panicked” or “mass hysteria” without attribution, source universe and category definition.
2. Do not translate Cantril’s “one million frightened or disturbed” into “one million panicked,” believers, callers, fugitives or injured people.
3. Do not present the 1,349 Michigan letters as a settled count until the 1,344/1,349 discrepancy is explained.
4. Do not quote the Sereda, Moore or George letters from the pack as independently verified evidence; their item pages were inaccessible.
5. Do not use the 2,000 Trenton calls as an independently audited total or extrapolate it nationally.
6. Do not report the *New York Times* headline or other newspaper anecdotes as verified population measurements.
7. Do not claim newspapers deliberately fabricated the reaction, coordinated an anti-radio campaign or caused the entire national legend without comparative press and motive evidence.
8. Do not claim the broadcast changed FCC rules. The accessible evidence supports complaints and public concern, not a specific regulatory outcome.
9. Do not claim suicides, injuries, hospitalizations, traffic jams or mass flight as verified outcomes without named primary or near-primary records.
10. Do not claim Welles intended the reaction, staged an apology for publicity, or privately anticipated its scale as fact.
11. Do not state that the broadcast was “clearly labelled, so frightened listeners were irrational.” The record supports formal disclosures plus a genuinely realistic form and uneven listener exposure.
12. Do not call the programme the “first fake-news story” or use another comparative superlative without a defined media-history comparison.

## Required corrections and additional primary-source checks

Before human approval of any later material, the human verifier should:

1. Listen to the complete IU recording and log stable timestamps for the opening identification, first bulletin, Grovers Mill reports, mid-programme identification, final out-of-character assurance and sign-off.
2. Retrieve an original or archival Howard Koch script and reconcile it with the specific audio, including the exact number and placement of disclaimers.
3. Read the Morton and Yaukey FCC scans, attachments and verso pages; record sender, recipient, dates, exact wording and whether the 2,000 calls were independently corroborated.
4. Inspect the complete 31 October *New York Times* page/article and at least two additional contemporary regional issues. Separate headline, reported anecdote, editorial comment and corroborated record.
5. Retrieve the original CBS/AIPO survey reports and the 5,000-household survey instrument or underlying publication. Reconcile sample frame, field date, question wording, “late” definition and denominators.
6. Read Cantril’s methods, tables and appendices in full. Record how the six-million and one-million estimates were derived and define every reaction category.
7. Reconcile the U-M corpus count (1,344 versus 1,349), collection scope, tags and transcribed/preserved distinction. Inspect the Sereda, Moore and George scans before quoting.
8. Read Pooley and Socolow’s full article before using claims about authorship, the “mass panic” memory or the magic-bullet theory.
9. Verify every injury, death, suicide, hospitalization, traffic-jam or flight anecdote against a named primary or near-primary record; otherwise remove it or label it only as an unverified contemporary report.
10. Complete rights checks for recording, script, newspaper scans, photographs and listener letters before any production use.
