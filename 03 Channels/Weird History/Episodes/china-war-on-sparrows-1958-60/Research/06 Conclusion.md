## Provisional assessment

The Four Pests campaign was a documented state-led mobilisation, not merely a later internet anecdote. In 1958, central instructions made sparrows an official target and People’s Daily reported mass campaigns in Beijing and elsewhere. The same record also shows why the headline numbers must be treated as claims: the reports were celebratory, incomplete and sometimes inconsistent.

The policy changed after a scientific and political reassessment. The Chinese Academy of Sciences account says the November 1959 report argued that sparrow harm and benefit varied by place and season, and that the aim should be to control damage rather than exterminate the species. The explicit instruction to stop killing sparrows and substitute bedbugs appears in March 1960, with formal policy wording following in April. This was a reversal, but not proof that the ecological consequences were nationally uniform or immediately repaired.

The famine connection must remain contested. Sparrows plausibly affected insect pressure and crop yields, and the 2026 Frank et al. working paper reports geographic yield and mortality associations consistent with a meaningful contribution. But peer-reviewed famine research assigns major explanatory weight to rigid procurement, central planning, resource diversion, food distribution, malnutrition, provincial implementation and weather. The defensible synthesis is: sparrow eradication may have worsened an already severe agricultural and institutional crisis; it did not, on the current evidence, single-handedly cause the Great Famine.

## What the pack can currently establish

- The February 1958 central directive named rats, flies, mosquitoes and sparrows as the Four Pests and called for mass mobilisation.
- Zheng Zuoxin’s 1956 People’s Daily article publicly supplied a crop-damage rationale and control methods, while later research and CAS records stressed seasonal and regional complexity.
- Beijing’s April 1958 campaign involved hundreds of thousands of reported sparrow deaths and millions of participants, according to conflicting People’s Daily reports.
- A Chongqing municipal party report provides a second local case study with its own large reported total and detailed methods.
- A 1959 official report claimed more than 2.1 billion sparrows killed in 1958, but this is an incomplete official statistic, not a verified census.
- CAS archival reporting places the reassessment in late 1959 and the explicit sparrow-to-bedbug policy reversal in March/April 1960.
- The Great Leap Forward famine was multi-causal in the scholarly literature; the share attributable to sparrow killing is unresolved.
- Frank et al.’s 2026 working paper is important new evidence, but its nearly two-million excess-death estimate is model-dependent and not a settled consensus.

## Safest episode promise

**China tried to make the sparrow a pest. The harder question is what the campaign changed—and what the evidence can actually connect to the famine.**

This promise supports a source-led episode about policy, science and uncertainty. A stronger promise such as “the sparrows caused the famine” is not supported by this pack.

## Editorial interpretation, clearly labelled

The episode’s strongest meaning is not that a single bird caused a national catastrophe. It is that a state campaign converted a partly understood ecological relationship into a national target, rewarded dramatic reports of success, and later had to revise the target when scientific evidence and practical consequences became harder to ignore. The famine context makes the story morally serious: ecological mistakes can compound institutional failures, but causal responsibility must be demonstrated rather than assumed.
