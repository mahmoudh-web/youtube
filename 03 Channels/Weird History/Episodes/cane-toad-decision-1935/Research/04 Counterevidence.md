## Counterevidence register

### “There was no evidence” versus overseas evidence plus a local applicability gap

The strongest criticism cannot accurately say that Queensland researchers had no evidence. Mungomery’s August 1935 article described Puerto Rican stomach-content work and overseas observations, and Bell had reported favourably after visiting Puerto Rico. That evidence made the proposal intelligible to contemporary agricultural scientists.

The stronger, supportable criticism is narrower: overseas evidence was not the same as a Queensland test. The period press material reproduced by the State Library said Queensland greyback beetles behaved differently from Puerto Rican beetles and that success depended on how long the beetles were accessible on the ground. The located record does not show a completed Australian field test of target-beetle control or a systematic environmental-risk assessment before the first release. The distinction matters because it separates a flawed inference from deliberate disregard of all evidence.

### “Warnings were ignored” versus a ban that was imposed and reversed

Walter Froggatt’s warning and the Commonwealth Health Department’s prohibition are not evidence that warnings had no institutional effect. They show that the risk was contested and that a federal intervention was attempted. The policy continued after Queensland ministers, BSES and cane-grower interests sought reversal, and later official history dates the lifting of the ban to September 1936.

The episode can therefore say that warnings were overridden or politically defeated only if the wording is attributed and the documentary chain is verified. “Officials ignored warnings” is too broad: some officials acted on them, while other institutional actors argued for release.

### “The toad was useless” versus limited target-pest effects

Queensland government material says that by 1941 the toad exerted only limited control over its intended prey. However, the 1941 *Queensland Agricultural Journal* search record reports that stomach contents contained a few greyback beetles and enough Frenchi beetles to encourage a belief in some useful control. This does not rescue the original policy, but it prevents the false claim that the toads never ate any target beetles.

Use: “The toad was a poor or limited control for the intended beetles,” not “it never touched them.”

### “No risk assessment” versus the period’s stated considerations

The Queenslander account said the proposal had been considered and that no evidence then showed the toad would be harmful. Mungomery’s article also discussed diet, habitat, overseas spread and beneficial insects. Those are genuine period considerations, even if they were incomplete and overconfident by later standards.

The evidence gap is not that no risk-related thought existed; it is that the located record does not show a systematic Australian ecological assessment or adequate demonstration that the toad would reach the specific beetle life stages in Queensland.

### “One person made the decision” versus a distributed institutional chain

Mungomery is the named operational importer and BSES is the documented institutional vehicle. Bell supplied earlier scientific impetus; the Queensland Agriculture Department and BSES later defended the program; Bulcock, Forgan Smith and Lyons are associated with the reversal of the federal ban in later accounts. The sources do not establish one sole authoriser, a cabinet vote, or a complete written chain.

Use “BSES-led release” and “the approval chain is incomplete,” unless primary administrative records establish more.

### “The toads destroyed all native wildlife” versus species-specific effects

Later science identifies lethal ingestion by susceptible predators as a major impact pathway but also finds strong variation among species, locations and time. Some native species are less affected or adapt; a species-specific crocodile mortality study is powerful evidence but not a universal ecological verdict.

Use current peer-reviewed sources for named impacts and retain their scope. Do not convert “key threatening process” into “every native animal was harmed.”

## Material items still needing verification

- The original Mungomery QAJ scan, including the full discussion of local beetle behaviour and the exact description of the proposed release.
- The original 1935 Courier-Mail, Queenslander and Rockhampton Morning Bulletin scans behind the SLQ reproductions.
- BSES quarantine/import paperwork, Meringa breeding logs, release records and the identity of the approving officer(s).
- The Commonwealth Health Department prohibition: date, statutory basis, wording, file number and whether it applied to import, release or distribution.
- Correspondence between Bulcock, Forgan Smith, Lyons, BSES and Director-General of Health J. H. L. Cumpston leading to the September 1936 reversal.
- The original Froggatt article and any response from BSES or Queensland Agriculture.
- The 1941 QAJ article and later departmental report to reconcile the extent of target-beetle control.
