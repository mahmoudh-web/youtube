## Chronology

| September–October 1938 | The Munich crisis and frequent European news interruptions form the contemporary radio context. | Cantril and the National Archives summary say some listeners were accustomed to urgent breaking-news interruptions and that this shaped interpretation. | Do not claim this context caused the reaction for all listeners; it is one proposed condition. |

| 1 Nov 1938 onward | FCC receives hundreds of letters, telegrams and petitions, both complaints and support. | S3 lines 57–62 and 69–75; S4 lines 18–24. | The exact file count, classification and date range require box-level review; the NARA summary says more than 600 contacts and roughly 40% supportive. |
| Early Nov 1938 | CBS and the American Institute of Public Opinion conduct follow-up surveys; NARA’s summary says 40–50% of listeners tuned in late. | S3 lines 66–68; S7 pp. 7–8 reports a 5,000-household telephone survey with about 2% tuned in and most aware it was fiction. | Survey wording, sampling frame, field dates and reconciliation between estimates must be retrieved before using the figures together. |
| Early Nov 1938 | Cantril’s Princeton team begins studying the reaction about one week after the broadcast. | S3 lines 63–65 says the team interviewed 135 people over about three weeks, including 100 known to be upset; S8 records the study’s published chapters and claims. | The recruitment process and category definitions determine what the 135 cases can represent; they cannot alone estimate the whole country. |
| 1940 | Cantril publishes *The Invasion from Mars: A Study in the Psychology of Panic*. | S8 pp. 47–64 gives the classic estimate of at least six million listeners and at least one million frightened/disturbed; later chapters distinguish reactions and “critical ability.” | Treat estimates as Cantril’s model and data interpretation, not settled fact. The 1940 publication date and collaborator credit also require bibliographic care. |
| 1950s–1970s | The event becomes a recurring example in communication research and popular accounts of powerful media effects. | S9 PDF pp. 1920–22, 1938–40 reconstructs how the study became a remembered “mass panic” classic and challenges the simple magic-bullet reading. | The history of textbook memory is not itself proof that the 1938 reaction was small or large. |
| 2013 | Pooley and Socolow publish an archival critique of the Cantril/Lazarsfeld story. | S9 abstract and PDF pp. 1938–40 argue that *The Invasion from Mars* was a collaborative Princeton project and more nuanced than the later “mass panic” shorthand. | Their institutional-history argument should not be overstated as a direct refutation of every contemporary report. |

| 2025 | University of Michigan announces the digitisation and transcription of more than 1,300 listener letters. | S10 and the U-M announcement make the corpus available for item-level reception research. | Collection coverage, tagging and self-selection still limit population inference. |

## Timeline reading rule

The sequence has three layers that must not be collapsed:

1. **The event:** a fictional broadcast with repeated but imperfectly heard disclosures.
2. **The immediate record:** local calls, letters, FCC complaints, supportive responses and newspaper reports.
3. **The retrospective story:** Cantril’s study, later communication theory, institutional summaries and the enduring “mass panic” legend.

The episode should move between these layers explicitly. A later summary can show how the story was remembered, but it cannot silently upgrade a headline or retrospective estimate into a direct measurement of the 1938 population.
