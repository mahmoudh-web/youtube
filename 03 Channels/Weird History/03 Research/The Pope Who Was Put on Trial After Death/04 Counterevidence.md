## Standard story being tested

The familiar version says that Formosus betrayed Lambert by inviting Arnulf, so Lambert and Ageltrude compelled their ally Stephen VI to exhume Formosus, seat him on the papal throne, have a deacon answer for him, mutilate him, throw him into the Tiber, and then reverse the result only after a popular revolt.

The first half of that sequence is a useful orientation, but it is not a single contemporary eyewitness account. The evidence is layered.

## Evidence that survives the standard story’s simplification

### 1. The core event is not merely a later legend

The *Annales Fuldenses* give a short, near-contemporary report: Stephen’s predecessor Formosus was removed from the grave, deposed through an advocate, and buried outside the ordinary papal burial place (S3, Anno 896, p. 129). The Ravenna dossier’s protocol/capitula also presupposes that a dead Formosus was removed and brought “as though to trial” (S9/S10, Ch. 5, around pp. 187–188).

This makes the central claim—posthumous proceedings and a deliberately altered burial status—strongly defensible even though the 897 acts are not securely preserved as an independent file.

### 2. The throne/deacon sequence has more than one medieval witness, but their details do not align perfectly

The *Annales Alamannici* and *Annales Laubacenses* add a seat/throne, a deacon answering, dragging through the basilica, and the Tiber; both add blood from the mouth (S4, pp. 286–287). Liudprand supplies the memorable direct question about Formosus’s move from Porto to Rome and the three-finger detail (S9/S10, Ch. 1, pp. 1–4; Ch. 5, around pp. 208–210).

That convergence supports a ritualized public humiliation, but not every detail at the same confidence level. The blood detail is a reported marvel. The sources vary between two and three fingers. Liudprand calls the presiding pope Sergius, an error of identification that warns against treating his narrative as a verbatim transcript.

## Motive counterevidence

### 3. The “Lambert’s revenge” explanation is traditional, not demonstrated

The *Annales Fuldenses* report that Formosus secretly invited Arnulf and promised him the imperial crown; later historians connected this to the anti-Formosan action. But the source does not directly say that Lambert or Ageltrude ordered the corpse trial. The old Catholic Encyclopedia formulation itself says “whether induced” or “perhaps ... compelled,” showing that the motive was already an inference in that summary (S14).

Monroe’s reassessment argues that the common revenge story overstates the evidence: Formosus also wrote to Fulk of Reims in support of Lambert, and the Ravenna material represents the Arnulf coronation as compelled by necessity or force (S10, Ch. 6, around pp. 212–215). These records do not prove Formosus innocent of every political maneuver, but they make a simple betrayal narrative unsafe.

### 4. Regino supports the coronation but does not repeat the invitation story

Regino records Arnulf’s capture of Rome, Formosus’s honorable reception of him, and the coronation (S5, Anno 896, lines 1155–1156). The cited passage does not mention a secret invitation from Formosus. This is an omission, not a refutation, but it matters when historians present the invitation as certain.

### 5. Formosus was not continuously condemned before 891

The earlier conflict with John VIII was real enough to leave synodal and epistolary traces, but Marinus later absolved Formosus and restored him to Porto (S10, pp. 5–6). Formosus then became pope in 891 (S1). The Cadaver Synod’s legal language therefore reopened old conflicts rather than simply enforcing an uninterrupted verdict.

## Source-transmission counterevidence

### 6. The 898 Ravenna material cannot be used casually as the direct transcript of 897

The critical catalogue records the Ravenna acts as a composite dossier with a protocol, synodal speech, capitula, and an imperial capitulary, preserved in differing manuscript witnesses (S6–S8). Older editions assigned parts to separate Roman and Ravenna councils; newer work places more material in Ravenna and questions the evidence for a distinct Roman council of John IX (S9/S10, Ch. 5, around pp. 174–181).

This does not erase the 897 event. It changes what can be said about the exact charges, who spoke, which council issued which canon, and how directly a later canon reflects the original trial.

### 7. Pro-Formosan sources are counterevidence, not neutral correction

Auxilius and related writers defend Formosus’s ordinations and attack the anti-Formosan settlement (S12). They are indispensable for showing the controversy’s aftermath and for preserving details missing from annals, but they are polemical works. Their account of Stephen’s role, the Tiber, weights, and the treatment of ordinations must be compared with the Fulda and Ravenna evidence, not simply adopted because it is sympathetic to Formosus.

## Working verdict

The safe historical core is: **a Roman council under Stephen VI posthumously condemned/deposed Formosus after removing his body from its grave; the burial status and ordinations were attacked; the settlement was quickly contested and later reversed.** The exact date, presiding political faction, sequence of reburials, number of fingers, presence of a deacon, and agency behind the Tiber episode require source labels and caveats.
