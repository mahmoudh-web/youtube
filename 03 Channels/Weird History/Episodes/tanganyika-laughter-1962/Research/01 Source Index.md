## Research question

What did the contemporary medical reports actually record about the outbreak that began at a mission-run girls’ school near Kashasha in January 1962, and how securely can later explanations such as mass psychogenic illness be applied without diagnosing the people involved or flattening local context?

## Conflict and episode promise

The familiar story is “an epidemic of contagious laughter.” The primary medical account is narrower and stranger: recurrent attacks of laughing and crying, restlessness and occasional violence affected pupils and later villagers; the attacks disrupted schooling and ordinary duties; examinations and laboratory tests reported no significant physical abnormality; and the doctors tentatively suggested “mass hysteria” while explicitly calling for cultural-context study. The episode’s unanswered angle is therefore not whether a funny joke infected Tanganyika, but how a cautious medical record became a confident story about laughter, contagion and diagnosis.

## Source-quality key

- **A — primary or near-primary:** contemporary medical report, journal record, government record or contemporaneous newspaper.
- **B — scholarly or institutional secondary:** peer-reviewed historical/medical analysis or authoritative institutional source that quotes or contextualises the primary record.
- **C — reproduction or popular secondary:** useful for retrieval and comparison, not sufficient alone for a material claim.

## Key entities and voices

- **A. M. Rankin:** Makerere University College professor of medicine and co-author of the 1963 report.
- **P. J. Philip:** Medical Officer in Bukoba, Tanganyika and co-author of the 1963 report.
- **Kashasha mission-run girls’ school:** Initial reported site; pupils were 12–18 in the report, and the school staff were not affected according to the report.
- **Nshamba, Ramashenye and Kanyangereka:** Later sites named in the report; retain the report’s distances and village/school descriptions until maps and local place-name history are checked.
- **Bahaya/Haya-speaking community:** The report records local names and explanations; community voice is underrepresented because the original investigators say they obtained no relevant information from the initially affected Kashasha pupils.
- **Christian F. Hempelmann:** Later scholar who argues the episode is better understood as motor-variant MPI and not as humorous or contagious laughter.
- **Yolana Pringle:** Historian who examines the wider East African psychiatric context and warns against treating colonial/postcolonial psychiatric categories as culturally transparent.

## Primary-source retrieval queue

1. Obtain and inspect the original pages 167–170 of Rankin and Philip; record the map, wording, sample sizes, test methods, dates and references exactly.
2. Retrieve Kagwa’s full 1964 article and distinguish its direct Bukoba material, wider East African comparisons, terminology and claims about cause.
3. Locate Tanganyika health-ministry, Bukoba district, school and mission records, if extant, for attendance, closure, medical referrals, food supplies, communications and local-language descriptions.
4. Check the cited Virus Research Institute at Entebbe material and determine exactly what samples, assays and time points were used; do not translate “no virus found” into “virus impossible.”
5. Consult a Tanzania/Haya historian or language specialist on Kashasha, Nshamba, Kanyangereka, Bahaya/Haya terminology and the translation of the recorded local names.
6. Treat later oral-history or media accounts as distinct testimonies. Obtain consent/rights and document who was interviewed, when, in which language and through which translator.
