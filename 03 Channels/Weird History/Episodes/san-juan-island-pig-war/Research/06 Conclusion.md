## Evidence-backed conclusion

The pig did not create the San Juan sovereignty crisis. It supplied the immediate incident in a dispute that already contained an ambiguous 1846 treaty, two competing boundary channels, an HBC farm asserting British presence, American settlement and county claims, earlier customs and tax confrontations, and a boundary commission that had failed to agree.

The escalation point was the decision to use troops. After the 15 June 1859 shooting, local accounts diverged about compensation and threats. American settlers petitioned for protection, and Gen. William Harney ordered a U.S. occupation. Capt. George Pickett landed on 27 July. British authorities responded with naval forces and Royal Marines, but commanders on the scene did not attack. The U.S. War Department later said the occupation was not approved, and President Buchanan sent Winfield Scott to contain the situation.

Scott's compromise converted an imminent confrontation into a joint military occupation. That arrangement was a holding device: it let both governments preserve their claim while avoiding the humiliation and danger of forcing the other side out. It lasted because the underlying Haro-versus-Rosario question remained unresolved, not because officials were still litigating the price of a pig. The 1871 Treaty of Washington finally required German arbitration; the 21 October 1872 award selected the Haro Channel, after which British forces withdrew in November. U.S. troops remained until July 1874.

The strongest answer to the episode question is therefore:

> The pig was the spark, but the twelve-year crisis was a boundary-and-jurisdiction dispute made dangerous by imperial competition, settler expansion, local officials acting ahead of national policy, and governments unwilling to surrender a sovereignty claim without arbitration.

## What can be stated as fact

- The 1846 treaty used boundary language that did not name Haro or Rosario.
- HBC operated Belle Vue Sheep Farm and British and American authorities asserted competing authority on the island before 1859.
- Cutlar killed an HBC pig on 15 June 1859.
- Harney ordered a U.S. occupation; Pickett landed on 27 July; British naval forces confronted the U.S. position without beginning a battle.
- Scott and Douglas arranged joint occupation while the governments pursued settlement.
- The 1871 treaty referred the channel question to German arbitration; the 1872 award selected Haro; the two military camps were then removed on different dates.

## What must remain qualified

- The exact compensation demand and alleged arrest or eviction threats.
- Harney's motive beyond the documented military decision.
- Exact force and gun totals.
- The phrase “only casualty” outside the narrow U.S.–British battle context.
- Any account of Indigenous experience that is not supported by Indigenous/community-informed research.
