## Purpose

This folder is the source-led research area for the provisional episode “Did America Really Panic over Martians?”, about the 30 October 1938 CBS broadcast of *The War of the Worlds*, the documented local alarm that followed, and the later construction of a national panic story. It is a research pack, not a script or production brief.

## Responsibilities

- Preserve a traceable source index, chronology, claim ledger, counterevidence record, uncertainty note and provisional conclusion.
- Separate what the broadcast actually presented from what listeners, newspapers, regulators and researchers later said happened.
- Distinguish documented individual or local fear from the disputed size and geography of the reaction.
- Record source quality, exact page/line/item locators, access dates, confidence and unresolved caveats.
- Stop for human verification before narration, packaging, visual generation, editing, QC or publication.

## Key Decisions

- Use `war-of-the-worlds-panic-1938` as the episode folder slug under the authoritative Weird History channel folder.
- Treat the broadcast audio and transcript as the primary record of a fictional drama presented in news-bulletin form; its Martian invasion is not a real event.
- Prefer National Archives FCC records, the broadcast record, contemporary newspaper objects, the University of Michigan fan-mail collection and Cantril’s 1940 study; use later scholarship to explain method and memory, not to erase disagreement.
- Preserve the strongest defensible conclusion as: some listeners were genuinely frightened or acted on the broadcast, but the extent of a nationwide “mass panic” remains contested and cannot be inferred from sensational headlines alone.
- Treat Cantril’s listener estimates, contemporary newspaper counts and later anti-panic critiques as claims with methods and limitations, not interchangeable population measurements.

## Constraints and Invariants

- Access date for this pack is 2026-09-14.
- Do not state that “America,” “the nation” or “millions” panicked without naming the underlying source, sample and limitation.
- Do not state that no one panicked. The FCC correspondence, local records and surviving letters document genuine fear, confusion, complaints and precautionary action.
- Do not convert 1,349 surviving fan letters, 600-plus FCC contacts, 135 Cantril interviews or one newspaper’s telephone count into a national prevalence rate.
- Attribute Cantril’s estimates to his 1940 study and record that later scholarship challenges the way the study and its “mass panic” label have been remembered.
- Historical quotations and descriptions of fear, injury or self-harm require careful wording and human review; avoid reproducing sensational claims as verified outcomes.
- Audio, photographs, newspaper scans and fan letters are archival leads, not approved production assets. Rights, permissions, fair-use rationale and platform suitability require a separate human decision.
