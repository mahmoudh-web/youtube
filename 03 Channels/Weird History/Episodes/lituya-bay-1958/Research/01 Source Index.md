## Research question

How were eyewitness testimony, tree-line destruction and geological measurements combined to estimate the 1958 Lituya Bay wave's run-up, and what does the reported “1,700-foot wave” actually measure?

## Episode promise

Reconstruct the evidence chain behind the number: a quake and slope failure, eyewitness observations of the source and moving water, a sharp forest trimline surveyed after the event, and later physical/numerical models. The safe resolution is a measured run-up elevation, not a 1,700-foot open-ocean wave height.

## Evidence rules

- **A — primary field evidence:** contemporaneous USGS field reports, survey measurements, photographs, maps and recorded eyewitness accounts.
- **B — peer-reviewed reconstruction:** physical or numerical models that state their assumptions and output type.
- **C — official synthesis:** an institutional explanation that usefully connects the original evidence but is not itself the original measurement.
- **D — later secondary account:** useful for orientation only; do not let it outrank the underlying report.
- Keep **run-up elevation**, **water-surface elevation**, **wave height/amplitude**, **flow depth** and **inundation distance** as separate quantities.

## Measurement vocabulary

- **Run-up elevation:** the highest elevation reached by water moving up a slope, measured relative to a datum. In this case the maximum observed trimline was about 1,720 ft above mean sea level on the spur southwest of Gilbert Inlet.
- **Wave height or amplitude:** the vertical scale of the wave in the water while it propagates or impacts. Later models estimate this in tens or hundreds of metres; it is not interchangeable with 524 m / 1,720 ft run-up.
- **Water-surface elevation:** the modeled or observed elevation of the water surface at a location and time; it can be far below the maximum upslope run-up.
- **Trimline:** the sharp upper boundary of near-total forest destruction used as a proxy for the highest reach of the destructive flow. It is evidence of inundation/run-up, not a direct photograph of the wave crest at that elevation.

## Source-handling rules

1. Anchor the episode in S1's eyewitness, field-observation, trimline and photogrammetric evidence.
2. Use S2 for fault movement and the early “more than 1700 feet” formulation, but preserve local-versus-UTC date conventions.
3. Use S5 to quantify the distinction: its literature review reports modeled maximum impulse-wave heights of roughly 94–162 m in several studies, while the observed maximum run-up is 524 m a.s.l.
4. Keep S6's two-slide interpretation alongside the single-slide interpretation; neither resolves every observation.
5. Do not use “1,700-foot wave” without the qualifier “run-up elevation” or an equivalent explanation.
