## “War” versus maritime confrontation

The source record supports three politically named fisheries confrontations, not a conventional declared war or a single battle. That does not make them harmless. UK Hansard records three shots in a December 1975 incident (S13), and multiple sources describe collisions and dangerous cut warps. An Icelandic exhibition summary says no cannon shots were exchanged (S16), so “bloodless” is unsafe. The pack should use “Cod Wars” as a historical label and “fisheries confrontations” as the factual description.

## “Who won?”

Iceland progressively established larger claimed zones, and later political accounts frame the outcome as a national victory. But the 1974 ICJ judgment rejected the unilateral opposability of the 50-mile extension while recognising preferential rights (S11). The 1976 notes regulated British access and explicitly preserved jurisdictional views (S12). Political leverage, legal position, and operational access therefore point in different directions; a one-word winner is interpretation, not an established fact.

## “Britain recognised 200 miles”

The later international trend moved toward 200-mile EEZs, and the UK accepted a tightly regulated arrangement after the 1975–76 crisis. The 1976 treaty nevertheless says the arrangement does not prejudice either party’s views on mutual jurisdiction and lasts six months (S12 pp. 162, 166). Safest wording: Britain accepted a negotiated, limited access regime in practice; do not claim unconditional legal recognition based on this treaty.

## Wire cutters and chronology

Wire/net cutting is well supported for the later phases (S1, S7, S8), but accounts differ on terminology and exact mechanism: special hooks, a chain, trawl clippers, or net cutters. NAT dates one 1972 event to 5 September and a 1975 event to 16 November (S17); neither date has yet been checked against Coast Guard and ship logs. Nothing in the current record supports putting wire cutting in the 1958 phase.

## Catch figures and quotas

The 1973 arrangement is described in Hansard as an estimated 130,000-ton package but explicitly without an actual catch limit (S10). NOAA says Iceland expected approximately 30,000 metric tons during the final six months of the 1976 arrangement, while the UN text sets effort, area, conservation and reporting conditions rather than an announced fixed quota (S15; S12). Keep negotiated estimates, expectations, and treaty obligations separate.

## Dates

Official Icelandic institutional material and the Commons Library place the 200-mile regulation’s effective date on 15 October 1975 (S4; Althingi). Some secondary web accounts contain conflicting dates, including a November date. Use 15 October provisionally and flag the Icelandic original for bilingual verification.

## Casualties and injury

The UK’s 12 December 1975 statement says no British vessels were damaged and no British casualties occurred in that particular incident (S13). NAT reports a fatal 1973 Icelandic engineer accident (S17), while the exhibition summary’s “no cannon shots” conflicts with the UK shot report (S16). Do not use “no deaths,” “bloodless,” or a total casualty number until death, injury, Coast Guard, RN, and vessel records are retrieved.

## Competing national and legal narratives

- Icelandic official sources emphasise exceptional dependence, conservation, and a coastal state’s right or priority to protect resources (S4, S5).
- UK official sources emphasise existing agreements, traditional fishing access, international law, danger to trawlers, and the costs to the distant-water fleet (S3, S10, S13, S14).
- The ICJ provides an adjudicated but mixed framework rather than a simple verdict for one side (S11).
- NATO foregrounds alliance cohesion and strategic geography and says Luns helped press Britain to back down (S7); that is valuable institutional evidence but not proof of a single causal explanation.

## Source-bias map

| Source family | What it is strong for | What it may overstate or omit |
|---|---|---|
| UN Treaty Series / ICJ | Text of agreements and legal holdings | Incidents, public emotion, operational realities |
| UK Hansard / Cabinet / RN records | UK decisions, stated legal position, deployments | Icelandic motives, unreported incidents, political self-justification |
| Icelandic Althingi / Ministry / Coast Guard | Icelandic policy, regulations, enforcement perspective | British legal/economic position; retrospective national framing |
| NATO history | Alliance context and mediation narrative | Causal compression and “victory/defeat” language |
| IWM / archival catalogues | Existence and provenance of visual records | Catalogue description is not complete incident verification; rights status |
| NOAA / scholarly retrospectives | Triangulation and broader chronology | Dependence on prior reports; may smooth source conflicts |
| Tourism/exhibition summaries | Leads, public memory, accessible chronology | Date, casualty, and mechanism errors; simplified national story |

## Terminology and editorial risk

Guðmundsson’s 2006 abstract (S19) attributes “Cod War” to Daily Telegraph journalist Llewellyn Chanter. That is a terminology lead, not a settled origin story until the contemporary newspaper usage is checked. “Fishery zone,” “territorial sea,” and “EEZ” are not interchangeable; use each source’s legal term and explain later UNCLOS context separately.

## Research implication

The strongest defensible through-line is not “small boats defeated a navy.” It is that escalating enforcement made continued naval protection costly and dangerous while international law and alliance diplomacy changed the bargaining environment. This remains an interpretation built from the ledger, not a source quotation or final narration.
