## Purpose

This note keeps the attractive “2,500-mile thorn wall” story from becoming a false certainty. Each counterpoint is a possible correction, not an instruction to minimize the coercive or fiscal consequences of the system.

## Counterevidence matrix

| Popular or tempting claim | Evidence that complicates it | Editorial consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Britain built one continuous 2,500-mile living hedge. | S1 gives 1,817 miles for a named 1867–68 Imperial Line excluding other sections. S2 records that in Hissar one-fifth lacked a proper live hedge, young plants covered much of the rest, and dry hedge/stone/earth barriers filled gaps. S11 describes cactus over a large part and stone walls/ditches elsewhere. | Say “a long customs line that incorporated living hedge sections,” not “a continuous hedge.” |
| “2,500 miles” is the line’s stable length. | S3 gives 1,200 miles for one 1873 portion; S7 identifies a 250-mile Bombay frontier segment; S11 gives nearly 2,500 miles in one retrospective, then records 760+ eastern miles abandoned in 1874 and only an Indus remnant after 1879. | Every mileage needs a date, component and source. |
| The hedge was a natural wonder that simply grew. | S2 reports experiments, dry-hedge backing and proposals for stone walls/scarped earth. S3–S8 show active administrative realignment, cordons, production controls, rail planning and expenditure. | Show maintenance, surveying, water/soil problems and bureaucratic labour, not just botanical spectacle. |
| The line existed only to stop “criminal smugglers.” | S3 says the main object was to prevent Native-State salt entering unless duty was paid; S9–S10 authorize searches, seizure, destruction and apprehension. The tax differential was designed by the fiscal system itself. | Attribute “smuggling” to the administration and explain the state-created price boundary. |
| The tax was uniformly oppressive in the same way everywhere. | S11 and S12 show different provincial rates and later equalization. S13 records critics’ claim that Madras/Bombay increases hit poor consumers after famine; government speakers defended equalization and removal of transit duties. | Preserve regional variation and both the fiscal rationale and the burden critique. |
| The tax’s household effect is already numerically known. | The located set has parliamentary argument, revenue figures and a consumption estimate, but no verified district-level household budgets or full price series. S16 and S20 point to records that may contain them. | Use “likely regressive” only with explanation; do not invent a percentage of income or a universal health outcome. |
| Equalization immediately freed people from the barrier. | S11 says the 1879 line was abandoned except the Indus portion; S11 also says rates later rose again in 1888 and the Kohat remnant lasted into 1896. | Explain administrative replacement and persistence of taxation separately from hedge abandonment. |
| British policy alone explains the line. | S3–S7 show negotiations with Rajputana and Central Indian states, Native-State salt sources and transit revenues; S5 says abolition would require consent and compensation arrangements. | Include princely-state fiscal interests and local political agency without treating negotiated participation as equality. |
| A later map proves the exact hedge route on the ground. | S17 says Moxham found a 1879 Agra District map and presents a map adapted from his book. S19 is ongoing fieldwork. The map’s date, scale, boundaries and legend require direct inspection. | Use maps as dated representations, not as self-proving proof of continuous vegetation. |
| The archive is silent because the hedge left no evidence. | S16 lists Plowden, salt-administration, Board of Customs/Salt/Opium and Northern India Salt Revenue records; S1–S3 already show surviving primary material. | Treat the current pack as incomplete retrieval, not proof of total archival absence. |

## Source-bias checks

- The administrative reports are unusually valuable for costs, mileage, plant quality and staffing, but they describe the state’s own goals and may understate coercion or local resistance.
- Hansard preserves public criticism and official defense, but parliamentary speeches are mediated arguments, not direct household testimony.
- The QDL Batten memorandum is a rare contemporaneous voice about fiscal mechanics and abolition, but Batten advocates a more “rational” imperial tax collection system rather than abolition of the tax itself.
- Modern accounts are useful for recovering the story and finding maps/oral histories, but S17 explicitly relies heavily on Moxham; the chain must be shown rather than treated as independent corroboration.
- Absence of named workers, consumers or local-language testimony in this pass reflects the search path and access limits, not absence of those people from the history.

## Required balancing evidence

1. A full contemporary Imperial Customs annual report for each stage used in the episode.
2. The official 1843 and 1875 Acts and any 1874/1879 notices implementing partial or final abandonment.
3. Plowden’s report and salt-administration manuals for prices, production, seizures, prosecutions and consumption.
4. Records or scholarship from Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Bengal/Orissa and princely-state archives.
5. A specialist review of “impact” language so the episode does not substitute British fiscal categories for local experience.
