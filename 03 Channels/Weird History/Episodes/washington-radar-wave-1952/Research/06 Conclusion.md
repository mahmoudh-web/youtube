## Provisional assessment

The surviving record supports two documented July 1952 radar-visual episodes around Washington National Airport and Andrews Air Force Base. Civilian controllers and military personnel reported radar returns; pilots and ground observers reported lights; and F-94 interceptors were dispatched during the 26–27 July episode. The strongest Air Force case file is explicit that it was incomplete: interceptor reports were missing and several commercial/CAA pilots had not been contacted.

The official explanation developed in public was temperature inversion/anomalous propagation, with reflected ground returns offered as a mechanism for radar targets. The CAA’s 1953 technical report includes July Washington entries in Table I and correlates the tabulated records with weather data. Its direct Washington motion-analysis and formal conclusions, however, focus on observations beginning 13 and 15 August 1952. It therefore cannot be used as a neat, complete solution to the July 26–27 story, nor dismissed as if it contained no July analysis. It also does not automatically explain independent visual reports.

The safest current conclusion is therefore: **Washington’s radar screens did record unusual, unidentified returns during two July 1952 episodes, but the evidence does not identify them as extraterrestrial vehicles. An inversion-linked radar phenomenon, ordinary returns or misidentification, independent visual effects, or a mixture remain competing explanations until the primary records are reconciled event by event.**

## What the pack can currently establish

- Project Blue Book/National Archives records preserve a case file for the 26–27 July episode involving Washington National ARTC and tower radar, Andrews approach-control radar, visual reports and two F-94 intercept flights.
- Air Force reports and source-indexed chronology place a separate radar-visual episode on 19–20 July, with reports from Washington National, Andrews/Bolling and commercial aviation witnesses.
- The case file records up to 12 simultaneous ARTC targets on 26–27 July, but target counts are not counts of confirmed physical craft.
- The 29 July Pentagon press conference presented temperature inversion/reflected ground returns as a probable explanation, while the transcript also records uncertainty about precise simultaneous radar fixes and a desire for better measurement.
- CAA Technical Development Report No. 180 tabulated July Washington records and weather correlations, and found that many directly analysed August air-traffic radar targets were probably secondary reflections associated with inversion layers and refracting areas. Its formal Washington conclusions concern August observations and must be scoped accurately.
- Later CIA and Air Force histories reject an established extraterrestrial or national-security explanation at program level, but that later institutional position is not the same as a fully resolved July case file.
- National press coverage is established; the distribution of belief, the accuracy of every witness memory and the meaning of the sightings are not.

## Competing hypotheses retained

1. **Anomalous propagation/temperature inversion:** technically supported for a class of radar targets and the official explanation; July records are tabulated/correlated, but direct application to every July return remains unverified.
2. **Ordinary traffic, surface returns, birds or radar artefacts:** supported by the case’s highway-vectoring episode and the press-conference discussion of reflected ground targets, but not sufficient without target-by-target reconstruction.
3. **A compound explanation:** radar artefacts plus independent lights or misidentifications may fit the evidence better than one universal cause; this remains an interpretation to test.
4. **Secret technology:** historically relevant to Cold War air defence, but unsupported by positive evidence in the reviewed record; retain only as historical context, not a conclusion.
5. **Extraterrestrial vehicles:** an unsupported allegation/hypothesis based on unresolved reports, not a conclusion supported by the source pack.

## Editorial interpretation, clearly labelled

The record should not be described as a confirmed invasion. Its historical interest is that a high-stakes air-defence system turned ambiguous returns and human observations into a public event before the underlying records were complete. The official response made a plausible atmospheric explanation prominent, yet the surviving paperwork also preserves missing reports, conflicting time notation and disagreement about whether a weak inversion could explain the strongest returns. The story’s durable mystery comes from that gap between observation, explanation and proof.

This is an interpretation of the source set, not a statement by a 1952 witness or agency. It must remain subordinate to the claim ledger and the human-verification gate.
