## Executive verdict

**Pass with corrections for human verification; not safe for narration or production yet.**

The central historical claims are supported: Carrington and Hodgson recorded an unusual solar optical event; Kew recorded a short disturbance near that observation and a separate major disturbance beginning on 2 September; contemporary compilations document widespread aurorae and heterogeneous telegraph effects. The modern-risk claims are appropriately conditional when tied to NOAA/NWS and Lloyd's/AER.

One load-bearing claim remains unresolved: the original newspaper page behind the Boston–Portland battery-free exchange could not be opened in this check. A 2024 AAS historical article reproduces a version of the exchange and identifies the New York Times page, but that is a secondary reproduction, not a substitute for the newspaper scan. Do not use the dialogue, operator names or a duration as a verbatim fact until the original page and copy chain are checked.

## Source quality and coverage

### Strong sources opened

- Carrington's paper is direct contemporary observation, including his own caution about connecting the solar flash and magnetic disturbance.
- Stewart's Kew paper is a near-contemporary observatory record with photographic traces and a clearly stated distinction between the 28 August and 2 September disturbances. Its causal discussion is historical interpretation, not a CME measurement.
- Loomis's 1860–61 papers are high-value near-contemporary compilations. They are not direct observation of every incident: their parenthetical references point back to earlier AJS pages, newspapers and reports that still need source-chain checking for dramatic anecdotes.
- The Hayakawa paper is peer-reviewed modern reconstruction. It supports the inferred ICME/CME framework and compares the event with other extreme storms; it cannot replace the original records.
- The BGS [digitised magnetogram dataset](https://www.data.gov.uk/dataset/9cffcc88-14a4-481e-955a-eea522ba0ed6/digitised-magnetogram-data-from-the-carrington-storm-25th-august-to-5th-september-1859-recorded), lines 24–40, confirms a ten-day 1859 record, modern scaling to SI units and quasi-minute values. Its processing is modern and its licence is not set on the landing page.
- NOAA/NWS are authoritative institutional mechanism explainers, not event records. Lloyd's/AER is a transparent but conditional risk model. The National Academies page supports a distinction between documented technology effects and less certain socioeconomic impacts, but the pack does not have a checked full-report locator for any exact National Academies figure.

## Unsupported, overstated, contradictory, or missing-source claims

- **Missing-source / unresolved:** the exact Boston–Portland dialogue, named operators, duration and verbatim wording in C10.
- **Over-precise:** the Kew/Carrington near-coincidence should not be presented as one exact 11:20 time; Stewart records approximately 11:15 GMT while Carrington's paper gives approximately 11:20 a.m. for the Kew record.
- **Locator error:** C06 and C11 cite page ranges that do not contain the most direct geographic/line-specific passages. The underlying claims are supported, but the locators need correction.
- **Potential conflation:** C17's NOAA cost wording must be separated from Lloyd's/AER's U.S.-scope model. “Trillions” is not one interchangeable estimate.
- **Not claimable from this pack:** a precise CME speed, mass, flare class, Dst value, universal internet collapse, global blackout, every-office fire, total telegraph failure, global death toll or fixed repair period.
- **Causal boundary:** “caused by a CME” is a modern reconstruction, not an observation by Carrington, Hodgson, Stewart or Loomis. The safe historical wording is that the records support a coupled solar-terrestrial interpretation.
- **Competing interpretation:** Hayakawa et al. conclude that the Carrington event is one of the most extreme storms but likely not unique. Avoid an unqualified “largest ever” claim unless a specific metric and comparison set are named.
