## Scope and stop condition

This note is the research stop for the *War of the Worlds* pack. It is not a script, title, thumbnail, visual treatment, legal opinion or production asset. ## Legal, ethical and historical flags

### Sensational harm claims

Headlines and later retellings mention suicides, injuries, hospital treatment, traffic jams and mass flight. These are potentially defamatory or misleading even when historical subjects are deceased because they can misrepresent institutions, communities and named individuals. Use only claims with a stable primary record and clear attribution; otherwise describe them as unverified reports or omit them.

### Self-selected correspondence

FCC contacts and fan letters are valuable voices, but people who wrote are not a random sample of listeners. A large archive can create a false sense of representativeness. Use letters to show the range and texture of reception, not to calculate the national proportion.

### Population estimates and category drift

“Heard,” “believed,” “was frightened,” “was disturbed,” “called for information,” “fled” and “required medical treatment” are different variables. Never move from one to another without a source that defines the transition.

### Press motive

The press-radio rivalry explanation is plausible and historically important, but motive should not be presented as settled fact. The source pack currently supports a documented sensational frame plus a later hypothesis about competition; it does not prove every editor coordinated a campaign.

### Historical language

Avoid mocking listeners as gullible. The broadcast’s form, familiar locations, authoritative voices, late tuning and the international crisis context made misinterpretation possible for some people. The narrator should explain the conditions without treating fear as proof of stupidity.

### Rights and privacy

The University of Michigan warns that some fan-mail materials may be protected by copyright and places responsibility for permissions on the user. Newspaper scans and photographs also need rights review. Do not publish names, addresses, letter images, audio or script excerpts until the human producer records the permission/fair-use decision.

## Mandatory human-verification gate

Before any material claim advances, a human must:

1. Listen to the Indiana University recording from beginning to end, log stable timestamps and compare the audio with S2’s transcript.
2. Retrieve an original or archival Howard Koch script, compare wording and scene order, and mark any transcript/audio divergence.
3. Read the full Paul Morton and J.V. Yaukey FCC records, including attachments, and record exact sender/date/recipient/page locators.
4. Inspect the 31 October *New York Times* page and at least two additional contemporary regional newspaper issues; distinguish headline framing, reported anecdote, editorial comment and verified record.
5. Retrieve the original CBS/AIPO follow-up survey reports and the 5,000-household telephone survey source; reconcile their universes, dates and definitions before using percentages.
6. Read Cantril’s methods, appendices and tables; record how “six million,” “one million,” “frightened” and “disturbed” were derived and what sampling limitations apply.
7. Sample the Michigan collection by reaction tag, location and date, record the archive’s selection/transcription limits, and do not calculate prevalence from it.
8. Read Pooley and Socolow in full and have a specialist review any statement about the press-radio war, Cantril’s authorship, “mass hysteria” or the magic-bullet theory.
9. Verify every injury, death, suicide, medical-treatment, traffic-jam or flight anecdote against a named primary or near-primary record; remove unsupported claims.
10. Complete rights checks for audio, script, newspaper scans, photographs and listener letters before any visual or audio production.

Until these checks are complete, this pack remains in **Research** and must not advance to scriptwriting, packaging, visual generation, editing, QC or publication.
