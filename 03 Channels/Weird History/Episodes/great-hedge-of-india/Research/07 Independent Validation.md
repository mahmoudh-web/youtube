## Executive verdict

**PASS WITH CORRECTIONS — safe to send to human verification, not safe to advance to script or publication.**

The pack's central correction is sound: the evidence supports a long, changing Inland Customs Line that used living hedge sections alongside dry brush, stone and earth barriers; it does not support a continuous 2,500-mile living hedge. Its strongest evidence concerns administrative purpose, line length at specified dates, infrastructure, staffing, revenue and legal powers. Evidence for household impact, local labour conditions, health effects and the earliest hedge construction remains incomplete.

The pack needs the following corrections before human approval:

1. Replace the repeated “Hissar division” reference to the 1867–68 snapshot with the report's printed heading **“HANSIE DIVISION”** (modern geographic identification should be checked separately).
2. Correct the 1875 Act locator from “sections 6–8” to **sections 6–7** for customs zones, lines, traffic restrictions and searches. Section 8 concerns a Central Provinces mileage-rate power; sections 17–19 add detention, seizure and search powers.
3. Keep the QDL figures explicitly attributed to Batten until the manuscript images, rather than only the automatic transcriptions, have been checked.
4. Do not use the Avery/Moxham claim that a 2,504-mile hedge was almost entirely living vegetation; it conflicts with the contemporary report's documented mixed and incomplete barriers.
5. Do not turn parliamentary speeches or the later official syntheses into neutral measurements of incidence, health, popular experience or causation.

## Timeline-only checks

- **Before 1843:** supported at a broad level. The Gazetteer pp. 249–250 and the Government of India's Salt Department history describe varying pre-existing systems and transit duties. Exact regional histories remain open.
- **1843:** the Act date and legal powers are supported, but the Act is regional in scope and does not establish a single physical construction date for the later line.
- **1840s–1850s hedge development:** unresolved at primary-source level. The current support is the Avery essay and Moxham's secondary book; retain the low/medium confidence label.
- **1857–1858 continuity:** plausible but not demonstrated by the cited passages inspected here. Add an administrative continuity record if this transition becomes narratively important.
- **1861–1866 revenue:** the Hansard statement is verified as a speech; independently verify against Indian financial returns before presenting the figures without attribution.
- **1867–1868:** verified. The report's printed pages 1–3 give the 1,817-mile Imperial Line and 10,832 officers and men; printed p. 139 gives the HANSIE local hedge snapshot.
- **1869–1870:** a stronger live-hedge program and experiments are plausible from the report sequence and Avery/Moxham, but the exact period-level claim needs the relevant 1869–70 pages.
- **1870–1874:** the Sambhar lease, railway rationale and 760-plus-mile abandonment are supported by the Gazetteer, QDL and Strachey; the implementing notices remain missing.
- **1875:** legal machinery is supported, subject to stable official-scan retrieval and the §6–7 locator correction.
- **1878:** rates and the equalization argument are supported by Hansard and the Gazetteer; regional effects must remain explicit.
- **1879 and 1896:** the Gazetteer and Strachey support the staged end of the wider line and the later Indus remnant, but the exact notices and ground-level meaning of “abandoned” remain unresolved.

## Source-quality and coverage assessment

### Strong sources

- The 1867–68 Imperial Customs report is a contemporary administrative primary source. Local PDF text and page images confirm the 1,817-mile/10,832-staff passage and the p. 139 HANSIE description. It is excellent for administration, infrastructure and official priorities, but it reflects the department's own perspective.
- QDL Mss Eur F126/74 is a contemporary British Library manuscript record. The record is primary and the folio pages are individually addressable, but QDL labels the transcriptions automatic. It is strong for Batten's proposal and stated figures once the images are checked; it is not neutral evidence of social impact.
- The 1843 and 1875 Acts are primary legal texts. The official India Code URLs were unstable or returned 404 in direct retrieval. Search-indexed official text and an accessible reproduction permit provisional checking, but final quotation requires the official scans.
- Hansard is primary evidence for what named parliamentary speakers argued. It is not a neutral survey of prices, consumption, health or popular opinion.

### Useful but limited syntheses

- The Imperial Gazetteer and Strachey's 1882 financial history are later official or administrative syntheses. They independently converge on the hybrid line, Sambhar, staged abandonment, duty changes and the Indus remnant, but both inherit a colonial administrative frame and should not replace contemporary notices.
- The British Library catalogue record IOR/V/27/324 is a retrieval aid, not event evidence. It confirms that the missing Plowden, 1894 administration, manuals and Board papers exist as relevant archival targets.
- The Government of India's modern Salt Department page is useful institutional context for pre-existing variation and Plowden, but it is retrospective and should be checked against the underlying reports.
- The Avery Review is a modern curatorial essay whose footnotes state that it relies heavily on Moxham. It is useful for leads, maps and reception, but its claims about “state-sponsored” famine, “salt deprivation” killing millions, forced takeover, slavery and an almost entirely living 2,504-mile hedge are not established by the primary sources checked here. Several are contradicted or materially complicated by the Imperial report.
- Moxham's Google Books record confirms the 2001 book and its broad subject, but full text/page evidence was not available. O'Carroll's page confirms an ongoing 2018–present fieldwork/design-research project in rural Uttar Pradesh; it does not prove nineteenth-century route, continuity or impact.

### Coverage gaps

The pack is well covered on official purpose, administrative scale, mixed construction, fiscal mechanics and legal powers. It remains weak on:

- district-level prices, household budgets and consumption returns;
- local-language testimony and regional histories;
- names, pay, recruitment and conditions of hedge-maintenance workers;
- prosecution/seizure frequency and how rules operated at particular crossings;
- the exact first hedge planting and the 1874/1879 implementation notices;
- health or famine causation attributable to the salt tax rather than contemporaneous political argument;
- popular response in British and princely territories.

## Unsupported, overstated, contradictory or missing-source claims

1. **“Hissar division” in C05 and related source notes:** the inspected primary page is headed “HANSIE DIVISION.” Correct the name and check the modern place identification.
2. **“2,504-mile hedge composed entirely of living vegetation” (Avery, paragraph 30):** contradicted by the primary p. 139 HANSIE snapshot, the report's general review pp. 83–84, and the Gazetteer's explicit stone-wall/ditch qualification. Do not use as fact.
3. **Continuous 2,500-mile hedge:** contradicted by the primary snapshot and hybrid descriptions. The conclusion correctly rejects it.
4. **2,504 versus 2,500:** retain 2,500 only as the Gazetteer's approximate wider-line figure unless a human inspects the Moxham page behind 2,504. Do not blend the numbers.
5. **Forced, unpaid or universally underpaid hedge labour:** not established. The report documents labour, maintenance, disputes over materials/property and poor pay for the customs force, but not one labour regime for all local planters.
6. **Salt tax caused millions of deaths, starvation or a quantified health crisis:** not established by the cited pack. The Avery essay's causal wording must be removed or separately sourced to demographic, medical and famine scholarship.
7. **Every traveller was searched or could not cross:** the Acts establish powers and regulated crossings, not universal enforcement. The pack correctly flags this.
8. **A single 1879 disappearance:** the later official sources support staged abandonment and an Indus remnant. The exact notice, date and physical afterlife of each section are missing.
9. **“The British forcibly took over” Sambhar:** the Avery essay uses that wording, while Strachey describes an amicable arrangement and Batten describes a lease/compensation context. Present the legal and political terms only after checking the lease and treaty records.
10. **Avery's “slavery,” “genocide,” “starved millions” and similar moral/causal language:** these are interpretation or advocacy, not findings established by the cited primary records. They must not be presented as settled fact.

## Required corrections and additional primary-source checks

Before human approval of any narration-ready claim:

1. Correct HANSIE/Hissar throughout the researcher material and verify the corresponding historical geography.
2. Inspect the Imperial report page images around the introduction, printed p. 139, and the general review pp. 83–84; preserve exact headings and pagination.
3. Inspect QDL folios 3r, 3v, 5v, 6r and 8r against the automatic transcriptions. Confirm £105,728, 8,578, 30 lakhs, £900,000-plus, £142,000 and 250 miles.
4. Retrieve stable official scans of Acts XIV of 1843 and VIII of 1875. Record exact sections, territorial scope, penalties, search conditions, seizure rules and safeguards.
5. Locate the contemporary 1874 abandonment record and the 1879 abolition/implementation decision. Separate line mileage, live-hedge mileage, administrative abandonment and surviving physical remnants.
6. Retrieve Plowden's report, the 1894 salt-administration report and relevant Northern India/Madras/Bombay records from IOR/V/27/324 for prices, production, seizures, prosecutions, consumption and local labour.
7. Add regional or local-language scholarship for Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Central India, Bengal/Orissa and princely-state fiscal arrangements.
8. Independently test any health, famine, “forced labour,” household-income or local popular-response claim against specialist evidence; do not infer lived impact from legal powers or revenue totals.
9. Preserve source attribution and counterevidence whenever using colonial terms such as “smuggling,” “Native States,” “barbarous,” “subjects” or “oppression.”
