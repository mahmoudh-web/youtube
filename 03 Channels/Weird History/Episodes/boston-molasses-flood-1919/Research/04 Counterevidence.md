## Central conflict

The story has two different questions that must not be merged:

1. **Who or what caused the tank to fail?** USIA alleged an external anarchist bomb; plaintiffs and their experts argued that the tank was structurally unsound.
2. **What did the legal process establish?** The criminal grand jury did not indict; the later court-appointed auditor found USIA civilly liable; the claims were settled before a final jury verdict.

## Sabotage allegation: evidence offered by USIA

- USIA’s defense presented the theory that an “evilly disposed” person had placed an explosive in the tank. Contemporary Boston had real anarchist bombing activity, and later accounts say an employee reported a threat against the tank (S5, lines 42–44; S15, lines 31–58).
- Defense experts, including engineering professors and state police chemist Walter Wedger, testified that the failure looked like an explosion. Reconstructed testimony says model-tank tests were used to support that interpretation (S15, lines 31–36).
- A large tank fragment’s distance and the pattern of damage were treated by some defense experts as evidence of internal explosive force (S9, lines 60–65; S15, lines 60–87).

These points establish that sabotage was a serious litigation theory, not that sabotage occurred. The claim was made by the defendant and supported by defense testimony; it was not corroborated by a recovered bomb, identified perpetrator or final criminal conviction.

## Evidence against the sabotage allegation

- The 1919 inquiry account says the District Attorney told the grand jury the evidence tended to show faulty construction, and the grand jury returned no manslaughter indictment (S5, lines 37–41).
- Wedger’s evidence was internally contested: the later transcript account says he had earlier reported no evidence of an explosion, then testified for the defense that an explosive could have caused the failure; cross-examination highlighted the absence of broken glass and other expected blast evidence (S15, lines 40–71).
- Ogden’s 1925 report, as reproduced by later accounts, said that no bomb/high explosive or trace was found at or near the scene and that no anarchist was seen at the tank (S16). This is the auditor’s evidentiary conclusion, not proof that every alternative mechanism has been fully reconstructed.
- Spofford’s contemporary engineering report attributed the failure to insufficient plate thickness and rivets, with static pressure alone placing the structure in a dangerous condition (S5, lines 42–43; S15, lines 425–437).

## Structural-failure case: evidence and limits

- The tank’s reported leaks, vibration, repeated caulking and repainting are supported by witness testimony summarized from the litigation (S15, lines 142–157), but the human verifier must inspect the original transcript and distinguish firsthand observation from later retelling.
- Jell’s reported testimony acknowledged that he lacked technical training, did not submit plans to an engineer/architect, did not obtain an independent steel inspection and did not perform the full water test (S15, lines 257–380). These are powerful admissions if the transcript is verified, but they do not alone identify the exact fracture initiation point.
- Mayville’s modern analysis identifies stress concentrations, rivet-hole/punching effects and fracture mechanics as plausible mechanisms and notes that contemporary experts disagreed (S9, lines 97–159). It is an interpretation made with later tools, not a contemporaneous finding.
- Fermentation/gas pressure and temperature changes appear in the list of possible causes and later explanations (S7; S15, lines 78–93). Treat them as contributing hypotheses unless the original chemical and engineering reports establish more.

## Legal counterevidence and procedural nuance

- Bolster’s inquest criticized public oversight and the building department as well as USIA (S5, lines 31–36). This prevents a simplistic “company alone was officially convicted in 1919” account.
- The grand jury’s no-indictment result means the criminal record did not establish manslaughter beyond the charging stage. It does not decide the later civil question.
- Ogden was an auditor appointed to hear evidence and report to the Superior Court. His 1925 report is a major civil finding, but it should not be described as a jury verdict or criminal judgment (S3–S5, S16).
- Settlement figures vary across later reporting. Until the docket and settlement papers are inspected, keep the amount approximate or omit it from any final narration (S11, lines 299–317; S14, lines 262–263).

## Source and measurement conflicts

| Issue | Competing evidence | Safe interim treatment |
|---|---|---|
| “Explosion” versus “rupture” | Contemporary headlines used “explosion”; later engineering accounts prefer structural rupture, while defense experts argued for an internal blast. | Say “tank failure/rupture”; explain the period “explosion” label when relevant. |
| Exact time | Fire report gives alarms at 12:40 p.m.; other accounts give about 12:30 or 12:45. | Use “around 12:40 p.m.” and cite the fire report, or “shortly before 1 p.m.” |
| Death dates | The Archives index includes 15, 17, 19, 20 January and 11 December certificate dates. | Use 21 total deaths; verify delayed deaths individually. |
| Injured count | Later institutional sources say about 150; early newspaper counts were provisional. | Say “many more were injured” or “about 150, according to the municipal summary.” |
| Settlement | Reports range from about $628,000 to higher rounded totals. | Do not use a precise total until the settlement accounting is found. |
| Wave height/speed | Contemporary and later sources give varying heights and model-based speeds. | Omit exact height/speed from the evidence-bounded conclusion. |

## What would change the working answer

The current answer would need revision if the original court record or a primary technical report reveals: a recovered bomb or identified bomber; a final judicial judgment contradicting Ogden’s report; a contemporaneous inspection/maintenance record materially changing the structural evidence; or settlement papers showing a different legal disposition than the current archival summaries.
