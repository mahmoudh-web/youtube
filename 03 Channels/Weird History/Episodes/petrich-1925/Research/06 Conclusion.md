## Evidence-bounded answer

The strongest located record does not show a League-recognised “war over a dog.” It shows a Greco-Bulgarian frontier incident at Demir-Kapu/Petrich, competing official accounts of the first shooting, an escalation into troop movements and Greek occupation of Bulgarian frontier districts, and a Bulgarian appeal to the League on 22 October 1925. Briand’s intervention and the Council’s 26 October withdrawal mechanism stopped the immediate military escalation; British, French and Italian officers were sent to observe the withdrawal, and a Commission of Enquiry was appointed on 29 October.

The dog explanation should therefore be treated as a memorable later or side-specific account whose provenance is not yet established. The accessible official League summaries use the formal frontier-incident framing and do not mention a dog. That absence is not proof that no archival file contains the story, which is why the original Commission report, Council minutes and diplomatic cables remain a mandatory human check.

The settlement was not merely an order to stop firing. The Commission investigated the facts and responsibility, and the Council adopted its conclusions on 14 December. The official legal record preserves the finding that, by occupying part of Bulgarian territory, Greece violated the Covenant. Contemporary summaries report compensation to Bulgaria—often as 30 million leva, while later English-language accounts use approximately £45,000—and describe preventive frontier/conciliation measures. The exact operative language, currency, payment and implementation remain to be verified in the Official Journal and follow-up files.

## Provisional interpretation

The episode is best understood as an interwar collective-security crisis in which a small frontier exchange was amplified by unresolved Greek-Bulgarian hostility, competing military narratives and the political willingness to move troops before the League could examine responsibility. The dog story is narratively powerful because it reduces a complex border and diplomatic crisis to an absurd human mistake. The documents presently support the opposite editorial lesson: the League had to manage claims, troop withdrawal, investigation and reparations precisely because the trigger was contested and the consequences were not.

The League’s success was procedural and limited. It stopped the immediate advance, secured withdrawal, commissioned fact-finding and adopted a responsibility/compensation settlement. That does not prove that every local cause disappeared or that the settlement was a neutral verdict on all Greek and Bulgarian grievances.

## Safest provisional wording

> In October 1925, a disputed shooting on the Greek-Bulgarian frontier escalated into a Greek incursion and a League of Nations emergency. The League forced a withdrawal, investigated the incident and later adopted a settlement that placed responsibility for the occupation of Bulgarian territory on Greece. The famous stray-dog explanation survives as a contested story; the official record is more cautious and more complicated.

This wording is not approved narration. It is the current wording gate and remains subject to human verification.

## What the record does not yet prove

- That a Greek soldier definitely crossed the border while chasing a dog.
- That the first shot was fired by Bulgaria or Greece.
- That Captain Vassiliadis’ death occurred exactly as either government described.
- That Petrich town was captured, or that an attack on it was stopped at a specific hour.
- That the crisis was a formal declared war or exactly ten days long.
- That the correct compensation figure is £45,000, 30 million leva or another amount without reading the adopted decision and payment record.
- That any single casualty total is reliable.
- That Pangalos, Bulgarian authorities or local armed groups deliberately manufactured the incident.
