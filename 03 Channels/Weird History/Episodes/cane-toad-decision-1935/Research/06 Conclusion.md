## Provisional assessment

The cane-toad introduction is firmly documented, but the strongest version of the premise needs narrowing. Queensland’s sugar researchers did have an evidence base: overseas reports from Puerto Rico and Hawaii suggested that *Bufo marinus* could reduce some cane beetles. The period Queensland record also exposed the weakness in transferring that result: local greyback beetles behaved differently, and effectiveness depended on whether the beetles were accessible to a ground-feeding toad.

The current record identifies a BSES-led operation under assistant entomologist Reginald William Mungomery: a colony arrived from Hawaii in June 1935, was bred at Meringa near Gordonvale and produced an August release of about 2,400 young toads. What it does not yet establish is a complete written approval chain or an adequate Australian pre-release test of target-beetle control and environmental risk. Those are evidence gaps, not proof that no one investigated the proposal.

Warnings were not simply absent or ignored. Froggatt objected; the Commonwealth Health Department prohibited further distribution in late 1935; BSES continued to argue for wider release; and the ban was lifted in September 1936 after Queensland political and industry pressure and further information supplied to Health authorities. The later outcome was limited control of the intended beetles and a large invasive population whose effects on native predators are now documented by modern science.

## What the pack can currently establish

- The BSES existed to conduct sugar-industry research and had worked on cane-beetle problems for decades.
- Arthur Bell encountered Puerto Rican evidence in 1932; Mungomery studied the toad in Hawaii and imported about 100 animals to Queensland in June 1935.
- The first colony was bred at Meringa near Gordonvale, and about 2,400 young toads were released around Gordonvale in August 1935 in the NMA’s institutional account.
- The 1935 Queensland record acknowledged that Queensland greyback beetles did not regularly return to the ground like Puerto Rican beetles, making effectiveness dependent on further observation.
- Walter Froggatt warned in 1936 that the toad could become as serious a pest as the rabbit or cactus.
- The Commonwealth Health Department prohibited further distribution after the first releases; BSES’s February 1936 report hoped more investigation would reverse the decision.
- Official Queensland history dates the lifting of the ban to September 1936 after BSES supplied detailed information about toad excreta/feeding.
- Later Queensland government material says that by 1941 the toad provided only limited control of the intended beetles, while period reporting still recorded some target-beetle feeding.
- Modern peer-reviewed science identifies lethal toxic ingestion by susceptible native predators as the major documented impact pathway, with effects varying by species and place.
- The 1935 operation’s full authorisation chain, original quarantine/release files, ban instrument and reversal correspondence remain open.

## Safest evidence-bounded episode proposition

**Queensland’s sugar researchers released a toad backed by overseas success stories—but the period record already showed that the local beetles behaved differently, and the full approval chain and pre-release testing remain unclear. When a federal ban followed the first releases, Queensland pressure helped reverse it; the control failed while the toad spread.**

This proposition keeps the conflict—agricultural urgency, incomplete transfer of evidence, warnings, institutional reversal and unintended consequences—without assigning unsupported personal motives or pretending that 1935 officials possessed modern invasion science.
