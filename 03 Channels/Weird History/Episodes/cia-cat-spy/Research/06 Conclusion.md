## Evidence-bounded answer

The located record supports a real CIA research effort to turn a cat into a mobile listening platform. The CIA’s current account dates the exploration to 1964, says a cat received a microphone/transmitter/antenna system, and says the technology worked in a narrow sense. The surviving 1967 “Views on Trained Cats” memo says repeated checks showed cats could be trained to move short distances, but concludes that environmental and security conditions made the technique impractical for specialized intelligence needs.

The record does not securely support the full operational legend. The CIA says the program ended in 1967 without operational use. The famous taxi death comes from Victor Marchetti’s later account, is repeated in the 2001 declassification-era press, and is disputed by former CIA official Robert Wallace and by the CIA’s current public account, which says the former official later admitted the story was a joke. The exact target, final anatomical layout, program cost and animal’s fate remain unresolved in the open record.

Adjacent declassified documents show that the CIA continued broader animal research, including canine vehicles and feline laboratory testbeds, after the 1967 cat memo. That does not prove that Acoustic Kitty continued, received operational approval or yielded intelligence. It shows a wider institutional interest in animal systems, not one seamless super-project.

## Provisional interpretation

The strongest interpretation is that Acoustic Kitty became a useful case study in the gap between technical possibility and operational control. A cat could be made to carry listening hardware and could be conditioned for limited movement, but the real environment introduced behavior, security and maintenance problems that a laboratory demonstration could not solve. Secrecy then created an evidence vacuum in which a redacted memo, a former officer’s dark anecdote and later institutional corrections competed to define the story.

The episode should therefore be framed as “real project, unreliable punchline.” It is not a story about an imaginary CIA program, but neither is the taxi death a settled documentary fact.

## Safest provisional wording

> The CIA really did test a cat fitted with listening equipment. Its surviving 1967 memo says cats could be trained to move short distances, but the technique was not practical for specialized intelligence work; the CIA now says the program ended without operational use. The famous taxi death, exact cost and precise device layout belong to a contested afterlife of testimony and press retelling, not to a fully open mission record.

This wording is not approved narration. It is the current claim-ledger wording gate and remains subject to human verification.

## What the record does not yet prove

- That the cat completed an operational espionage mission.
- That it was killed by a taxi or bus during the first field test.
- That no cat survived or that the CIA’s current welfare account is independently confirmed.
- That the CIA spent a specific amount such as $10m, $15m, $20m or $25m on the project.
- That the Soviet embassy, Kremlin or a named Asian head of state was the confirmed target.
- That microphone, transmitter, battery, control and antenna components were installed in exactly the layout described by any one later account.
- That every 1967–69 animal-program document concerns Acoustic Kitty rather than a separate canine, dolphin, bird or laboratory effort.
- That a formal congressional, inspector-general or intelligence-committee investigation of the cat program did or did not occur.
- That the CIA’s 1979-joke account can be independently verified from the open record.
