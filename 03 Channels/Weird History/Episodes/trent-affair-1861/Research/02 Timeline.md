## Reading guide

Dates are separated from interpretation. “Contemporary record” means the event or position appears in a document made during the crisis. “Later institutional account” means a modern repository or government history describing the record. The most secure dates are the 8 November interception, 26 December Seward note and early-January release. The exact timing and size of British military preparations, and whether 1 or 3 January should be called the release date, require primary record checks.

## Timeline conclusions

1. The crisis moved through four distinct stages: Confederate diplomatic escape; unilateral naval seizure; British legal/military pressure; and a carefully worded release that preserved an American legal argument while satisfying Britain’s demand for custody.
2. The legal issue was not one question. It included the right to stop and search a neutral mail packet, whether envoys or dispatches were contraband, whether the *Trent* could be taken to a prize court, whether the passengers could be removed, and what constituted adequate reparation.
3. “Nearly started a war” is a defensible provisional description of the danger, not evidence that war was inevitable. Britain prepared for armed contingencies and debated the measures; both governments also preserved routes to settlement.
