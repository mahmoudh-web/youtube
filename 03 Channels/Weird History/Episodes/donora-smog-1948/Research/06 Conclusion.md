## Evidence-bounded answer

The located record supports a severe late-October 1948 air-pollution episode in Donora and nearby Webster, Pennsylvania. The PHS investigation counted 20 deaths during or shortly after the smog and 5,910 people affected to some degree—42.7% of the study-area population. The illness was dominated by respiratory and mucous-membrane irritation, and severity increased with age; pre-existing cardiorespiratory disease was prominent among the fatalities (S1; S3, lines 6145–6194).

The mechanism is best understood as an interaction. The Monongahela valley’s terrain constrained air movement; humidity and fog were present; low wind and strong atmospheric stability persisted; and active sources continued to emit pollutants. The PHS atmospheric summary identified the zinc smelters and steel operations as major contributors for different pollutant categories, while also identifying domestic heating, locomotives and steamboats as significant contributors. The report concluded that no single substance was responsible and recommended emission reductions plus weather forecasting to warn communities (S3, lines 6242–6370).

That conclusion matters because later retellings often ask for one culprit. The evidence does not support assigning the whole event to hydrogen fluoride, sulfur dioxide, the zinc works, American Steel and Wire, or the inversion alone. It supports a systems explanation in which ordinary emissions became acutely dangerous when the atmosphere stopped dispersing them. The exact date window, death-count scope beyond the PHS total, and the state reports’ detailed source apportionment still require primary-record review.

## What changed afterward

The event entered policy discussion quickly. A resident’s 31 October letter to Governor James Duff demanded smoke control and blamed the zinc works; Pennsylvania’s official historical record says the state established a Division of Air Pollution Control in 1949. Truman’s May 1950 message to the U.S. Technical Conference on Air Pollution explicitly cited Donora as an exceptional case in which contaminants shortened human life and called for research and local laws (S5; S10). EPA’s later legal history places the federal research and control sequence in 1955, 1963, 1967 and 1970 (S9).

This is evidence of influence and public-health learning, not a proof that Donora alone caused the Clean Air Act or EPA. The safest historical claim is that Donora became a landmark example that helped move air pollution from a local nuisance debate into a national research and policy conversation.

## Long-term question

The 1957/1961 follow-up matters because it asked what happened to members of the original study population after the acute event. Later summaries report associations between having been affected by the fog and subsequent cardiovascular or cancer outcomes, but these are observational findings with possible confounding from pre-existing illness, age, smoking, housing, employment and continuing exposure (S11–S13). They must not be added to the 1948 acute death toll or converted into claims about a particular person’s cause of death.

## Safest provisional wording

> In late October 1948, a thick smog settled over Donora and nearby Webster in Pennsylvania’s Monongahela Valley. The U.S. Public Health Service later counted 20 deaths and 5,910 people affected. Its finding was more complicated than a single poison: industrial and community emissions accumulated near the ground during several days of unusual atmospheric stability. Donora became a landmark in the growing argument that air pollution was a public-health problem—not because one company was found legally liable or one chemical was proven responsible, but because the episode showed what routine emissions could do when the weather stopped them from dispersing.

This is not approved narration. It is the current wording gate, subject to inspection of the original PHS and Pennsylvania records.

## What the record does not yet prove

- That 20, 21 or 22 is the only valid death count without defining geography and time window.
- That the event began on exactly 25, 26 or 27 October without the daily observations and contemporary chronology.
- That one chemical—especially hydrogen fluoride or sulfur dioxide—caused every illness or death.
- That American Steel and Wire or the zinc works was legally found solely responsible.
- That a 1951 settlement, if confirmed, admitted liability or exonerated the defendants.
- That later cardiovascular/cancer associations prove the 1948 smog caused an individual later death.
- That Donora single-handedly caused the 1955, 1963 or 1970 federal statutes.
- That “clean air started here” is a sufficient substitute for the documented legislative sequence.
