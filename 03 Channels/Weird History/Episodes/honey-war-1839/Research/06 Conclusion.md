## Provisional answer to the research question

The colourful story is partly real and partly compressed memory.

The contemporary record securely supports a serious jurisdictional crisis. Missouri’s 1837 Brown survey and 1839 boundary legislation claimed a line north of the Sullivan line; Iowa’s governor answered with proclamations asserting territorial and U.S. authority; Missouri’s governor issued a counter-proclamation ordering enforcement and militia readiness; a Missouri sheriff attempted tax collection and was later arrested; and both governments prepared armed forces. President Van Buren’s 24 December 1839 message confirms that the disagreement had produced intense excitement and merited federal attention.

The bee-tree story is plausible and repeatedly attested, but this source pass cannot yet treat every colourful detail as eyewitness fact. Later accounts disagree on three versus four trees. The 1924 *Palimpsest* article reports that a Palmyra Whig poem about the incident appeared on 26 December 1839, but the original newspaper and the county court record have not been inspected. The federal 1839 message—our strongest located contemporary file—does not mention bees. The safe conclusion is therefore that bee trees became the durable local emblem/name of a conflict whose documented cause was boundary jurisdiction and tax enforcement.

The “war” label is also larger than the demonstrated combat. The sources show official threats, civil arrests, militia orders, assemblies and a December stand-off. They do not establish a conventional battle in this pass. “Bloodless” is a later shorthand, not a complete casualty audit. The episode should say that no battle is documented in the current source pass and that later histories call it bloodless, while leaving open whether any minor injury or unrecorded confrontation occurred.

Finally, the legal ending should be told in two stages. In 1849 the Supreme Court upheld the practical Sullivan line as the boundary. In 1850–51 commissioners resurveyed and marked it with monuments, and the Court recorded/accepted that work. A clean “Iowa won” ending is less precise than the court’s actual resolution of a boundary question that had outlived the 1839 mobilisation.

## Recommended editorial angle

> A border could be legally uncertain for decades; a tax collector, a few bee trees and two angry governors turned that uncertainty into a militia crisis.

The episode’s original value is the gap between the comic title and the serious machinery underneath: survey language, treaty-era boundary references, state versus territorial authority, taxation, slavery/free-territory context and federal judicial settlement. The bee-tree anecdote is the hook, not the whole explanation.

## Narration gate

No script is approved. The following paragraph is the furthest wording that can safely advance to human review:

> In 1839, Missouri and Iowa Territory were not arguing over a neat line on a modern map. They were arguing over which old survey and which reading of Missouri’s boundary language controlled. Missouri officials tried to collect taxes north of Iowa’s recognised line; Iowa officials treated that as foreign jurisdiction. Proclamations, an arrest and militia orders followed. Later accounts say bee trees were cut in the disputed strip, giving the crisis its memorable name—but they disagree on the count, and the original local records still need checking. No battle is documented in this source pass. The Supreme Court eventually upheld the practical Sullivan line in 1849, with the boundary marked in 1850–51.
