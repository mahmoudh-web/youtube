- [ ] Inspect the 18 April 1960 *Straits Times* page. Determine whether the “no more trouble with rats and mice” line is a telegram, a reporter’s paraphrase or an editorial success claim.
- [ ] Obtain the complete 1959 *Colony of North Borneo Annual Report* scan, verify the page number and wording, and explicitly mark that its rats-and-cats observation concerns Sabah/North Borneo rather than Sarawak.
- [ ] Read the full de Zulueta/LaChance WHO paper and the 1962 Sarawak annual-report scan. Record what they do and do not say about domestic animals, rats, roof damage, insecticide changes and Bario.
- [ ] Obtain the full M.J. Colbourne, W.H. Huehne and F. de S. LaChance, “The Sarawak Anti-Malaria Project,” *Sarawak Museum Journal* 9 (1960): 215–248. Search for Bario, Kelabit, rats, cats, DDT, dieldrin and supply drops; do not infer silence from a secondary citation.
- [ ] Request or inspect Sarawak State Library file DOL/12/1, “Medical & Health — Sarawak Malaria Pilot Project” (1956–1959), and any adjacent 1960 malaria-eradication files. Record catalogue identifiers and access restrictions.
- [ ] Locate Tom Harrisson’s original 1965 two-page account and any cited correspondence. Resolve whether “1965” is a date error, a separate delivery or a memory of the 1960 event. Preserve the original wording and publication context.

- [ ] Seek veterinary, toxicology or public-health records that identify cat deaths in Sarawak. Do not infer DDT biomagnification from the existence of DDT spraying or from cat deaths elsewhere.
- [ ] Verify all geographic names and spelling: Bario, Kelabit Highlands, Sarawak, North Borneo/Sabah, Kuching, Changi, Jesselton/Kota Kinabalu, and the names McSporran/MacSporran and LaChance.

## Legal, ethical and cultural sensitivity

- The communities involved were living in a colonial administrative system. Do not frame the Kelabit people as passive recipients of a whimsical British rescue; identify their crops, homes, requests and local knowledge where the sources support them.
- Avoid “the Dayaks” as an undifferentiated label. Use the specific community and place named by a verified source, and check historical terminology against local or scholarly guidance.
- Do not imply that WHO, RAF personnel or named local officers caused harm unless a record establishes responsibility. This is primarily a historical attribution risk rather than a current-person defamation case, but careless blame remains misleading.
- Do not turn DDT into a universal “poison” story detached from context. The Sarawak medical evidence documents substantial malaria-control results; the ecological harms are source-specific, disputed and temporally/geographically mixed.
- Animal welfare matters. Avoid comic language about terrified or injured cats, graphic imagined survival scenes, or unsupported claims that all cats survived or died.
- The episode concerns colonial health policy, indigenous communities, environmental effects and public-health trade-offs. Human editorial review should check whether the framing preserves local agency and does not reduce people to a setup for a punchline.
