## Provisional assessment

The source record supports a remarkable but legally bounded answer: the reserve was breached through sustained access to a rented warehouse, where syrup was extracted in repeated loads, substituted with water or inferior syrup, and routed into resale channels. The Supreme Court’s account identifies Caron with warehouse operations and Vallières with receiving/reselling the syrup and handling the money. Vallières’s own jury convictions, admissions recorded by the Court and final appellate disposition provide the clearest responsibility record currently located.

The central number for the adjudicated case is **9,571 barrels**, or **5,935,250 pounds**, with a court-record value of approximately **$17,805,750**. Early Federation and news estimates were higher or rounded differently. The final legal coda is not a $1m fine: the Supreme Court’s unanimous 2022 judgment restored a **$9,171,397.57 fine-in-lieu**, payable over ten years, with six additional years’ imprisonment on default.

## What the pack can currently establish

- The Federation-managed reserve was designed to buffer poor harvests, supply markets and reduce price instability; its own 2012 records describe the reserve’s purpose and scale.
- The warehouse held 16,224 barrels in July 2011 according to the respondent’s court filing; 9,571 were later accounted for as emptied in the criminal case.
- The Supreme Court describes a year-long extraction and substitution process using trucks, tankers and pumps, followed by resale.
- Richard Vallières was convicted in 2016 of theft, fraud and trafficking in property obtained by crime.
- The Quebec Court of Appeal reduced the fine-in-lieu amount in 2020, but the Supreme Court reversed that legal result in 2022.
- Police and producer records document a large multi-jurisdictional investigation, but arrest, accused and recovery figures must remain dated and attributed.

## Editorial interpretation, clearly labelled

The episode’s strongest original question is not simply “who stole the syrup?” but how a commodity became legible as a strategic reserve, how a warehouse routine allowed a repeated substitution scheme to persist, and how a court translated a distributed flow of goods and money into individual legal responsibility. That is an interpretation from the reserve records and judgments, not a court’s statement of motive or a licence to call the whole industry corrupt.
