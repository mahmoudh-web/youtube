## Counterevidence register

### “Churchill ordered a secret attack” versus an incomplete authorisation chain

The PBS chronology reports a 2 January 1942 Defence Committee go-ahead for production, and later PBS material attributes a separate anthrax-bomb order to Churchill in March 1944. Those are meaningful institutional leads, but they do not establish that Churchill personally signed or authorised every component of Operation Vegetarian, nor that a deployment order was ever issued. The underlying memo, Defence Committee minutes, War Cabinet/Porton committee papers and any cancellation record must be checked.

Use: “The public chronology records institutional approval to produce a retaliatory capability; the exact chain of authority remains a human-verification item.”

### “Britain used anthrax in Germany” versus preparation without deployment

The accessible institutional and review sources agree that the cattle-feed plan was never implemented. The Guardian also reports that neither side used biological weapons in the war. Gruinard proves that Britain conducted domestic field trials; it does not prove German deployment. Production, testing and use are three separate claims.

Use: “Prepared and tested in Britain; never used against Germany.”

### “The plan would have killed millions” versus unsupported counterfactuals

The plan was intended to damage livestock and agriculture, and its designers considered retaliation and disruption. The located sources do not provide a verified model of human deaths, the effect of food distribution, or the military consequences of use. Popular estimates are counterfactuals and often merge cattle mortality, human infection and famine into one certainty.

Use: describe intended target and strategic rationale only. Do not narrate a death toll that did not occur.

### “Germany had already used biological weapons” versus a weakly evidenced premise

The WOAH review describes Operation Vegetarian as a response to suspected German foot-and-mouth-disease weapons but explicitly says evidence was limited. The Guardian’s report says the committee discussed retaliation and defensive necessity. Neither source clears a claim that Germany had used FMD or anthrax. The plan’s existence is secure; the enemy-use premise is not.

Use: “British planners prepared for a possible biological attack and retaliation,” not “Germany attacked Britain biologically.”

### “Gruinard was the weapon” versus a related test site

Gruinard was a test site for anthrax weapon concepts in 1942–43. The public record does not establish that the exact cattle-feed plan was trialled there in the same form, and the IWM film catalogue describes a wider field-trial programme. Keep the island’s role as experimental and evidential, not as a synonym for Operation Vegetarian.

Use: “Gruinard tested related anthrax delivery concepts within the wider Porton Down programme.”

### “The cleanup failed” versus a staged official remediation record

The 1983 Hansard answer shows that methods, effectiveness, cost and ecological effects were still under review. The 1987 answer says an independent advisory group was convinced the treatment had worked well but required an agricultural-use demonstration. The 2022 parliamentary answer records a later safety declaration and return to the original owner’s estate. The sequence shows caution and verification, not a simple failure or a magical one-day fix.

Use: “The island was subject to prolonged review, decontamination and follow-up before public confirmation of safety.”

### “Safe” equals “no historical risk” versus an administrative conclusion

“Declared safe” is the wording of the modern government answer. It does not mean the island was never contaminated, that every historical uncertainty is solved, or that the public should be given an operational access guide. The island’s ownership and safety status are distinct from the ethics of the wartime experiments.

Use: quote the official conclusion with date and source, and keep the historical contamination visible.

## Material items still needing verification

- The 7 December 1941 memo and 2 January 1942 Defence Committee minutes cited by the PBS chronology.
- War Cabinet/Porton experiments sub-committee minutes and appendices reported as released in 2010; search The National Archives by the committee name, Porton Down, Operation Vegetarian, anthrax and cattle cakes.
- The exact file path for the cattle-cake production and disposal records, including whether the five-million figure is exact or rounded.
- The exact cancellation/abandonment decision and whether it was recorded as a seasonal decision, a strategic reprioritisation after Normandy, or simply non-use after the war changed.
- Gruinard’s 1942–43 trial reports, the independent advisory group’s full 1987–90 evidence and any environmental monitoring records.
- The precise relationship, if any, between the cattle-feed plan and separate anthrax-bomb orders in 1944.
- Rights and sensitivity clearance for the IWM film, photographs, maps and any archival documents.
