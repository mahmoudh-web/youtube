## Perspective and evidence controls

- **Union institutional view:** S1–S3 and Seward’s response foreground belligerent rights, Confederate treason/rebellion and the procedural error of not taking the *Trent* to a prize court.
- **British institutional view:** S4–S8 foreground neutral protection, the British flag, maritime law, national honour and the need to demonstrate credible force.
- **Confederate objective:** S1–S2 show that the envoys’ diplomatic mission was part of a campaign for recognition, credit and commercial legitimacy; do not treat their cause as politically neutral.
- **Public and press reception:** S2, S7, S14–S16 show celebration, rumours, hostility, moderation and changing opinion. A newspaper editorial is evidence of reception, not proof of government policy.
- **Canadian dimension:** The troop movement made the crisis geographically broader, but Canada’s own agency, militia capacity and colonial politics need separate research before a narrative assigns a single British motive.

## What would change the conclusion?

1. A primary Navy record could change the account of warning shots, boarding resistance, papers taken, precise location or Wilkes’s authority.
2. The complete 1861 correspondence could show that the public summaries omit a material condition, deadline or threat.
3. War Office/Admiralty files could materially change the scale, speed or purpose of Canadian and naval preparations.
4. Lincoln Cabinet records could change the relative roles of Lincoln, Seward, Welles, Sumner and other advisers.
5. The original legal opinions could reveal a stronger or weaker basis for either side than the printed summaries suggest.
6. Verified contemporary newspaper sampling could alter the current cautious description of American celebration and British anger.
