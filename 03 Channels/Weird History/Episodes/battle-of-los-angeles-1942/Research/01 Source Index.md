## Research question

How did a real wartime blackout and anti-aircraft response develop over Los Angeles on 24–25 February 1942, what did officials and witnesses say they saw, and how did an unconfirmed target become a “battle”?

## Working answer and scope

The record securely establishes a large defensive response: reports and a radar contact were followed by a blackout, searchlights, and roughly 1,400–1,430 rounds of anti-aircraft fire. No enemy aircraft was confirmed or shot down, and no bombs were dropped. The Army and Navy did not initially agree about the target: the Navy called the event a false alarm, while Army reporting allowed that one to five unidentified aircraft may have been involved and Secretary of War Henry Stimson publicly discussed as many as fifteen. A later antiaircraft account identified a meteorological balloon as the initiating object and described a chain reaction in which firing and searchlights produced additional perceived targets. That explanation is plausible and institutionally repeated, but the original radar, command, and firing records still need inspection.

This pack covers the event, its immediate information conflict, the preceding Japanese submarine shelling and Executive Order 9066 context, and the later UFO reclassification. It does not treat later alien claims as evidence and does not draft narration.

## Source-quality key

- **A — primary/contemporary or official record:** 1942 newspaper scans, Executive Order 9066, official government history, and the Navy’s official historical account of the Ellwood shelling.
- **B — authoritative institutional synthesis:** U.S. Space Force oral history, Smithsonian history article, and official-history access copies.
- **C — retrospective military testimony:** 1949 *Antiaircraft Journal* recollection by a participant in the inquiry; valuable but memory-based.
- **L — archive lead, not inspected evidence:** listed Western Defense Command/Fourth Antiaircraft Command histories and the Marshall-to-Roosevelt memorandum.

## Archive leads requiring human retrieval

- *History of the Western Defense Command, 17 March 1941–30 September 1945*, especially the G-2 chronology of enemy operations and the cited February 1942 appendices.
- *History of the Fourth Antiaircraft Command, 9 January 1942–1 July 1945*, cited by the official Army history at pp. 87–88.
- George C. Marshall memorandum to Franklin D. Roosevelt, 26 February 1942, cited in the official Army history as “Memo, CofS for President Roosevelt, 26 Feb 42, OCS 21347-86.”
- 37th Coast Artillery Brigade firing logs, Aircraft Warning Service/radar records, and local coroner or police records for deaths and shell damage.

## Source-handling rules

1. Call the event an anti-aircraft barrage or air-raid alert; reserve “battle” for the qualified episode title.
2. Keep the Army’s “one to five” and Stimson’s “as many as fifteen” as separate statements with separate sources.
3. Never turn “reported,” “unidentified,” or “probably” into “confirmed.”
4. Attribute the balloon/war-nerves explanation to the 1949 retrospective and later institutional histories.
5. Treat “enemy agents,” Japanese signaling, and extraterrestrial explanations as allegations or later interpretations, not facts.
6. No visual asset is approved for production by this research pack.
