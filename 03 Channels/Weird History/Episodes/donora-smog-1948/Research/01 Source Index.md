## Research question

How did ordinary industrial emissions become an acute health disaster in Donora in October 1948, what did investigators actually establish about the mechanism and responsibility, and how did the episode influence later air-pollution policy?

## Working answer and scope

The strongest located record supports a five-day episode in which industrial and community emissions accumulated in a river valley during unusually stable, stagnant weather with fog and an inversion. The 1949 U.S. Public Health Service (PHS) report counted 20 deaths during or shortly after the smog and 5,910 people affected to some degree, but concluded that no single substance was responsible. Its source analysis identified major contributions from the zinc smelters, steel-plant operations, domestic heating, and transport sources, while its meteorological analysis identified prolonged stability and stagnation as necessary to the accumulation.

The episode is therefore best framed as a systems failure: routine emissions, vulnerable residents, a constrained valley, and a delayed recognition of the hazard interacted. Pennsylvania records show an immediate demand for “smoke control,” a state air-pollution division in 1949, and later state and federal legislation. The evidence does not justify saying Donora alone caused the Clean Air Act, that one company was legally found liable, or that one chemical has been proven to account for every death.

## Source-quality key

- **A — primary or official record:** original study/report, government archive, presidential message, statute/history page, or official contemporary record.
- **A/D — primary underlying item accessed through a reproduction or OCR:** useful for discovery and provisional claims; inspect the original scan before quotation.
- **B — institutional or peer-reviewed synthesis:** useful for triangulation and framing; not a substitute for the primary record on disputed details.
- **C — reputable retrospective reporting or local history:** useful for reception, memory, and leads; not sufficient alone for causal or legal claims.

## Source-handling rules

1. Use “the PHS report counted…” for the 20 and 5,910 figures; do not write an unqualified “22 people died” or “7,000 were sickened.”
2. Use “the report found multiple contributors” rather than “the zinc works alone caused the deaths.”
3. Separate the acute episode from the 1957/1961 follow-up; association with later mortality is not proof of a specific individual death’s cause.
4. Attribute Bainbridge’s accusation and later industrial criticism to the source making it. Do not convert community testimony into an adjudicated finding.
5. Describe Donora as an important influence on later air-pollution research and policy discussion. Do not say it single-handedly caused the 1955, 1963, or 1970 statutes.
6. No image, scan, newspaper page, or archival photograph is cleared for reuse by this source index.
