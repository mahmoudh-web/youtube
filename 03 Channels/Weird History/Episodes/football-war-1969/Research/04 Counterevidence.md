## 1. “A football match started the war” versus football as accelerant

The familiar story has a real chronological hook: the qualifiers, crowd violence and anti-national rhetoric preceded the July fighting. US diplomatic reporting in S4–S5 explicitly links the immediate crisis narrative to football and riots. FIFA’s later English and Spanish retrospectives (S11), however, state that the war followed “for reasons outside football.” The 2025 process-tracing study (S17) argues that the matches did not start the chain or cause the decision to use force.

The strongest bounded conclusion is not “football was irrelevant.” The matches created a high-visibility national arena, gave newspapers emotionally legible symbols, and helped mobilise public pressure. The available evidence does not show that a match, crowd or football authority ordered or compelled either government to launch military action.

## 2. “The war was purely about land” versus a multi-causal crisis

Migration, demographic pressure, agrarian reform, labour competition and the unresolved border are repeatedly documented (S5, S13–S15). They should be treated as structural conditions, not as a single monocausal explanation. The Common Market, domestic legitimacy, military pressure, nationalist ideology, media framing and diplomatic failure also appear in the record.

Safer formulation: structural disputes made the relationship vulnerable; football and press campaigns accelerated the crisis; state actors made the decisions that produced hostilities. The relative weight of each factor is an interpretation, not a measurable fact in the present pack.

## 3. Salvadoran and Honduran state narratives conflict

The Salvadoran government framed the crisis around attacks, expulsions and the safety of Salvadoran emigrants; FRUS S5 records its “genocide” charge. Honduran complaints to the IACHR and OAS records frame Honduran visitors, sovereignty and later occupied towns as victims of Salvadoran violence/aggression. OAS and IACHR records preserve both governments’ allegations and also document rights violations in both countries.

Do not use “aggressor,” “genocide,” “legitimate defence,” “invasion,” or “ethnic cleansing” as narrator-owned conclusions unless a source is explicitly named and the legal status is explained. The word “genocide” in the 1969 diplomatic record is a political accusation, not a judicial finding in this pack.

## 4. The chronology has small but consequential discrepancies

- The second qualifier was played on 15 June; IACHR records violence on 14–16 June, while the US narrative begins its post-match account on 16 June. This likely reflects event versus aftermath framing, but the original records should be checked.
- The deciding match is listed as 26 June by RSSSF and related accounts, and 27 June by the Honduras football list. The result is stable; the date is not.
- “Four-day war,” “five-day war” and “100-hour war” use different counting conventions around 14–18 July and cease-fire/withdrawal phases.
- FRUS d644 reports attacks and notes some details are not confirmed. A report in a diplomatic cable is not identical to a verified operational log.

## 5. Casualty and displacement figures are not reconciled

The source landscape includes approximately 1,000–2,000 dead (Cable, S13), 1,900 dead and 12,000 wounded (Bar-On & Lewis, S17), and larger popular figures. Refugee figures likewise distinguish people already living in Honduras, people fleeing before hostilities, people displaced during the war and people crossing at particular border points. The FRUS estimate of approximately 300,000 Salvadorans in Honduras is a population estimate, not a refugee total.

The IACHR evidence is more useful for documented categories of harm—death, injury, property, residence, transit and detention—than for a final body count. No single count should be presented as settled until national records, OAS field records, humanitarian reports and the underlying scholarly references are compared.

## 6. Press influence is documented, but intent and reach are not uniform

The IACHR recommended that both governments stop press/radio propaganda that could induce persecution (S2–S3). S17 identifies inflammatory Honduran and Salvadoran headlines and also records calls for calm. Thus “the media caused the war” is as reductive as “football caused the war.” The safe claim is that press and radio narratives helped shape public hostility and constrained political room, with content and impact varying by outlet and audience.

## 7. The football label’s first use is unsettled

S17 cites a 28 June 1969 UPI headline and discusses the persistence of the “Soccer War” myth. Older accounts often attribute popularisation or coinage to Ryszard Kapuściński. Those are different claims: earliest use, popularisation, and later literary consolidation. Until the earliest newspaper and wire copy are checked, write “the label appeared in contemporary/soon-after reporting and was later popularised,” not “Kapuściński coined it.”

## 8. OAS mediation was central but not necessarily neutral or sole-causal

FRUS records the OAS cease-fire architecture and subsequent pressure (S6–S8); IACHR records a parallel human-rights role (S1–S3). Yet US documents are policy records with their own interests, and the OAS bodies excluded the two disputants from at least one council vote (S6). “The OAS stopped the war” is too simple; “OAS mechanisms constrained the fighting and organised a diplomatic exit” is better supported.

## 9. Later treaties must not be projected backward

The 1980 General Peace Treaty and 1992 ICJ judgment establish later legal and territorial processes (S19–S20). They support the claim that unresolved border questions outlasted the fighting, but they cannot be used as direct evidence of what decision-makers in July 1969 knew or intended.

## Editorial/legal-risk flags

- Civilian deaths, injuries, expulsions, property destruction and alleged atrocities are high-sensitivity claims; retain attribution and avoid invented precision.
- National labels can reproduce dehumanising propaganda. Quote historic language only when necessary, mark it as a period claim, and do not repeat slurs for colour.
- Military responsibility and “first attack” claims can be politically contested; pair every claim with the issuing record and competing account.
- Translation from Spanish to English should be checked by a fluent human reviewer, especially for legal terms such as *genocidio*, *agresión*, *expulsión*, *refugiado* and *alto el fuego*.
