## Narrowest defensible conclusion

The Great Stink of 1858 did not create London’s sewer project from nothing. The longer record begins at least with Chadwick’s 1842 sanitary report, continues through the 1847 Metropolitan Commission of Sewers and its compulsory drainage policy, and includes repeated interception proposals before Bazalgette prepared a plan in 1854. The 1855 Metropolis Management Act then created the Metropolitan Board of Works, gave it a statutory duty to prevent metropolitan sewage entering the Thames, and set a completion deadline—while also requiring plan approval that contributed to later delay.

In June and July 1858, heat, dryness, pollution, population growth, sewage expansion, and tide made the Thames’ smell politically unavoidable. Hansard and contemporary newspaper reprints show that the crisis reached Parliament and intensified pressure, but the debates also addressed outfall design, capacity, cost, finance, municipal authority, and public health. The smell was therefore a visibility shock and political accelerator: it helped turn a long-running engineering and administrative problem into urgent legislation.

The 2 August 1858 Metropolis Management Amendment Act ordered works to begin promptly, authorized up to £3 million borrowing with Treasury guarantee, imposed a 3d-in-the-pound main-drainage rate for 40 years, removed the prior plan-approval bottleneck, and extended the completion date to 1863. Construction began in 1859 and continued through later milestones. The Act enabled and financed the project; it did not complete the sewer system in eighteen days.

The safest wording is therefore **“the Great Stink accelerated political authorization of a sewer project already under development.”** Do not say that smell alone created the system, that Parliament only cared when its own members suffered, or that odour caused cholera. Those statements fail to preserve the earlier public-health record, the poor and riverside experience, the technical disputes, and the distinction between Victorian medical belief and modern causal evidence.

## Confidence summary

- **High:** Chadwick’s 1842 report exists and addresses sanitary conditions; the MCS and MBW predate 1858; the 1855 Act created a statutory main-drainage duty; Bazalgette’s engineering work predates the Great Stink; the 1858 Act was dated 2 August and changed authority, finance, and procedure; works began in 1859.
- **High with attribution:** Faraday’s 1855 observation; parliamentary descriptions of smell, dry weather, expanded drainage, poverty, and health fear; the July debate over Barking and Sea Reach; the wording and financial provisions of the 1858 Act.
- **Medium:** the exact first use and duration of “Great Stink”; the precise meteorological/hydrological mechanism; the 1847 predecessor-commission count; the exact status and cost of each pre-1858 Bazalgette plan; the completion milestone selected for the “finished” system.
- **Low / unresolved:** any claim that smell itself caused disease; any modern medical diagnosis from miasma-era language; a claim that Parliament’s action was solely or directly caused by its own discomfort; an allegation of negligence or criminal responsibility without legal review; an assertion that the system purified all sewage rather than moved it to controlled downstream outfalls.

## Legal and ethical boundary

This pack contains no allegation about a living person. It does contain historically sensitive institutional attributions. Political speeches must remain attributed; period public-health claims must not be presented as current medical fact; and terms such as “negligence,” “cover-up,” “criminal pollution,” or “government failure” require direct evidence and human legal review. The account should not turn the suffering of poorer riverside residents into a punchline about parliamentary noses.
