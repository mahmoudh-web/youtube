## Evidence-bounded answer

On 15 January 1919, a large molasses tank on Boston’s Commercial Street failed and released a destructive wave through the North End. The official Massachusetts Archives collection provides a 21-record casualty anchor. Contemporary fire and newspaper records establish the disaster and response, while engineering reports describe a tank whose plates, rivets, inspection and safety margin were contested.

USIA’s sabotage theory belongs in the episode as an allegation made by the defendant: the company argued that anarchists had planted an explosive. The located record does not establish that a bomb was found, that an anarchist planted one, or that sabotage caused the failure. The February 1919 grand jury’s no-indictment result is a criminal-procedure fact, not a finding that the company was innocent or that the bomb theory was true.

The later civil proceeding is the legal hinge. Hugh W. Ogden was appointed as an auditor to hear the consolidated Suffolk County claims and report to the Superior Court. His 28 April 1925 report rejected the sabotage explanation and assigned liability to USIA on structural and safety grounds. That is a court-appointed auditor’s civil finding, not a jury verdict or criminal conviction. The claims were settled afterward; the exact final amount needs primary settlement-record verification before it is stated as fact.

## Working interpretation

The best-supported interpretation is not “anarchists blew up a tank” and not a fully solved single-mechanism engineering story. It is that USIA’s defense tried to externalize responsibility, while the evidence assembled in the civil proceeding pointed to an unsafe structure and management decisions that left little margin for failure. Spofford’s 1919 report and later structural/metallurgical analysis support that direction, but the precise interaction of stress, rivets, steel, temperature and possible gas pressure remains source-attributed rather than finally resolved here.

## Safe editorial proposition

The research supports an episode about how a bizarre industrial disaster became a test of evidence and corporate responsibility: a company’s sabotage allegation met a criminal inquiry that produced no indictment, a multi-year civil record, and an auditor’s finding that the tank’s structural weakness—not a proven anarchist bomb—made USIA liable. This is a research proposition, not narration or a production brief.
