## Executive verdict

**FAIL for approval or script handoff; pass only as a clearly caveated pack for human verification.**

The central event is well supported: on 17 October 1814 a Meux vat ruptured, beer and debris damaged nearby buildings, and the contemporary inquest reporting names eight dead. The pack is unusually good at preserving uncertainty and counterevidence. It nevertheless cannot be approved as narration-ready because its load-bearing evidence is mostly a later transcription of newspaper material rather than the original newspaper scans, the quantities and some victim details conflict, and the later ninth-death account is only an institutional/retrospective claim. The human verification gate must remain open.

## Source quality and coverage

### Strong evidence

- The 1814 *Gentleman's Magazine* entry is a near-contemporary digest and independently gives the date, 3,555-barrel figure, affected streets, structural damage, eight deaths, injured survivors, collections, and estimated brewery loss. The Google Books record confirms the 1814 volume, but the event text was checked in the later searchable transcription rather than the page image.
- The Rootes Journal reproduces a dated *Morning Post* inquest report and provides a useful page locator, names, ages, coroner, jury view, and verdict. It is a later host, not the original newspaper scan. Its “Notes” after the jury-view paragraph are modern editorial material and must not be cited as 1814 testimony.
- Cornell and Starmans preserve overlapping versions of the inquest testimony. Their agreement on the core mechanics, witnesses, victims, and verdict is useful cross-checking, but both remain later transcriptions. They are not substitutes for the original newspaper image.
- Crymble's peer-reviewed urban-history study is the best source in the pack for bounded geography and the danger of treating “Irish slum” as a homogeneous description. It is contextual evidence, not proof of each victim's identity or exact address.

### Weak, indirect, or inaccessible evidence

- The BNA feature was not directly retrievable during this pass. Its modern litre conversion therefore remains a reported secondary figure, not independently verified evidence.
- The Brewery History Society PDF timed out in direct retrieval. Its 7,664-barrel figure was visible in search indexing, but the underlying cited source and page image still need inspection.
- Historic England is authoritative for its current image record, but its nine-death/alcohol-poisoning summary is a later institutional claim and conflicts with the contemporary-era eight-person inquest account.
- Wikidata is a catalogue/knowledge record, not the Act itself. The Google Books statute volume was confirmed bibliographically, but the operative Act text and any exact allowance amount were not read.
- The National Archives direct page returned an access error in this pass, although its indexed official text confirms that most post-mid-eighteenth-century inquest records are held locally and that newspapers may be the only surviving account. The [official guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/coroners-inquests/) should still be used for the archive search plan. The London Archives page confirms that a coroners' inquests research guide exists, but does not itself establish that this particular file survives.

## Unsupported, overstated, contradictory, or missing-source claims

1. **Not independently established:** a single total of 1.5 million litres or over 320,000 gallons. The pack correctly warns against this, but the BNA and Historic England figures should not be narrated as equivalent.
2. **Not established:** the hoop's mass. The transcription reads “700 cwt” or “about seven hundred weight,” while popular retellings say 700 pounds. The pack correctly withholds it; retain that restriction.
3. **Not established:** a modern engineering diagnosis such as hydrostatic pressure, fermentation pressure, domino effect, blast, tidal wave, or tsunami. The period evidence supports rupture, reported force/pressure damage, slipped rivets as Crick's supposition, and masonry failure.
4. **Contradictory:** Hannah Banfield's mother's fate. One reproduced account says hospitalisation; another report is said to say both mother and daughter died. Resolve or qualify.
5. **Contradictory in detail:** Catherine/Catharine Butler's spelling and age (63 versus 65), plus OCR variation in Eleanor Cooper's recovery time.
6. **Unsupported as established fact:** the ninth alcohol-poisoning death, mass drunkenness, hospital riot, body-viewing-floor collapse, and “free beer” rescue obstruction. Cornell explicitly reports that the contemporary newspapers he reviewed do not support those stories; Historic England repeats only the ninth-death tradition.
7. **Missing primary check:** the original *Morning Post* scan and the other period issues listed in the pack (*Times*, *Morning Chronicle*, *Evening Mail*, *London Courier*, *Caledonian Mercury*, and *Kentish Gazette*).
8. **Missing archival check:** any Middlesex coroner's return, inquest paper, depositions, jury-view record, Quarter Sessions filing, workhouse record, or death/burial entry.
9. **Missing map check:** a period map placing the brewery, Banbury/Bainbridge Street, New Street, George Street, Great Russell Street, the Tavistock Arms, and the numbered houses.
10. **Missing legal check:** the operative text and legal context of 55 Geo. 3 c. 189, and specialist interpretation of the 1814 accidental-misfortune verdict. The Act should not be used to imply compensation or legal clearance.

- Keep the Banfield mother discrepancy explicit; do not collapse the two accounts.
- Omit the hoop mass and modern failure diagnosis until an original scan and technical review settle them.
