## Provisional assessment

The evidence supports a powerful atmospheric explosion over the Siberian taiga on 30 June 1908. Witness reports describe a luminous body, heat, violent sound, shaking and air movement; later instrumental and field records support a very energetic event. Leonid Kulik’s 1927 expedition, following a 1921–1922 regional investigation that could not reach the site, documented a vast radial/butterfly-shaped forestfall but did not recover a proven large meteorite or identify a conventional crater.

The strongest current scientific fit is a **stony asteroid airburst**, with modern model summaries converging around 10–20 megatons released at roughly 10 km altitude and a body on the order of 50–80 m. That is a model-based leading explanation, not a recovered object or certain compositional identification. NASA’s historical synthesis explicitly preserves uncertainty between cometary and asteroidal origin because the material evidence does not discriminate decisively. Lake Cheko remains a published but contested fragment-crater proposal, not a settled answer.

## What the pack can currently establish

- The event date is 30 June 1908 Gregorian; many Russian records use 17 June old style.
- Multiple reports from different locations record light, heat, sound, shaking and air disturbance, but their times, directions and details are not identical.
- A July 1908 newspaper record contains a more credible remote description of a luminous body and explosion; a separate Filimonovo meteorite story was later characterised as partly fictitious.
- Kulik’s 1921–1922 work gathered regional testimony but did not reach the remote blast area; he reached the site in 1927 after a difficult journey.
- Field investigations recorded extensive forest destruction in a radial/butterfly pattern, with some stripped standing trees near the centre.
- Repeated searches did not produce a proven macroscopic fragment or a conventional main-site impact crater; this is not the same as proving that no extraterrestrial microparticles exist.
- Peer-reviewed and NASA workshop models favour atmospheric fragmentation of a weak stony body; exact size, energy, altitude and composition remain parameter estimates.
- Lake Cheko is the subject of a real scientific disagreement: one line of research proposes a fragment crater, another finds the morphology, vegetation and lack of impact material strongly against it.
- Exotic explanations appear in the historiography but have no comparable support in this source set and should not be presented as co-equal hypotheses.

## Safest research wording

> On 30 June 1908, a cosmic body exploded in the atmosphere above Siberia, flattening a vast area of taiga without leaving a confirmed ordinary crater. Witnesses supplied the first clues; Leonid Kulik’s delayed expeditions supplied the forest pattern. Modern models favour a stony asteroid airburst, but the exact object—and whether any fragment reached the ground—remains open.

This is a research wording gate, not approved narration. It avoids claiming that a meteorite struck the ground, that the body’s composition is proven, that a specific lake is the crater, or that the witness accounts form one consistent instrument record.

## Editorial interpretation, clearly labelled

The historical puzzle is not simply “where is the crater?” It is how a large atmospheric event can leave an unmistakable landscape signature while withholding the most satisfying physical object: a recoverable meteorite or crater. The absence of that simple object made room for competing models and later speculation, but the combined witness, forest and atmospheric evidence still strongly favours an airburst over a ground strike. The responsible conclusion is therefore asymmetric: the event itself is well established; its exact cosmic body is not.

This paragraph is an interpretation of the indexed evidence, not a quotation from a witness or scientist.
