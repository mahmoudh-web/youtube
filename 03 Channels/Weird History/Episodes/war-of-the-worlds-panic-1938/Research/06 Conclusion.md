## Provisional answer

Yes, some people were genuinely frightened, confused or moved to act after the 30 October 1938 broadcast. The FCC file, Trenton’s city-manager letter, the surviving listener correspondence and the broadcast’s realistic form make “nothing happened” untenable.

But the evidence does not justify the clean popular image of an entire nation falling into one uniform panic. The sources measure different things: headlines and anecdotes, local call loads, regulator complaints, self-selected letters, Cantril’s interviews and later surveys. Cantril’s famous estimate is historically important, but it is an attributed 1940 research claim, not a direct census of people who believed Martians had landed. Later scholarship also shows how the study became a simplified “mass panic” story in communication history.

The safest episode conclusion is therefore:

> A real and uneven reaction happened. The disputed part is how large it was—and how much the newspapers, the research design and later retellings turned local alarm into a national legend.

## What the pack can currently support

- The programme was a CBS/Mercury Theatre drama adapted by Howard Koch from H. G. Wells and performed/narrated by Orson Welles.
- Its simulated bulletins, interruptions, familiar New Jersey locations and authoritative voices made misinterpretation possible for some late or partial listeners.
- Local alarm and complaint are documented, including Morton’s Trenton letter and mixed FCC/fan-mail responses.
- Contemporary newspapers used a strong national-panic frame, including the *New York Times* headline “Radio Listeners in Panic, Taking War Drama as Fact.”
- Cantril’s study supplied the classic numerical estimate and a differentiated account of listener response, while later scholarship disputes the way that study has been remembered.
- The episode’s original value is the evidence problem: how a genuine but uneven media reaction became a durable story about mass influence.

## What the pack cannot yet support

- A settled national headcount of people who believed the broadcast was real.
- A claim that millions fled, attempted suicide, required medical treatment or were otherwise harmed without case-level verification.
- A claim that the newspapers deliberately fabricated the entire reaction or coordinated a campaign against radio.
- A claim that nobody panicked or that all reported fear was performative.
- A universal statement that listeners missed every disclaimer, or that the disclaimers protected every listener.
- A “first fake news story” superlative or a definitive claim about Welles’s private motive.

## Suggested editorial spine after approval

1. Open with the famous headline and ask what it actually measured.
2. Reconstruct the broadcast from the audio: a drama openly introduced as drama, then made unnervingly news-like through interruptions and eyewitness authority.
3. Move from the programme to Trenton, FCC letters and named listener records to establish real local alarm without claiming a national total.
4. Put the newspaper frame beside the counterevidence: late tuning, survey results, supportive letters and mixed reactions.
5. Introduce Cantril’s 1940 estimate, then explain its sample and category limits before comparing it with later scholarship.
6. End on the unresolved but defensible distinction: a real reaction occurred; the scale of “America’s panic” is a historical argument, not a settled fact.
