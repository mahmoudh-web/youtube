## Research question

Did the Great Stink of 1858 create London’s sewer project, or did it accelerate an older engineering and public-health campaign that had already produced plans, institutions, and statutory duties?

## Episode boundary

This pack covers the sanitary and engineering record from Chadwick’s 1842 report through the 1858 Metropolis Management Amendment Act and the start of construction in 1859. It tests the role of smell, parliamentary visibility, public-health argument, administrative deadlock, finance, and Bazalgette’s pre-existing plans. It does not draft narration, infer modern disease causation from Victorian miasma language, or treat later institutional summaries as substitutes for the original papers.

## Source-quality key

- **A — primary-era official or first-person source:** statute, Hansard debate, contemporary report, or Faraday’s published observation. It may contain advocacy, error, or period scientific assumptions.
- **B — contemporary source reproduced in a later host:** useful when the scan is unavailable, but the original issue or volume still needs checking.
- **C — scholarly/specialist secondary source:** useful for chronology and source criticism; not a substitute for a primary passage where one is available.
- **D — institutional/archive catalogue or research guide:** reliable for holdings, scope, and access routes; not sufficient alone for the historical event or causal claim.

## Key entities and evidence-led research opportunities

- **Edwin Chadwick:** Poor Law official and author of the 1842 sanitary report; his report is evidence of an earlier public-health programme, not a neutral modern epidemiological study.
- **Metropolitan Commission of Sewers:** the 1847–1855 body that expanded compulsory sewer connections and altered where household waste went; its success and unintended river pollution need to be described from Bazalgette’s retrospective account and surviving records.
- **Metropolitan Board of Works:** created by the 1855 Act, legally charged with main drainage, but constrained by plan approval, borrowing, and outfall disputes until 1858.
- **Joseph Bazalgette:** engineer associated with plans before and after the Great Stink; do not portray him as designing the entire system from nothing in July 1858.
- **Benjamin Hall, Palmerston, Disraeli, and other parliamentary speakers:** political actors whose speeches reveal arguments and pressure, not independent proof of technical or medical causation.
- **Michael Faraday and John Snow:** scientific observers whose 1855 river description and 1849 waterborne-cholera argument show that pollution and health were debated before the 1858 crisis.

## Source-handling rules

1. Keep **observed smell**, **period miasma explanations**, and **modern disease causation** in separate claim rows.
2. Treat the 1855 Act’s duty, the 1857 plan deadlock, and the 1858 Act’s financing and removal of vetoes as separate events; do not collapse them into “Parliament built the sewers after the stink.”
3. Treat Bazalgette’s 1865 account as valuable but interested retrospective evidence, especially for “30,000 cesspools,” “116 schemes,” commission chronology, and responsibility for delay.
4. When describing the 1858 Act, say **authorized and financed accelerated main-drainage works**. Do not say it completed the system.
5. Preserve the difference between Parliament’s experience and the longer burden on riverside and poorer communities. Parliamentary discomfort is evidence of political visibility, not the beginning of public suffering.
6. Never state that the smell itself caused cholera. Record the Victorian fear of miasma and the separate evidence for waterborne transmission as period interpretation and modern historical context.
