## Why counterevidence matters

The familiar version says “the BBC fooled Britain.” The surviving record supports a more precise account: the BBC broadcast a deliberate fiction, some respondents treated it as plausible, others recognised or questioned the joke, and the scale of belief is not measurable from the current pack. The items below prevent a dramatic summary from outrunning the evidence.

## Alternative readings that remain open

1. The intended target may have been television credulity generally, not only unfamiliarity with pasta. S4 says the team wanted viewers to exercise critical scepticism; this is a retrospective statement of intent, not a 1957 audience study.
2. The report may have worked more through production realism and Dimbleby’s authority than through widespread ignorance of spaghetti. The evidence supports this as a plausible interpretation, but cannot rank the causes.
3. “The nation” may describe the scale of the public conversation rather than universal belief. Use it only as a period or later headline, not as a factual count.

## Required editorial safeguards

- Pair every audience-reaction claim with the limitation that calls and letters are self-selecting.
- Pair every description of the fake harvest with “the staged report” or “the fictional narration.”
- Do not convert a later BBC or press retelling into a primary 1957 record.
- Do not use “fooled Britain,” “millions believed,” or “everyone was taken in” as narration without new primary evidence and human approval.
