## Timeline with evidence status

| Date | Event or record | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|
| 1842 | Edwin Chadwick’s official sanitary report links urban drainage, accumulated filth, overcrowding, and preventable mortality in the evidence it collects. It recommends stronger public-health administration and treats sanitary works as prevention rather than merely relief. | **High that the report made this case; period medical claims are not modern causal findings.** S1–S2. |
| 1847 | The Metropolitan Commission of Sewers replaces multiple local commissions outside the City, according to the archive guide and Bazalgette’s later account. | **High for institutional consolidation; medium for whether the correct count is seven or eight predecessor commissions because sources define the City boundary differently.** S4, S7. |
| 1847–1855 | The MCS compels or encourages connections from houses to sewers and abolishes large numbers of cesspools. Bazalgette later says about 30,000 cesspools were abolished and house/street refuse thereby reached the river. | **High for the direction of policy; medium for the exact 30,000 figure and for attributing all river pollution to this one policy.** S4, S7. |
| 31 August 1848 | The Public Health Act creates a general sanitary framework for England and Wales, including sewerage, drainage, cleansing, and water supply, while excluding named metropolitan sewer-commission areas from ordinary application. | **High.** Official statute, S3. |
| 1848–1849 | London suffers a major cholera visitation. John Snow’s 1849 work argues from water-supply evidence for a waterborne route, while many contemporaries still used miasma language. | **High that the competing explanations existed; causal details require source-specific attribution.** S7, S17. |
| 1849 | The MCS invites designs for river purification and receives 116 schemes, according to Bazalgette’s 1865 account; the Commission resigns amid difficulty choosing. | **High that Bazalgette reports this; medium until the original MCS papers are checked.** S7. |
| 1850–1854 | Frank Forster and other engineers develop interception schemes. Bazalgette succeeds Forster in 1852 and is directed in 1854 to prepare a main-drainage plan with William Haywood; Stephenson and Cubitt eventually recommend the plan. | **High for the broad engineering sequence; medium for the exact approval chronology.** S7. |
| 9 July 1855 | Faraday’s *Times* letter describes the Thames as an “opaque pale brown fluid,” records visible suspended feculence, and tests its opacity with white cards. | **High as a published first-person observation; verify the original issue before final quotation.** S6. |
| 14 August 1855 | The Metropolis Management Act creates the Metropolitan Board of Works, transfers main sewers, gives it the duty to prevent sewage entering the Thames, and sets a 31 December 1860 completion target. Section CXXXVI requires outside approval of plans and estimates. | **High.** Official statute, S5. |
| 1 January 1856 | The MBW’s Act takes effect. It inherits a metropolitan drainage problem but does not yet have an implemented main-drainage system. | **High for commencement date; medium for the exact operational state of every sewer.** S5, S7, S10. |
| 1856 | Bazalgette’s plan is submitted within the new institutional framework; the MBW’s 1858 record identifies Bazalgette’s answers to questions at its 2 June 1858 meeting, showing technical work was active before the summer’s legislative climax. | **High for a pre-crisis plan/technical record; exact plan versions and approval status require the original MBW booklet and minutes.** S7–S8. |
| 11 December 1857 | The First Commissioner reports rejecting an MBW scheme whose outfall would be within the metropolis or vulnerable to tidal reflux. | **High as the parliamentary record of the dispute; the engineering merits remain contested.** S9. |
| 18 June 1858 | A Commons debate describes the 71 main sewers, the 1855 legal deadline, and the Board’s failure to deliver the required works. The speaker says conditions had become worse and calls for urgent action. | **High as evidence of political pressure and deadlock; the accusations are advocacy, not an audit.** S10. |
| June–July 1858 | Hot, dry conditions coincide with an exceptionally offensive Thames. Parliamentary debates and contemporary newspaper reprints describe closed windows, smell in corridors, nausea, and concern for health; the precise duration and local intensity vary by report. | **High that the smell became a public and parliamentary crisis; medium for meteorological mechanism and exact geographic extent.** S11, S12, S15, S16. |
| 25 June 1858 | Lords debate links the worsening river to expanded drainage, population, and dry weather, while stressing that poorer riverside residents could not simply leave. | **High as a period argument; low as a modern causal explanation.** S11. |
| 15 July 1858 | The amendment bill receives first reading after ministers describe the previous six or eight weeks as exposing the scale of the metropolitan evil and the weakness of the municipal machinery. Stephenson again says Bazalgette’s earlier plan would have been effective. | **High.** Hansard, S13. |
| 19 July 1858 | Second reading debate compares the MBW’s Barking proposal with a more expensive Sea Reach alternative. Palmerston argues that the bad smell experienced by Parliament was part of a wider and growing pollution and public-health problem. | **High for debate content; technical superiority remains disputed.** S12. |
| 2 August 1858 | The Metropolis Management Amendment Act receives Royal Assent. It orders works to begin promptly, permits up to £3 million borrowing with Treasury guarantee, establishes a 3d-in-the-pound main-drainage rate for 40 years, removes the prior plan-approval barrier, and extends the completion date to 1863. | **High.** Official statute, S14. |
| 1859 | Main-drainage works commence after the political and financial barriers are altered. | **High for commencement in Bazalgette’s 1865 account; verify against MBW annual reports.** S7, S18. |
| 1865 | Bazalgette presents his engineering account; the works are described as practically completed and in operation, with the main system’s objectives including interception, deep outfalls, and removal of tide-locked accumulations. | **High for the 1865 account; do not treat “practically completed” as the end of every associated work.** S7. |
| 1874–1875 | Later specialist records place completion of the major system and treatment/pumping works in the 1859–1874 period, with some related construction extending beyond 1865. | **Medium-high; verify the exact completion milestone used.** S18. |

## Chronology guardrails

- The Great Stink is a **late-stage accelerator** in this record, not the origin of sanitary concern, metropolitan sewer administration, or Bazalgette’s engineering work.
- The 1855 Act’s **legal duty** and 1858 Act’s **financing/expediting powers** are different. The earlier Act already aimed to stop sewage entering the Thames.
- “Parliament passed a bill in 18 days” is defensible only as the interval from first reading on **15 July** to the Act dated **2 August 1858**. It does not mean the engineering problem was conceived or solved in 18 days.
- The 1858 Act allowed interim deodorizing/protective works and authorized large expenditure; it did not make the new network operational on the day it passed.
- Keep Victorian miasma fears, Snow’s waterborne theory, and present-day microbiological knowledge separate. No claim in this pack proves that smell caused cholera.
