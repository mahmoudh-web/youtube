## Research stop condition

This is a source-led evidence pack, not a script. Stop at this handoff until a human editor or historian has checked the original statutes, parliamentary print, newspaper scans, MBW papers, and the medical-history distinctions below.

## Primary-source and archive checks

1. Inspect the original scan of Chadwick’s 1842 report in the Wellcome record (S1), and record page numbers for the drainage, refuse, disease, and administrative recommendations used in C01.
2. Obtain the original 1847–1855 MCS reports, minutes, drainage applications, and plans. The London Archives identifies MCS applications in **MCS/244–304**, indexes **MCS/305–306**, and plans **MCS/P/001–026** and **MCS/PR/001–043** (S4).
3. Obtain the full 1858 MBW booklet, *Main drainage of the metropolis: Mr. Bazalgette’s replies to questions put to him at the board held on June 2nd 1858* (S8). Compare its plan, quantities, outfall, and answers with Bazalgette’s 1865 retrospective account.
4. Inspect the original MBW annual reports for 1856–1865 and the Board’s Main Drainage Committee minutes. Use them to separate plan approval, financing, land acquisition, construction commencement, and completion milestones.
5. Compare the original British newspaper issues for June–July 1858 with the Newfoundland Express reprint (S15), including the *Times*, *City Press*, *Observer*, *Westmorland Gazette*, *Wrexham Advertiser*, and *Illustrated London News*. Record the first secure use of “Great Stink,” the exact dates of parliamentary disruption, and whether reports describe different days or locations.
6. Verify the 1855 Act and 1858 Act against the official scans (S5, S14), including the section numbers, the scope of the approval repeal, the £3 million ceiling, the 3d rate, Treasury guarantee, navigation constraints, and the changed completion date.
7. Resolve the predecessor-commission count: Bazalgette says eight distinct commissions before 1847, while the archive guide describes the MCS as replacing seven separate commissions outside the City. Explain the boundary convention rather than silently choosing one number.
8. Check the 1854 Bazalgette/Haywood plan and Stephenson/Cubitt recommendation in the original engineering papers. “Bazalgette had a plan before the stink” is central, but the exact version, approval, and cost must be documented.

## Engineering and hydrology checks

1. Have a civil engineer or historian of Victorian infrastructure review the terms **intercepting sewer**, **tide-locked sewer**, **outfall**, **reflux**, **deodorization**, **pumping**, and **discharge at high water**.
2. Do not present Bazalgette’s 1865 account as an unbiased failure investigation. Identify where he is describing his own work, defending institutional decisions, or relying on earlier reports.
3. Keep the 1855 legal duty, 1857 plan rejection, 1858 bill, 1858 Act, 1859 construction, and later completion as separate milestones.
4. Do not convert “purification” into modern wastewater treatment. State what the period scheme did at the stated date and outfall.
5. If using the 18-day figure, show the arithmetic explicitly: first reading **15 July 1858** to Act date **2 August 1858**. Do not imply all design work occurred in that period.

## Public-health and medical-history checks

1. Have a medical historian compare Chadwick’s miasma-era language with John Snow’s 1849 waterborne argument and later germ-theory knowledge.
2. Treat “poisonous gases,” “pestilence,” and “health destroyed” in parliamentary speeches as period fears or attributed arguments unless supported by independent epidemiological evidence.
3. Do not claim that the Great Stink caused cholera. The cited July debate says there had been no London epidemic and discusses cholera hypothetically (S12 lines 327–331).
4. Verify any 1848–1854 mortality figures before using them. Bazalgette’s 1865 figures are a retrospective summary and may combine or label outbreaks differently.
5. Do not state that the sewer project “saved millions” or ended cholera without a date-specific epidemiological source and a careful causal caveat.

## Institutional and legal-sensitivity checks

1. Parliamentary accusations that the MBW was impotent or that ministers began at the “wrong end” are political positions. Attribute them; do not turn them into adjudicated findings.
2. “Negligence,” “cover-up,” “criminal pollution,” “government failure,” “no liability,” and “Parliament only cared once it smelled the river” are legally or rhetorically loaded. They require a legal historian and direct evidence before use.
3. The 1858 Act’s repeal of approval provisions should be described narrowly. A human legal reviewer should confirm what was repealed and what navigation, compensation, Treasury, and nuisance controls remained.
4. No living person is implicated. Historical institutional blame still carries reputational and accuracy risk if presented without attribution or qualification.
