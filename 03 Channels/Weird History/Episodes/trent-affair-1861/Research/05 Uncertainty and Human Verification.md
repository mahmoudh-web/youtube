## Stop condition

This pack stops at research. No narration, title/thumbnail package, visual generation, archival download, edit, upload or publication decision is authorised by these notes. A human editor must inspect the primary records and approve any material claim before downstream work begins.

## Unresolved factual questions

- What exact orders, if any, did Captain Wilkes have before intercepting *Trent*? What did he know about Mason and Slidell, and what did Lieutenant Fairfax advise?
- Where precisely did the interception occur, what shots were fired, how long did the boarding take, and what did the boarding party search for or remove besides the four men?
- Did Wilkes’s report describe the passengers as contraband, dispatches, enemy agents or another category? Which legal authorities did he cite at the time?
- What were the precise dates on which London received the news, the British cabinet approved the demand, Lyons delivered it, and Washington received each dispatch? How much did communication delay shape the escalation?
- What were the exact Nassau and intermediate travel dates before the *Trent* left Havana on 7 November? The direct copy S18 confirms the Havana departure but not the complete itinerary.
- What did the original British instructions say before Prince Albert’s intervention, what did Albert change, and how much authority did Palmerston, Russell, the Cabinet and the Queen exercise at each stage?
- What did the British War Office and Admiralty order for Canada and the Western Atlantic? Which forces were already planned for colonial defence, which were triggered by the *Trent*, and which actually sailed or arrived?
- Were British military preparations intended primarily to deter, defend Canada, prepare for war, reassure Parliament, or serve several purposes at once?
- What did Lincoln’s Cabinet discuss on 21, 25 and 26 December? Can the often-repeated “one war at a time” phrase be tied to an authenticated contemporary diary, memorandum or recollection?
- What is the complete wording of Seward’s note to Lord Lyons? Does its use of “inadvertency,” legal defence and release amount to an apology, a disavowal, a concession, or a deliberately mixed formula?
- Why do official and institutional accounts differ on 1 January release/transfer and 3 January departure? What do Fort Warren, State Department and HMS *Rinaldo* records establish?
- Did British public opinion materially constrain the Cabinet, or did the Cabinet use public anger to justify decisions already made? Sample the *Times*, *Morning Post*, *Observer*, provincial papers and parliamentary speeches rather than relying on one editorial.
- How should the Confederacy’s slavery-based diplomatic objective be integrated without turning the episode into a generic Civil War summary or sanitising Mason and Slidell’s politics?

## Required primary-source checks

1. **Naval record:** retrieve Charles Wilkes’s report, Navy Department correspondence, Lieutenant Fairfax’s account, *San Jacinto* deck log and orders. Record archival page/image locators for the encounter.
2. **Diplomatic record:** inspect the complete Library of Congress correspondence volume S11 and the official FRUS documents around the November–January sequence; compare them with the direct copy S18. Compare the American and British versions line by line.
3. **British Cabinet and Foreign Office record:** locate the original 30 November demand behind direct copy S18, the 19/20 December communications, final British acceptance and the drafts showing Prince Albert’s intervention. Verify whether the words “apology,” “disavowal,” “reparation” and “liberation” refer to the same or different documents.
4. **Military record:** retrieve War Office, Admiralty and Canadian colonial records for reinforcement orders, transport manifests, garrison strength, naval deployments and the dates troops reached Halifax/Canada.
5. **Executive decision record:** read Lincoln’s Cabinet notes/diaries, Seward’s draft, Navy Secretary Welles’s diary and Charles Sumner’s papers. Do not attribute the decision to Lincoln alone.
6. **Congressional record:** inspect the original House/Senate proceedings for Wilkes’s commendation, medal proposal and any debate over the legality or release. Distinguish a motion, recommendation, resolution and enacted award.
7. **Press record:** sample contemporaneous British, Northern U.S., Southern U.S. and Canadian newspapers. Label original reporting, reprint, editorial, rumour and caricature; preserve publication dates and political affiliation.

## Language and translation checks

- Keep *Trent*, *San Jacinto*, *Theodora*, HMS *Rinaldo*, Fort Warren, Havana, Nassau, the Old Bahama Channel and Halifax distinct; do not merge ship, port and detention locations. Treat Nassau and intermediate route dates as unresolved leads, not established itinerary.
- Use “Confederate commissioners/envoys” rather than “diplomats” when discussing formal status. The British legal record treated recognition and immunity as separate issues.
- Distinguish **neutrality**, **belligerency**, **neutral flag**, **contraband**, **dispatches**, **visit and search**, **capture**, **prize court**, **release**, **disavowal**, **apology** and **reparation**. They are not interchangeable.
- Do not call the British deployment an “ultimatum” unless the exact document and condition are identified; use “formal demand” or “instructions” where that is what the record supports.
- Treat “one war at a time,” “surrender,” “humiliation,” “victory” and “nearly war” as attributed or interpretive language until provenance and scope are checked.
- Historical actors are deceased, but claims about treason, pro-slavery politics, British aggression or American illegality remain reputational and legal-history risks. Attribute rather than editorialise.

## Legal, ethical and editorial-risk flags

| Risk | Why it matters | Required control |
|---|---|---|
| War inflation | The title can imply a war, battle or casualties that did not occur. | Use “crisis,” “naval seizure,” “diplomatic confrontation” or “risk of armed conflict” in factual prose; retain “nearly started a war” only as a sourced interpretation. |
| Legal overclaim | Britain and the United States argued different maritime-law positions; there was no trial deciding the matter. | Attribute each legal argument and preserve the unresolved distinction between search, capture and removal. |
| Apology/disavowal confusion | Modern summaries compress different forms of British demand and U.S. response. | Quote or paraphrase the exact primary document; say what Britain accepted, not what a later summary assumes. |
| Military-number inflation | Sources use different numbers for planned, shipped, arrived and available forces. | Never present a single troop total without War Office/Admiralty evidence and a defined date. |
| Confederate sanitisation | Mason and Slidell’s mission sought recognition for a slaveholding breakaway government. | State the diplomatic objective and slavery context without turning the episode into advocacy for either side. |
| Rumour as fact | Reports of orders from Seward, British war intentions and the “one war” phrase circulated quickly. | Tag rumours and later recollections; require primary provenance before quoting. |
| Nationalist framing | “British insult” and “Union triumph” are competing political frames. | Present U.S., British, Confederate and Canadian perspectives and identify each source’s position. |
| Archival rights | Navy, LOC, newspaper and manuscript images have separate reproduction conditions. | Rights/provenance review is a later human gate; this pack clears no asset. |
