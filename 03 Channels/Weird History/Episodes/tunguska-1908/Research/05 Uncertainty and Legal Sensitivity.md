## Legal, ethical and editorial sensitivity

- **Living-person defamation is not the main risk:** the event is historical and the named researchers are deceased, but the usual accuracy duty still applies to descendants, institutions and communities. Do not attach unsupported wrongdoing to named witnesses, scientists or Evenki people.
- **Cultural representation:** do not turn Evenki beliefs, reluctance to speak, or historical labels into exotic spectacle. Preserve the difference between a community’s recorded interpretation and a narrator’s claim about what “really happened.”
- **Translation risk:** Russian archival wording may contain outdated ethnonyms, imperial vocabulary or ambiguous technical terms. Quote sparingly, preserve the original-language locator, and have a Russian-reading human verify the translation.
- **Scientific uncertainty:** “asteroid,” “meteorite,” “bolide,” “comet” and “airburst” must not be used as synonyms. The safest current wording is an atmospheric explosion caused by a cosmic body, with a stony asteroid as the leading model.
- **False balance:** fringe proposals do not deserve equal treatment merely because the exact body was not recovered. Mention exotic ideas only to explain the history of speculation, not to imply parity with peer-reviewed airburst models.
- **Human and animal harm:** avoid “bloodless,” “harmless,” or playful treatment. The event destroyed forest, damaged or destroyed dwellings according to witness summaries, and was associated with reported animal losses.
- **Archival imagery and documents:** the existence of a scan or photograph in a museum/archive does not automatically grant reuse rights or prove the caption’s full interpretation. Rights/provenance must be handled separately by the production team after research approval.

## Human verification checklist

1. Read S9’s document images; record witness names, dates, places, original language, interviewer and whether each account is first-hand or hearsay.
2. Retrieve the original *Sibir* and *Sibirskaya Zhizn* issues cited by S10; verify the dates, wording and the partly fictitious Filimonovo account.
3. Verify old-style/new-style dates and local-time assumptions with a historian of Russian/Siberian records.
4. Read Kulik’s original 1927 paper and expedition report; distinguish his direct observations from local testimony and his expectation of an iron meteorite.
5. Read the full NASA workshop report and Chyba et al. paper; preserve ranges, assumptions and disagreements over energy, altitude, size and composition.
6. Review the complete Lake Cheko debate, including later age/sediment studies, before deciding whether the story can safely say “no crater.”
7. Ask a Russian-reading historian and an East Siberia/Evenki specialist to review terminology, translation and perspective.
