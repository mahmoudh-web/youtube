## Evidence-bounded answer

Yes, a small RAF cat delivery to Bario in Sarawak is strongly supported. The best current trail is a reproduced No. 48 Squadron Operations Record Book entry for 13 March 1960 describing a “unique drop” from Changi to Bario, 7,000 pounds of stores and more than 20 cats, followed by a message that the cats were safe. Contemporary Singapore newspaper records independently point to the same episode: a 30-cat recruitment notice, a report published on 17 March, and a follow-up published on 22 March. The original RAF page and newspaper images still need human inspection, so this is not yet cleared for narration.

The larger “Operation Cat Drop” legend is less secure. WHO’s Sarawak malaria programme is documented from 1952 onward, and the 1956 WHO report records effective DDT/BHC control in the Baram and Tinjar valleys. A 1959 North Borneo annual report separately says field rats were unusually troublesome partly because antimalarial spraying accidentally killed cats. Those facts make the ecological story plausible as a hypothesis, but they do not prove that DDT killed Bario’s cats, that the Bario rats were caused by spraying, or that WHO ordered the RAF delivery.

The specific claim that DDT biomagnified through insects or lizards into cats is not established. The peer-reviewed historical review by O’Shaughnessy says this central part of the story was never verified and that later versions combine details from Sarawak, North Borneo/Sabah and other countries. Harrisson’s 1965 account is valuable evidence of how the story was told, but its date conflicts with the 1960 RAF record and its causal explanation is not a toxicology report.

## Working interpretation

The strongest interpretation is not “14,000 cats saved Borneo” and not “the entire story was invented.” It is a documented, small 1960 RAF supply drop that later became attached to a much broader ecological parable. The public-health campaign and the cat delivery are real evidence tracks; the tidy causal bridge between them remains unresolved. The story’s persistence likely reflects the force of its image—cats descending in baskets—combined with later debates about DDT and unintended consequences, but that reception history should not be mistaken for proof of the mechanism.

## Evidence-bounded research boundary

If human verification confirms the underlying records, further research should compare the documents supporting the small RAF flight with the records and retellings that fail to establish the DDT-to-cats-to-rats chain. The research must preserve uncertainty and the agency of the Kelabit community rather than turn the event into a simplified ecological fable.

This is a research proposition, not a script, title package, visual brief or production plan.

## Decision

**Hold — research verification required.**

Advance only after a human verifier checks:

- the original March 1960 RAF Operations Record Book page;
- the 9, 17 and 22 March plus 18 April 1960 newspaper scans;
- the 1959 North Borneo annual report and the full WHO Sarawak records;
- the Sarawak Museum Journal article and relevant Sarawak archival files;
- Harrisson’s original 1965 account and the provenance of later retellings;
- all number, date, chemical and agency-attribution wording;
- community framing, animal-welfare treatment and image rights.

No creative or production work is created or authorized by this pack.
