## Research question

Who interrupted WGN-TV and WTTW on 22 November 1987, what can the surviving broadcasts and records establish about access, technical method and motive, and why did the public investigation not identify a perpetrator?

## Working answer and scope

The located record firmly supports two unauthorised interruptions of Chicago television on the same Sunday night: a short WGN interruption during its 9 p.m. news and a longer WTTW interruption during *Doctor Who*. Contemporary reporting describes engineers recovering WGN by changing the studio-to-transmitter link frequency; later WTTW reporting records engineers’ view that the perpetrator required sophisticated technical knowledge. Those sources narrow the technical problem but do not identify the person, prove insider access, establish a particular RF setup or establish a motive.

The FCC is documented as investigating in contemporary and later reporting. Later FBI FOIA logs show requests titled “Max Headroom Broadcast Intrusion” or “Max Headroom Incident,” but a log entry is not an investigative file. A 2022 FCC FOIA request for records about the incident was marked “No Responsive Documents” in the public request record. The pack therefore treats the survival, location and contents of any original law-enforcement file as unresolved.

## Source-quality key

- **A — official or contemporaneous source:** station publication, government FOIA log, contemporaneous newspaper/trade report or original broadcast record.
- **A/D — primary material accessed through a mirror or transcription:** useful evidence, but the originating scan, PDF or recording must be independently checked.
- **B — institutional retrospective:** broadcaster history or station interview that preserves later testimony; strong for attributed recollection, not automatically proof of 1987 technical facts.
- **C/D — archive or secondary access page:** useful for discovery and context; not sufficient alone for identity, motive or technical conclusions.

## Source-handling rules

1. Use “the person or people responsible remain unidentified,” not a named internet suspect.
2. Say “contemporary engineers reported or speculated that the studio-to-transmitter link was overridden,” not “the culprit used [specific equipment]” unless a primary engineering record is found.
3. Distinguish the FCC’s documented investigation/reporting from later FOIA requests and the absence of responsive records.
4. Treat motive as interpretation: references to WGN, Chuck Swirsky, Coca-Cola/Pepsi and “Greatest World Newspaper” may show media familiarity, but they do not prove a target or ideology.
5. Treat the circulating videos as archival research copies, not cleared production assets.
