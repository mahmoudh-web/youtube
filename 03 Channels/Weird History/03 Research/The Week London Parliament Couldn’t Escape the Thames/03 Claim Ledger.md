## Ledger conventions

“Confidence” measures how safely a claim can be stated with its source label attached. **Fact** means the source records an event, text, observation, or statutory provision. **Allegation** means a participant’s accusation or attributed claim, not an adjudicated finding. **Interpretation** means a reasoned synthesis from several facts. **Uncertainty** marks unresolved source conflict, missing originals, or limits on causal inference. Open caveats must travel with the claim.

## Allegations and attributed positions requiring labels

- The June 1858 Commons speaker alleges the MBW was “utterly impotent” and had achieved nothing (S10 lines 46–47). This is a political attack, not a finding.
- The December 1857 First Commissioner rejects an MBW plan as contrary to the 1855 Act’s intent (S9 lines 18–20). Attribute the rejection; do not call it proof of incompetence.
- Bazalgette attributes earlier failures to commission turnover, opposition, and procedural delay (S7 lines 174–227, 270–281). Treat this as a retrospective institutional account.

## Not yet claimable

- “The Great Stink invented London’s sewer system.”
- “Parliament only acted because MPs smelled it.”
- “The smell caused cholera,” “the Thames was literally poisonous,” or any modern disease diagnosis based only on odour.
- “Bazalgette designed the system in 1858” without acknowledging 1854 plans and earlier engineers.
- “The 1858 Act built the sewers,” rather than authorized, financed, and expedited works that began in 1859.
- “The river was cleaned” without specifying the outfall, tide, deodorization, treatment, date, and remaining pollution.
- “The MBW was negligent,” “the government committed a crime,” or “the 1858 Act guaranteed public health” without specialist legal and medical-history review.
