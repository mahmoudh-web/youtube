## Evidence-bounded answer

On 18 August 1976, a UNC work party entered the Panmunjom Joint Security Area to prune a poplar tree that obstructed observation. North Korean guards attacked the party, killing U.S. Army officers Arthur Bonifas and Mark Barrett and wounding other UNC personnel. The immediate U.S. response raised the military alert and assembled a visible combination of aircraft, ground forces and naval power.

Three days later, Operation Paul Bunyan returned to the JSA and cut down the tree. The choice was deliberately conspicuous, but the internal record shows that the operation was designed to avoid confrontation. Artillery fire and direct strikes were considered; JCS and WSAG discussions repeatedly focused on the exposed work party, local firepower, rules, diplomatic optics and the risk of triggering a larger exchange. The tree came down without a reported clash. The exact total force and duration remain disputed: later Army accounts give 813 soldiers and 45 minutes, another Army account gives 42 minutes, and the Ford/CIA material describes smaller cutting and quick-reaction components.

The immediate result was not a clean apology or a proven North Korean surrender. North Korean reporting called the tree cutting a provocation and described the original attack as self-defence. A later message associated with Kim Il-sung expressed regret that the incident had occurred but, in the U.S./U.N. summaries, did not accept responsibility. Subsequent JSA arrangements separated forces by the Military Demarcation Line, guaranteed personnel safety and removed KPA posts from the UNC side.

## Provisional interpretation

The crisis was contained through a combination of visible coercion and controlled restraint. The United States and South Korea made the cost of another attack legible through DEFCON 3, air deployments, the *Midway* group, protected ground forces and the public fact of cutting the tree. At the same time, they withheld a direct strike during the operation and kept diplomatic channels open. The evidence supports this as a plausible explanation for why the response did not become a second clash; it cannot prove the counterfactual that the operation alone “prevented war.”

The episode also contains a less flattering finding: the initial reaction system failed, senior officials criticised the delayed response force and later acknowledged inadequate JSA command-and-control procedures. De-escalation followed an operational crisis; it was not evidence that the first encounter had been perfectly managed.

## Safest provisional wording

> After North Korean guards killed two U.S. officers during a tree-pruning mission at Panmunjom, the United States and South Korea returned three days later to cut down the tree under a deliberately overwhelming display of military power. But the operation was not a battlefield attack: U.S. records show that artillery and strikes were debated while the tree-cutting force was kept protected and exposed to as little risk as possible. The tree fell without a second clash, and the crisis moved into regret messages and new rules for separating the two sides in the Joint Security Area.

This wording avoids a fixed troop total, a single duration, a proven central order, a legally loaded “apology,” and the unprovable claim that one operation alone prevented war.

## What the record does not prove yet

- That the 18 August killings were ordered by Kim Jong-il or another central leader.
- That the total Operation Paul Bunyan force was exactly 813, 110, 300 or any other one number without defining the perimeter counted.
- That the tree fell in exactly 42 or 45 minutes.
- That “apology” is a safe translation of the later North Korean message.
- That North Korea’s non-interference was caused solely by fear of U.S. force.
- That the operation alone prevented a second Korean War.
- That all listed aircraft were overhead simultaneously or that every nuclear-capable platform carried nuclear weapons.
- That the incident was legally a declared war or conventional battle.
