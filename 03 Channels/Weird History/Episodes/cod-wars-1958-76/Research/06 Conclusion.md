## Evidence-bounded answer

The confrontations did not end because wire cutters defeated a navy in a battle. They ended in stages. In 1961 Britain accepted Iceland’s 12-mile fisheries zone while receiving temporary, defined access to parts of the outer six miles. In November 1973, after the 50-mile crisis, the governments adopted a two-year interim arrangement with vessel, area, conservation and inspection rules and withdrew naval/tug protection. After Iceland’s 200-mile move and the dangerous 1975–76 escalation, the governments exchanged notes in Oslo on 1 June 1976.

The 1976 arrangement regulated a limited British presence: an average of 24 trawlers per day, a maximum of 29, conservation stand-offs of 20–30 miles, reporting, inspection and delisting provisions. It lasted six months and expressly preserved the parties’ views on mutual jurisdiction. The defensible description is therefore a negotiated, de facto access settlement—not an unconditional legal recognition or a battlefield surrender. (S2, S10, S11, S12, S14.)

## Provisional interpretation

The outcome came from the interaction of small-state leverage, Iceland’s exceptional dependence on fisheries, conservation concerns, the changing law-of-the-sea environment, the economic and political costs of protecting Britain’s distant-water fleet, and alliance diplomacy. The ICJ’s 1974 holding was mixed: it rejected Iceland’s unilateral opposability against the UK/FRG but recognised preferential rights and a duty to negotiate. NATO and Norway helped create diplomatic pressure, but the final instrument was bilateral. This is an interpretation from the ledger, not a single source’s settled verdict.

## Safest provisional wording

> Between 1958 and 1976, Iceland and the United Kingdom fought a series of fisheries confrontations over who could fish around Iceland. Iceland repeatedly extended its claimed fisheries jurisdiction—from 12 to 50 to 200 nautical miles—while Britain used naval protection to keep trawlers operating. In the later confrontations, Icelandic Coast Guard vessels cut trawl wires and British ships intervened, producing dangerous collisions. The disputes ended through staged agreements and, in June 1976, a government-to-government exchange of notes that tightly regulated a limited British presence while leaving the underlying legal positions formally unresolved.

This wording deliberately avoids unverified casualty totals, a claim that NATO alone brokered the outcome, and unconditional legal recognition.

## What the record does not prove yet

- That a formal war was declared, that there was a decisive battle, or that the conflict was bloodless.
- That wire cutters were used throughout 1958–76 rather than principally in the later phases.
- The exact total of cuts, collisions, injuries, or deaths.
- That the 1976 agreement was a fixed 30,000-ton quota; NOAA’s estimate and the treaty’s effort rules are different claims.
- That Britain unconditionally recognised Iceland’s 200-mile legal jurisdiction in June 1976.
- That NATO’s strategic position alone caused Britain’s concessions.
- That any quotation, ship name, incident date, or Icelandic translation is safe without the checks in `05 Uncertainty and Human Verification.md`.
