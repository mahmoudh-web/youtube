## Stop condition

This is a source-led research pack, not a script. No narration, title, thumbnail, visual treatment or production asset should be built until a human editor verifies the archival wording, translations and uncertainty flags below.

## Source and translation risks

- Inspect or obtain authenticated images/transcriptions for **ADBR 12 J 1495**, **AMS III 200/15**, and **AMS 1 MR3, f° 72r**. The web summaries and secondary translations are not the originals.
- Preserve the distinction between the 25 July municipal letter, Brant's surviving extracts, Imlin/Duntzenheim traditions, Wilhelm Rem, Specklin and Paracelsus. They are not one contemporary eyewitness account.
- Verify whether “succumbed” in a translated chronicle means became affected or died. Never use the verb as a death count without the original wording and context.
- Check the German/French translations of the 25 July explanation: astral conjunction, heat, hot blood, and the recommendations about food and instruments are period medical theory.
- Do not call the Brant material a complete diary. Clementz says the original annals were lost in the 1870 Strasbourg library fire and survive through extracts/copies.

## Disputed scale and deaths

- **Secure broad window:** mid-July to September 1518.
- **Reported numbers:** roughly 34, about 50, and more than 400 occur in different source traditions. Treat them as reported counts with provenance, not cumulative census data.
- **Deaths:** the pack found later claims of people dancing to death and up to 15 deaths per day, but no verified exact count. Clementz explicitly says the number cannot be determined and notes that some historians find no document certifying deaths from exhaustion.
- Do not state “400 people danced to death,” “hundreds died,” or “15 died each day” as fact. Do not replace those with “nobody died”; the evidence is unresolved.

## Cause and diagnosis

- “Epidemic” and “plague” are convenient modern labels. The municipal record calls the condition a natural illness, but that does not establish infectious contagion.
- Do not diagnose ergotism. It is a historical hypothesis with symptom and epidemiological objections; Marillier and Godard still leave the precise etiology open.
- Do not diagnose mass hysteria, mass psychogenic illness, conversion disorder or a neurological disease. These are modern interpretive frameworks and need qualified attribution.
- Do not describe the dancers as pretending, immoral, possessed, mentally weak or voluntary performers. Those judgments belong to later polemic or modern stereotype, not verified fact.
- Avoid claiming that Saint Guy cured the episode. The records show pilgrimage and a later decline; temporal order is not proof of efficacy.

## Religious, medical and ethical handling

- “Saint Vitus dance” is a historical label associated with a regional religious and medical culture; it is not the same thing as modern Sydenham chorea.
- Explain that early authorities could combine astrology, humoral medicine, civic control, guild responsibility, prayer and pilgrimage without seeing those as contradictory.
- The episode involves sufferers, not a spectacle to ridicule. Avoid “rave party,” “zombie outbreak,” “patient zero,” and “mass hysteria” unless the language is explicitly identified as modern shorthand and challenged.
- The subjects are long-dead, so modern defamation risk is low. The main editorial risks are medical misinformation, religious caricature, anachronistic diagnosis and false precision about deaths.

## Human verification checklist

1. Compare the 25 July letter against **ADBR 12 J 1495** and approve a line-accurate translation.
2. Inspect **AMS III 200/15** for the 23 July commission, Saverne instruction, guild note and any count or treatment detail.
3. Inspect **AMS 1 MR3, f° 72r** for the exact 2 August ban, instruments and end date; compare the separate 3 August Brant note for the 30-shilling fine.
4. Reconcile Brant, Imlin, Duntzenheim, Rem and Specklin dates and numbers against the original language or a specialist edition.
5. Decide whether any death wording is safe enough for narration; if not, omit deaths rather than infer them.
6. Have a historian of early-modern medicine review the terms “ergotism,” “conversion,” “mass psychogenic illness,” “trance,” “chorea” and “Saint Vitus dance.”
7. Keep the episode in research until every material claim in [Claim Ledger](03%20Claim%20Ledger.md) has a human-approved source label and caveat.
