## Evidence-bounded conclusion

The strongest located evidence supports a documented outbreak in the Bukoba district of Tanganyika that began on 30 January 1962 at a mission-run girls’ school near Kashasha. Rankin and Philip’s 1963 report recorded sudden, recurrent attacks of laughing and crying, restlessness, fear and occasional violence when restraint was attempted. It described 95 of 159 pupils affected before the school’s first closure, 57 further pupils involved after reopening, and later reported episodes in Nshamba, Ramashenye, Kanyangereka and nearby boys’ schools.

The report’s medical record is more careful than the popular story. It says attacks generally lasted minutes to hours, with respite and recurrence, though a few cases lasted up to 16 days. It reports no significant abnormal physical signs, no fatalities and no serious sequelae, while also recording incapacity, fear, educational disruption and difficulty controlling attacks. Investigators reported normal findings in the tests they performed, no virus growth or antibodies in the samples described, no foreign seeds in examined food samples and no food factor peculiar to affected people. Those findings weakened the infectious and toxic explanations they considered; they did not prove that every organic cause was impossible.

Rankin and Philip tentatively suggested “mass hysteria in a susceptible population” and called the condition “probably a culturally determined disease,” but they also said they could not explain why it began and that cultural context required further study. Hempelmann later recast the event as motor-variant mass psychogenic illness and argued that laughter was one symptom, not a humorous stimulus or a contagious mechanism. Pringle’s historical analysis warns that East African psychiatric interpretations must be read alongside colonial legacies, post-independence institutions and the gap between medical categories and local explanations.

The defensible answer to the episode question is therefore: the records describe a real, disruptive pattern of recurrent emotional/motor attacks in schools and villages, not a countrywide joke or continuous laughter epidemic. The best-supported later model is an attributed MPI interpretation, but the cause of the initial outbreak remains unresolved, and the surviving record is too thin to diagnose the affected people or to treat a Western psychiatric label as the community’s final explanation.

## Safe episode promise

> The Tanganyika “laughter epidemic” was not the story of a joke that infected a country. The surviving medical report describes recurrent attacks of laughing and crying that moved through schools and villages, found no cause in the tests it performed, and ended with a cautious suggestion—not a definitive diagnosis.

This is a provisional editorial proposition, not approved narration. A human editor should decide whether “moved through” is sufficiently cautious or should be replaced with “appeared in succession across.”

## What can safely be said after verification

- A contemporary medical report exists and is identifiable by authors, title, journal, year and pages.
- The initial school, closure/reopening sequence, attack pattern, reported counts and investigation categories can be described with explicit attribution.
- The doctors considered infectious, toxic and psychological explanations and reported the results of their examinations and tests.
- The report itself was tentative about mass hysteria and cultural context.
- Later MPI language is a scholarly interpretation that helps explain why laughter was only one symptom, but it is not a retrospective diagnosis of individual pupils.
- The social and institutional context—including Tanganyika’s recent independence, mission schooling, family separation and medical authority—can be investigated as context without being promoted to a proven cause.

## What must not be said without new evidence

- A joke triggered the outbreak.
- People laughed continuously for months or years.
- Laughter itself was contagious or viral.
- The whole country was paralysed.
- 1,000 people, 14 schools, 18 months or 2.5 years are settled primary-record totals.
- The attacks were imaginary, feigned, harmless or caused by Tanzanian cultural deficiency.
- Independence, Western education, family separation or a particular toxin caused the first cases.
- “Mass hysteria” is a modern diagnosis of the pupils or villagers.
