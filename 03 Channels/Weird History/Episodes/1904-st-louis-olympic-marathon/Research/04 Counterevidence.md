## Standard story being tested

The popular version says that 32 men ran 25 miles through a 90-degree afternoon with almost no water; cars filled the road with dust; one runner nearly died from dust, another rode to the finish, a Cuban ate rotten apples and slept, a South African was chased by dogs, and the winner survived injections of “rat poison” and brandy. Much of the shape is traceable to period reporting, but the standard version silently merges conflicting measurements, later anecdotes and a handler's retrospective account.

## Counterevidence to false precision

### 1. “32 starters” is not the only historical count

Lucas says 41 were carded and 31 answered the clerk. Sullivan's 1909 handbook says 29 starters. Modern databases commonly give 32. These may reflect entries, scratches, clerk attendance or later classification rather than a single simple error. State the conflict and use 14 finishers as the safer common result.

### 2. One water station and two water points are different claims

Lucas says there was one place after the stadium where athletes could obtain fresh water, at 12 miles, from a well. Smithsonian and later accounts report a water tower at six miles and a well at 12 miles. The defensible statement is that water access was restricted and the exact official layout is unresolved. The “purposeful dehydration” theory also needs a direct Sullivan source.

### 3. The heat number is a source statement, not a reconciled weather observation

Lucas reports 90°F in the shade. A modern weather aggregation derived from historical data lists an 84°F high for St. Louis on 30 August 1904, while the NWS warns that historical transcriptions and pre-1874 records require care. Station, observation time, shade/sun exposure and data reconstruction may explain the difference, but no located source settles it. Use “Lucas reported 90°F in the shade” or “period accounts described extreme heat,” not a falsely exact climatological claim.

### 4. The course distance shifts between records

The event is commonly listed as 24 miles 1,500 yards / 40 km / 24.85 miles. Lucas also calls the road section a “trifle over twenty-three miles,” and Sullivan's 1909 summary rounds it to 25 miles. Do not calculate pace or compare modern marathon records from an unverified single distance.

### 5. “Injected rat poison” is not the core primary wording located

Lucas records two one-sixtieth-grain strychnine-sulphate doses by mouth, egg, brandy and warm sponging. A later medical-history account quotes an injection of one milligram and a large glass of brandy. Those may derive from different recollections or sources, but they cannot be merged. Strychnine was toxic; calling it “rat poison” is a modern explanatory label that can obscure the period medical context.

### 6. Hicks was not explicitly carried across the line in Lucas's account

Lucas says Hicks tried to increase speed, could barely run the last 440 yards, was caught by Thomas F. Riley after breaking the tape, and was too weak to accept the trophy. This supports collapse-level exhaustion but not every later image of handlers carrying a limp body over the tape.

### 7. Garcia's hemorrhage was observed, but the dust mechanism is an attributed diagnosis

Lucas says dust eroded the stomach wall and caused the hemorrhage. Later summaries repeat this as settled medical fact. The located evidence establishes the reported hemorrhage and emergency removal; it does not include a hospital chart, autopsy or modern examination. Use “Lucas attributed the injury to dust” rather than diagnosing it.

### 8. Carvajal's food and nap story has grown in transmission

Lucas says Carvajal stopped at the author's automobile, asked for peaches, playfully took two when refused and ate them while running. The familiar rotten-apple and nap version is not in this core account. It may come from another contemporary article, but the exact passage must be found before use.

### 9. Dog interference is secure only at broad scale

Lucas says both South African finishers were chased by two dogs. Other retellings specify one dog and a mile-long detour. Preserve “dog interference was reported” until the original reports and route are compared.

### 10. Lorz's conduct, motive and sanction are separate questions

The sources support a car ride, re-entry and disqualification. Lucas records Lorz saying the automobile broke down and he wanted to get home; later reporting calls it a joke. Lucas says one-year suspension, while later summaries preserve a life ban that was commuted. Do not convert a disputed motive or sanction into a single moral verdict.

### 11. The two automobile narratives must not be conflated

Lorz's unauthorized ride and the official/support cars around Hicks are different events. Lucas's own car followed Hicks from the ten-mile mark, and the referee's car was said to be about 100 yards ahead. A protest alleged pacing; Lucas defended the arrangement. The existence of cars near the winner does not prove the protest was frivolous, and Lorz's ride does not prove Hicks was paced.

### 12. Period racial language is evidence of the source's worldview, not a fact about athletic ability

Lucas presents the race as evidence of “Caucasian” and English superiority, while the two South African runners finished ahead of most of the field. This is a period ideology and editorial interpretation, not a valid scientific conclusion. Modern narration should name the racism as part of the record and avoid repeating it as analysis.

### 13. “Doping scandal” is anachronistic without a rules qualifier

S1 itself praises the apparent usefulness of drugs, and S13 says no rule prohibiting strychnine was in force in the located period account. The treatment can be discussed as dangerous performance assistance and a later anti-doping-history precursor, but not as a 1904 rule violation unless the actual rules say so.

## Working verdict

The narrowest defensible account is: on 30 August 1904, an afternoon Olympic marathon in St. Louis sent a small field over a hilly, dusty road course in severe conditions described by the period report as 90°F in the shade, with restricted access to fresh water. Cars and handlers were part of the race environment. Fred Lorz crossed first after a reported car ride and was disqualified. Thomas Hicks was then recognised as winner in 3:28:53 after his handlers recorded oral strychnine, egg, brandy and warm-sponging treatment. Fourteen runners finished in the accepted period list. The dramatic details around dehydration intent, exact weather, starter count, injection, Carvajal's nap, dog distance and the finish-line assistance remain source-dependent.
