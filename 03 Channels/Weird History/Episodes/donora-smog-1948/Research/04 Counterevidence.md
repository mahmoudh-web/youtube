## Why counterevidence matters

The tempting story is simple: a zinc works and steel plant poisoned a town, a temperature inversion trapped one lethal chemical, and the event directly produced modern clean-air law. The located record is stronger and more interesting when its limits are retained. The following points constrain the story without denying the disaster.

## 1. The primary investigation did not identify one chemical culprit

The PHS abstract says sulfur dioxide, particulates and carbon monoxide were major contaminants and concludes that no single substance was responsible (S1). Its reproduced atmospheric summary identifies different major sources for different pollutants and says the distribution of sulfur dioxide and particulate matter indicates multiple sources (S3, lines 6242–6321). The sulfur-oxide criteria review likewise says no measurements were taken during the episode and that the observed effects were attributed to a chemical agent together with particulate matter, with sulfur dioxide and oxidation products significant (S14, lines 11841–11858).

**Control:** “industrial emissions” is defensible; “hydrogen fluoride killed everyone” or “sulfur dioxide alone killed everyone” is not supported by this pack. The report’s biological summary also found no evidence of excessive fluoride inhalation or ingestion in the community (S3, lines 6209–6212), which is direct counterevidence to a single-fluoride explanation.

## 2. Industry was central, but not the only source category

The source analysis names zinc smelters and steel operations as major contributors, while also identifying domestic heating systems, steam locomotives and steamboats as significant contributors (S3, lines 6242–6275). The PHS recommendations address all of those sources (S3, lines 6336–6370). A resident’s letter focused on the zinc works because it was visible, long-standing and associated with damaged paint and vegetation; that is important testimony, not a neutral source-apportionment study (S5, lines 45–54).

**Control:** distinguish “the mills were major contributors” from “one company was solely responsible.” Retain the lived experience and the source-analysis caveat together.

## 3. The inversion was a mechanism, not an independent cause

The weather did not create pollutants. The PHS meteorological conclusions say a combination of high stability and stagnation was necessary and sufficient for accumulation in the valley (S3, lines 6045–6059 and 6305–6308). The source summary requires nearby active sources, favorable terrain, humidity and seasonal conditions (S3, lines 6108–6122).

**Control:** say “weather trapped and concentrated emissions,” not “the weather caused the deaths” in a way that erases emissions, or “the factories caused the disaster” in a way that erases exposure conditions.

## 4. The count is stable at the PHS level but unstable in later retellings

The PHS report counted 20 deaths during or shortly after the smog (S1; S3, lines 6177–6178). PHMC separately describes 19 deaths in a 24-hour period and reproduces a contemporary letter referring to 20 deaths across Donora and Webster (S5, lines 13–14 and 45–49). The local historical society uses 21, while a later JAMA retrospective uses 22 (S16, S19). These may reflect different geographic boundaries, delayed deaths, or later counting choices; the located records do not yet reconcile them.

**Control:** use 20 with “the PHS report counted,” and list 21/22 only as unresolved later variants. Never imply that the higher figures have been disproven or that they are interchangeable.

## 5. “Five days” and the first day vary by source definition

The PHS meteorological section labels the episode 25–31 October, EPA’s handbook starts 27 October, and Pennsylvania Heritage says the smog enveloped Donora on 26 October (S3, S6, S7). This is not necessarily a factual contradiction: a low-level smoky period, the severe episode and the health crisis may have different onset definitions.

**Control:** narrate “late October 1948” until daily observations and contemporary newspapers establish a defensible day-by-day sequence.

## 6. The acute study cannot answer every long-term question

The original report studied the acute event and included mortality and morbidity context. A later follow-up examined mortality from 1948–1957 and morbidity in 1957 among members of the original study population (S11, S13). Modern discussion reports higher later cardiovascular and cancer outcomes in some comparisons (S12), but these are observational associations with confounding risks such as prior disease, age, smoking, housing, employment and continuing exposure.

**Control:** say “later studies reported an association” and identify the study. Do not add the later outcome to the 1948 death toll or make an individual-level causal claim.

## 7. The event influenced policy, but no single legislative chain is proven

Truman’s 1950 message explicitly used Donora as evidence for air-pollution research and local laws (S10, lines 15–20). EPA’s legal history then identifies federal milestones in 1955, 1963, 1967 and 1970 (S9). The records support influence and policy attention. They do not establish that Donora alone caused any one act, nor that the 1970 EPA framework was simply an immediate response to the 1948 episode.

**Control:** use “helped make air pollution a national public-health issue” or “became a landmark case in the policy conversation.” Keep statutory history separate from episode history.

## 8. Litigation is not the same as a finding of responsibility

Smithsonian reports that lawsuits were filed against American Steel and Wire and later settled without assessing blame (S15, lines 27–29). That is a useful lead, but the source is retrospective and does not provide the docket, settlement instrument, admissions, releases or allocation among claimants.

**Control:** do not say the company was “found guilty,” “cleared,” or “paid for killing” residents. Locate the primary court record before using legal language or settlement figures.

## Source-bias map

| Source cluster | What it does well | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| 1949 PHS report and later EPA reproductions | Acute epidemiology, source categories, atmospheric stability, recommendations | A complete real-time pollution record; sole chemical or legal responsibility |
| Pennsylvania State Archives / PHMC | Contemporary resident testimony, surviving state-file leads, administrative response | Neutral source apportionment or a final mortality reconciliation without the underlying reports |
| EPA legal and historical pages | Statutory chronology and national institutional memory | Donora’s precise causal weight in each statute |
| Truman’s 1950 message and Congressional Record | Evidence of political attention and public framing | Proof that a speech or debate caused enacted law |
| Ciocco/Thompson follow-up and modern review | Long-term study design and later associations | Individual-level causation or an expansion of the 1948 acute death count |
| Local society and retrospective journalism | Community memory, human response, reception and leads | Independent confirmation of disputed numbers, chemical attribution or legal outcome |

## Research implication

The counterevidence narrows the episode rather than weakening it: Donora was a real acute public-health disaster in which industrial and other emissions accumulated under unusual weather. The record is strongest on the scale of illness, the meteorological mechanism and the need for controls; it is weaker on a single chemical, a single responsible actor, the exact mortality definition beyond the PHS count, and a direct legal or legislative verdict.
