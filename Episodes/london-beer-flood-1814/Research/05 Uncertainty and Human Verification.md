## Research stop condition

This is an evidence pack, not a script. Stop at this handoff until a human editor or historian has checked the period newspaper scans, the inquest wording, the street geography, and the conflicting quantities below.

## Primary-source and archive checks

1. Retrieve the original scans for the *Morning Post* report **“The Catastrophe at Mr Meux's — Coroner's Inquest”**, 20 October 1814, and the *Times*, *Morning Chronicle*, *Evening Mail*, *London Courier*, *Caledonian Mercury*, and *Kentish Gazette* reports listed in the newspaper bibliography. Compare the reproduced text against the images, including names, times, units, and the verdict.
2. Ask The London Archives or the relevant Middlesex record custodian whether a coroner's return, inquest paper, jury view, depositions, or workhouse/parish record survives for George Hodgson's October 1814 inquest. The National Archives guide warns that local records are often the only route for post-mid-eighteenth-century inquests and that newspapers may be the only surviving account.
3. Locate a period map, preferably Horwood or another 1814 map, and plot the brewery, Banbury/Bainbridge Street, New Street, George Street, Great Russell Street, the Tavistock Arms, and the named house numbers. Do not infer a precise flow route from a modern street map alone.
4. Verify whether the “Horse Shoe,” “Horseshoe,” and “Meux and Co.” names refer to the same premises throughout the reports and note period/current street-name changes.

## Mechanics and quantity checks

1. Keep **3,555 barrels** as the reported content of the failed vat, not automatically as the total released.
2. Reconcile the neighbouring-vat figure reported as approximately **2,100** in one transcription and **2,400** in another, with about **800** remaining.
3. Reconcile the total-loss figures **7,664 barrels**, **8,000–9,000 barrels**, **over 320,000 gallons**, and **about 1.5 million litres**. Record the original unit, source, conversion convention, and whether the figure includes the cascade.
4. Have a brewing historian or engineer review the period account before using “hydrostatic pressure,” “fermentation pressure,” “domino effect,” “tidal wave,” or “blast” as explanatory language. The source directly supports rupture, pressure damage, slipping hoops/rivets, falling masonry, and cascading vessel loss; it does not supply a modern failure analysis.
5. Resolve the hoop-unit reading (“700 weight,” “700 cwt,” or “700 pounds”) from a scan. Until resolved, omit the hoop mass.

## Victim and inquest checks

1. Verify names and ages from the original issue: Elenor/Eleanor Cooper; Mary Mulvey; Thomas Murry/Murray; Hannah Banfield/Bamfield; Sarah Bates; Ann Saville; Elizabeth Smith; Catherine/Catharine Butler. Butler's age appears as 63 or 65 in later transcriptions.
2. Confirm the exact relationship and location details: Cooper at the Tavistock Arms; Smith and Bates in New Street; Mulvey, Murry and Butler in the recovery account; Banfield's mother and the other child; Saville's recovery among the butts.
3. Establish whether the five-person wake grouping appears in the original 1814 article or only in later reports. Do not merge a wake narrative with the inquest list without attribution.
4. Resolve the contradiction about Hannah Banfield's mother: the inquest excerpt says she was taken to hospital; another report is said to describe her as dead.
5. Keep the contemporary inquest total at **eight** unless a contemporaneous, independently verified record proves a ninth death. Historic England's current nine-death summary is a reception/source-history flag, not a correction of the inquest.
6. Ask a legal historian to explain the 1814 meaning of “Died by Casualty, accidentally, and by misfortune” before translating it into “Act of God,” “no liability,” or “no compensation.”

## Social and ethical handling

- “Rookery” was a historical label; UCL's work shows both real deprivation and later stereotype. Describe the physical conditions and evidence, not a caricature of residents.
- Do not infer that all victims were Irish, Catholic, drunk, or attending the wake because the neighbourhood had strong Irish/Catholic connections.
- The victims were overwhelmingly women and children in the available list. Do not make the dark porter or the “free beer” motif eclipse their names and circumstances.
- Avoid using the alleged ninth alcohol-poisoning death, mass drunkenness, hospital riot, or body-viewing stories without a source label and an explicit uncertainty flag.
