## Why “the internet would collapse” is not a historical conclusion

| Simplified claim | Counterevidence or qualification | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| “The Carrington Event knocked out the world’s communications.” | The primary/near-primary record concerns 1859 telegraph lines. Loomis records a mixed pattern: interruptions, abnormal currents, sparks, fires, shocks and battery-free operation on selected lines. | Reject as an unqualified historical claim. Say which lines and reports are meant. |
| “Every telegraph caught fire.” | Loomis’s list includes fires in selected places, but also lines that continued to communicate, lines that were merely difficult to operate and lines whose currents varied with direction. | Reject. Preserve location-specific reports. |
| “The aurora powered telegraphs everywhere.” | The Boston–Portland exchange is a reported case; other lines were unusable or intermittently affected. Current strength depended on line geometry, length, grounding, equipment and time. | Use only with attribution and scope. |
| “The wires generated free electricity from the aurora.” | Contemporary writers described an electrical current; modern language explains geomagnetically induced currents from changing magnetic fields. The 1859 authors did not have the modern mechanism. | Use “storm-induced/auroral current” with a source and avoid present-day textbook certainty in a period voice. |
| “Carrington saw the CME hit the Sun.” | Carrington observed a white-light solar outburst; “CME” is a later interpretation and no 1859 coronagraph existed. | Say “solar flash/white-light flare” for the observation; label CME as inferred. |
| “The 1 September flare and 2 September storm were one instantaneous event.” | Kew’s record distinguishes a short 1 September disturbance from a major storm beginning on 2 September; the wider sequence includes a 28 August precursor. | Preserve the sequence and time conventions. |
| “The storm was measured exactly by a modern index.” | Historical photographic magnetograms and later digitisation allow reconstruction, but scaling, baseline choice, time standards and model assumptions matter. | Attribute any nT/Dst or flare-class estimate to the specific modern study. |
| “It was the largest solar flare in history.” | The event is often called the largest geomagnetic storm in recorded history, while exact flare magnitude is reconstructed and depends on the metric. | Prefer “one of the most extreme recorded geomagnetic storms” unless a source defines the comparison. |
| “The event was just a beautiful aurora.” | Telegraph reports document shocks, sparks, fires, heated coils, chemical effects and communication failures in selected locations. | Reject as incomplete. Include both spectacle and infrastructure effects. |
| “A Carrington-level storm today would definitely destroy the grid.” | NOAA describes possible impacts; Lloyd’s/AER models conditional North American outage and transformer scenarios with explicit assumptions. | Use “could,” name the model and state the geographic/assumption limits. |
| “A Carrington-level storm today would definitely collapse the internet.” | The cited modern sources discuss power grids, HF radio, GPS/GNSS, satellites and communications, but do not establish a universal internet-collapse outcome. | Reject. Treat as an unverified analogy. |
| “The modern risk is just the 1859 telegraph problem scaled up.” | Modern systems have different conductors, transformers, satellites, radio links, redundancy and mitigation; risk is pathway-specific. | Explain the analogy as a prompt for modelling, not a direct scale conversion. |
| “A 1989 blackout proves a Carrington storm would cause the same outage.” | Quebec 1989 is an observed comparison event, not a duplicate storm; its grid, latitude, geology, duration and mitigation context differ. | Use 1989 as a bounded precedent only. |
| “The 20–40 million and 1–2 year numbers are what happened in 1859.” | Those figures are from a 2013 North American model scenario and are repeated by later NOAA summaries. | Keep them in the modern-inference lane and cite Lloyd’s/AER. |

## Source and perspective problems

- Carrington’s paper is a direct observation, but the most important causal connection was developed later. His own published caution about “hastily connecting” the solar and magnetic events should remain visible.
- Stewart’s Kew paper is an observatory record and an early theory paper. The photographic traces are primary evidence; the solar-current explanation is a historical interpretation, not a modern CME measurement.
- Loomis’s 1860–61 papers are unusually valuable because they compile reports from telegraph superintendents, operators, observatories and newspapers. They are still a compilation: the source chain must be retained for each dramatic incident.
- Newspaper language can be vivid, local and copied across papers. “Read a newspaper by the aurora” documents a report of brightness; it does not measure luminosity without a controlled comparison.
- The Boston–Portland exchange is often retold as a two-hour proof that the “aurora powered the telegraph.” The responsible formulation is that a contemporary report says operators disconnected batteries and continued communication on the induced current, pending scan and provenance verification.
- “Fires” may refer to paper, wood, sparks or equipment in different accounts. Do not convert every spark, hot coil or scorched smell into a building fire.
- “CME,” exact flare class, exact Dst, energy and arrival time are modern reconstructions. They should be attributed to the modern paper and not projected backward into the voices of Carrington, Stewart or Loomis.
- Modern loss estimates differ because grid topology, transformer inventory, ground conductivity, magnetic-field time series, mitigation and outage assumptions differ. No single scenario is the forecast.

## Required counterevidence search

1. Compare the Carrington and Hodgson scans with the OCR text; record any time or wording corrections.
2. Read Stewart’s original plates and the historical time-convention note; keep the Kew trace and later interpretation separate.
3. Retrieve the original reports cited by Loomis for Boston, Pittsburgh, Washington, Springfield, Norway, France, Germany and Switzerland; classify direct report, superintendent summary, newspaper or later compilation.
4. Inspect the New York Times and Springfield *Weekly Republican* scans before quoting the Boston–Portland dialogue or any fire/shock anecdote.
5. Check the full BGS dataset documentation and compare one or more digitised traces with the photographic source before presenting numerical magnetic values.
6. Read the National Academies report and Lloyd’s/AER model sections on assumptions, transformer damage and outage duration; record that the outputs are conditional.
7. Compare NOAA/NWS language about possible modern impacts with the actual historical evidence; remove any sentence that silently crosses from observed to inferred.
