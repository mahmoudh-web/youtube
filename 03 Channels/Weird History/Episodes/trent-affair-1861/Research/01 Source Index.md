## Research question

Why did a celebrated Union naval capture become a diplomatic liability, and how close did the United States and Great Britain come to armed conflict before the Lincoln administration released James Mason and John Slidell?

## Conflict and episode promise

On 8 November 1861, Captain Charles Wilkes of USS *San Jacinto* stopped the British mail steamer *Trent* in the Old Bahama Channel and removed Confederate commissioners James Mason and John Slidell, who were travelling to seek British and French recognition. The seizure was popular in the United States, but Britain treated it as an affront to its neutral flag. The crisis combined an unsettled maritime-law question, slow transatlantic communication, public pressure, military preparations around Canada and the Western Atlantic, financial anxiety, and the Union’s strategic need to avoid adding Britain to its Civil War enemies.

The safest provisional promise is not “the capture almost caused a war” as an inevitability. It is: a naval act celebrated as a Union victory forced both governments to decide whether legal principle, national honour, or strategic survival would control the next move.

## Source-quality key

- **A — contemporary primary record:** diplomatic despatch, official parliamentary record, government publication, or manuscript produced during the crisis.
- **B — official/archival record:** government or archival institution’s historical account, catalogue or finding aid pointing to primary material.
- **C — scholarly or institutional secondary history:** useful for synthesis and source criticism; not a substitute for the underlying record.
- **D — later popular/retrospective account:** lead-finding or reception evidence only.

## Primary-source retrieval queue

1. Read the complete S11 scan and compare it with direct copy S18. Identify the exact dates, authors, enclosures and publication sequence for Russell’s demand, Lyons’s delivery, Seward’s response and the final British acceptance.
2. Retrieve Captain Wilkes’s report, Lieutenant Fairfax’s account, Navy Secretary Gideon Welles’s orders, and *San Jacinto* logs. Record the interception position, warning shots, boarding sequence, number of prisoners and whether the ship was searched for papers.
3. Locate the British Foreign Office and War Office files for the 30 November demand, the softened instructions after Prince Albert’s intervention, orders to Canada and Western Atlantic naval dispositions. Separate planned, ordered, sailed and arrived forces.
4. Inspect the 1861 House resolution thanking Wilkes and the Navy Department’s commendation. Record whether a medal was proposed, passed, or merely reported, rather than repeating the popular “Congress thanked him” shorthand.
5. Read the original Seward note in S11 alongside Earl Russell’s legal response in S4. Keep distinct: right to stop/search, status of agents/despatches as contraband, right to remove people, treatment of the *Trent*, and form of reparation.
6. Compare contemporary British, Northern U.S., Southern U.S. and Canadian newspapers. Record publication date, location, editorial stance and whether an item is original reporting, reprint, rumour or caricature.
7. Verify the release chronology against Fort Warren records and the *Rinaldo* log: 1 January transfer/release, 3 January departure, and later arrival dates should not be collapsed.
