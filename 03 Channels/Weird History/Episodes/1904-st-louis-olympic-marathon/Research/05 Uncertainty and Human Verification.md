## Research stop condition

This is an evidence pack, not a script. Stop here until a human editor or historian checks the period newspaper scans, the rules, the route map, weather record and medical wording. No material claim should advance to narration, visual generation or publication merely because it appears in a popular retelling.

## Primary-source and archive checks

1. Inspect the original [St. Louis Post-Dispatch issue/map](https://archive.org/details/per_st-louis-post-dispatch_st-louis-post-dispatch_1904-08-30_57_9/page/n5/mode/1up) and the 31 August–2 September reports cited by Genty: *St. Louis Republic*, *Boston Daily Globe*, *Kansas City Times*, *Yonkers Herald*, *Chicago Tribune* and *Courier-Journal*. Compare names, start count, start/finish times, road miles, water points and Lorz's own explanation.
2. Locate the period programme, marathon rules and any official protest or disqualification record. Confirm whether assistance by cars, pacing, food, drinks and drugs were governed by separate rules in 1904.
3. Search the IOC Historical Archives, Missouri Historical Society and Library of Congress for organising-committee papers, James E. Sullivan's Department of Physical Culture report, W. J. McGee correspondence and any direct statement that the water restriction was designed to test “purposeful dehydration.”
4. Plot the course from the period map, not only a modern road map. Verify Francis Field, Clayton, Des Peres/Fourteen-Mile House, Ballas Road, Manchester Road, Denny Road and the North and South Road, and note where the period mileage begins/ends.
5. Check the 1905 Lucas scan against the HTML transcription, especially “one place” for water, the 90°F observation, the one-sixtieth-grain doses, the “caught” finish wording and the list of finishers.
6. Establish whether the 1909 Sullivan statistic’s 29 starters is an OCR error, a different counting convention or a genuine later correction.

## Weather and course checks

1. Preserve Lucas's 90°F-in-the-shade claim as a period observation until it can be compared with the original newspaper and the St. Louis station log.
2. Ask a weather historian to reconcile the period figure with NWS/NCEI data that are reported differently by modern aggregators. Record station, time, exposure, units and whether a value is observed, reconstructed or converted.
3. Verify the official water layout: Lucas's single 12-mile well versus later accounts' six-mile water tower plus 12-mile well. Do not write “only one water station” until the rule/programme and newspaper scans agree.
4. Reconcile 24 miles 1,500 yards, 40 km, 24.85 miles, 25 miles and Lucas's “trifle over twenty-three miles” before calculating pace, route length or “slowest” comparisons.

## Medical and safety checks

1. Ask a sports-medicine or toxicology reviewer to distinguish what was recorded from what can be inferred: oral strychnine, egg, brandy, distilled/warm sponging, weakness, hallucination, weight loss and post-race examination.
2. Resolve the conflict between Lucas's two oral one-sixtieth-grain doses and later accounts of a one-milligram injection. Until resolved, do not depict a syringe or state an injection as fact.
3. Do not call strychnine safe, therapeutic or “rat poison” without context. The first is medically misleading; the second may be anachronistic; the third is a modern shorthand for a toxic substance with a different period use.
4. Keep Garcia's hemorrhage as a period report. Do not state that dust definitively tore his stomach lining or that he would certainly have died without the exact reported intervention unless a hospital record is found.
5. Do not diagnose Hicks with heatstroke, dehydration, strychnine poisoning, hallucination or organ failure from Lucas's descriptive account alone.

## Person, race and historical-language checks

1. Verify the spelling and identity of Hicks, Lorz, Corey, Newton, Carvajal, Garcia, Taunyane and Mashiani across the period scans and result databases.
2. Keep the Lorz car ride, his statement about the broken-down car, the “joke” explanation and the sanctions as separate attributed claims. Confirm the final sanction from AAU records rather than combining “one year,” “life” and “commuted.”
3. Separate the official/support cars around Hicks from Lorz's ride. Verify the reported protest and the applicable pacing rule.
4. Treat Carvajal's peaches as Lucas's account and the apples/nap/travel story as unverified until a primary passage is found.
5. Verify dog count, detour distance and whether period photographs support barefoot claims. Do not state that Taunyane and Mashiani were barefoot or identify them with a colonial racial label without a source and terminology review.
6. Flag Lucas's “Caucasian race” and “Kaffir” language as historical racism. Do not repeat it in narrator voice; if quoted for source criticism, add context and avoid using it to describe people.

## Legal, ethical and editorial sensitivity

- **Defamation:** no living individuals are at issue, but “fraud,” “cheating,” “drugged” and “negligent” still require attribution and rule/legal context when describing historical reputations.
- **Medical misinformation:** high sensitivity. Historical drug use is not medical advice, and a dramatic dose should not be presented without unit/source verification.
- **Historical casualty/harm:** Garcia's near-fatal illness and the runners' suffering should not be used as a joke or as evidence that the event was entertainingly “crazy.”
- **Race and colonial framing:** the 1904 exposition's racial categories shaped the official account. Avoid exoticising the South African and Cuban runners or treating their clothing, food and language as spectacle.
- **Visual rights and authenticity:** period scans and photographs may be public domain, but confirm the archive's reproduction terms and credit requirements. Do not fabricate a newspaper front page, route, syringe scene or finish-line carry as if it were archival evidence.
