## Evidence-bounded answer

In September 1950, the U.S. military conducted a documented biological-warfare vulnerability test in the San Francisco Bay Area. The formal report identified six trials between 20 and 27 September, an offshore ship, 43 sampling locations, and the use of *Bacillus globigii*, *Serratia marcescens* and fluorescent particles. Its stated purpose was to test whether an offshore aerosol could reach a seaport, measure the defensive problem and study downwind behaviour. It concluded that an offshore seaport attack was feasible and that meteorological conditions mattered greatly.

The test was carried out over an unwitting civilian population. The located report and 1977 Senate record document environmental sampling, but do not establish a public-health monitoring plan. At the time, Army witnesses said the two bacterial simulants were believed nonpathogenic. Later medical knowledge showed that *Serratia* could cause serious opportunistic disease, especially in compromised patients, and later Army policy restricted releases where hospitals or sanitariums could be affected.

Soon afterward, Stanford physicians reported eleven *Serratia* infections in patients over a roughly six-month period, including one fatal bacterial-endocarditis case. The timing and location create a serious association. But the medical report also recorded urinary-tract manipulation in every case and did not identify the source. The Army argued that the cluster was compatible with hospital-acquired infection, and later strain analysis did not establish a match between the military test strain and examined clinical outbreaks. The Stanford isolates were reportedly not preserved for comparison.

Edward Nevin's family later alleged that the military release caused his death. The Ninth Circuit recorded that allegation and held that selecting the site and bacterial strain fell within the Federal Tort Claims Act's discretionary-function exemption, remanding for dismissal on jurisdictional grounds. That legal outcome is not proof that no risk existed or that the release was ethically acceptable; it also is not a scientific strain-match finding.

## Provisional interpretation

The strongest interpretation is that the episode shows a gap between technical success and public safety: military records demonstrate that an offshore aerosol could be dispersed and measured over a city, while the same record set leaves civilian consent, medical surveillance and individual causation unresolved. The story is therefore not “a harmless test definitely killed a man,” nor “the illness was proven unrelated.” It is a documented unwitting exposure followed by an unusual hospital cluster whose causal chain the surviving evidence cannot close.

## Safest provisional wording

> In September 1950, U.S. military researchers tested whether a ship offshore could spread biological simulants across San Francisco. Their report said the experiment showed an offshore attack on a seaport was feasible. Days later, Stanford doctors began recording an unusual cluster of *Serratia* infections, including one death. The dates are documented; the causal link is not. The medical paper pointed to urinary procedures as a possible route, later testing found no proven match to the military strain, and a court ultimately addressed the government's legal immunity rather than settling the science.

This wording avoids a proven-kill claim, a settled exposure total, a single-agency label, an objective “mass human experiment” superlative and a legal conclusion beyond the record.

## What the record does not prove

- That the military test strain caused any Stanford infection or Edward Nevin's death.
- That the San Francisco test was a single release, a single day or an operation with one settled ship and command chain.
- That every resident inhaled a verified dose of viable *Serratia*.
- That “Operation Sea-Spray” was the official 1950/1951 code name rather than a later label.
- That the test was solely a Navy operation or solely an Army operation.
- That officials had no knowledge whatsoever of possible risk; the 1950 literature and approval files remain to be checked.
- That the test permanently changed Bay Area microbial ecology or caused later infections.
- That the Ninth Circuit decision established scientific exoneration or historical ethical approval.
- That the event was an operational biological-weapons attack rather than a vulnerability test using simulants.
