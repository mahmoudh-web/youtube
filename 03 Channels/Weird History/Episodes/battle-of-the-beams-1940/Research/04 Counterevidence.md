## Counterevidence register

| Popular or tempting claim | Evidence that complicates it | Safe treatment |
|---|---|---|
| “The Germans used radar beams.” | The located sources describe radio-navigation signals and beam receivers, while radar appears in the broader air-defence context. | Call them radio-navigation beams; explain that detection and direction-finding are separate from radar interception. |
| “The British bent the beams.” | Price says unsynchronised Morse dashes were the normal Knickebein method and that deliberate beam aiming was not used; occasional signal interactions might have sounded like bending. | Use “interfered with,” “spoofed” or “confused” unless a primary record supports literal beam steering. |
| “A single June flight discovered everything.” | The intelligence trail includes Y Service intercepts, technical examination, agents, captured aircrew and aircraft evidence; NSA and official-history narratives compress the flight sequence differently. | Present discovery as a chain, with the Anson flight as a confirmation milestone. |
| “Bromide was operational on 5 September and on Coventry in the same proven form.” | RAF Museum dates first use against “Ruffian” beams to 5 September, while Price says the first X-Gerät BROMIDE jammers entered service by mid-November and describes a modulation mismatch at Coventry. | Keep the date/code-name discrepancy visible; do not silently harmonise it. |
| “Bromide failed because British science was incompetent.” | Price’s account points to incomplete knowledge of X-Gerät modulation and a captured receiver leading to modification; it also says later results cannot be assigned to jamming alone. | Describe an intelligence/engineering learning problem, not a character judgement. |
| “Bromide alone defeated German pathfinders after Coventry.” | Price reports only four effective X-Gerät-jamming occasions and identifies moonlight, target characteristics, marking limitations, air defences, crew losses and German redeployment as additional factors. | Attribute the later reassessment and say effectiveness was mixed and system-dependent. |
| “The beams gave pinpoint bombing.” | Official histories describe accuracy as system-dependent; Price distinguishes beam alignment from actual bomb/marking accuracy and says KGr 100’s results were not theoretically precise. | Avoid false precision; explain that navigation aid, release calculation and bomb dispersion are different things. |
| “Knickebein, X-Gerät and Y-Gerät were successive versions of one system.” | The sources describe different receiver requirements and operating principles: Knickebein equisignal beams, X-Gerät multi-beam automatic release, Y-Gerät transponder/range control. | Treat them as related but distinct systems. |
| “The Battle of the Beams was a formal 1940 operation.” | The located named operation is “Cold Water”; “Wizard War” and “Battle of the Beams” function as retrospective umbrella terms. | Use the episode title as a qualified label, not as a claimed wartime codename. |
| “British intelligence knew exactly where every beam was.” | AIR 2/5238 records continuous watch and uncertainty about target areas; 80 Wing records and exact station files remain to be inspected. | Say intelligence progressively identified routes and stations, not that it had complete visibility. |

## Alternative causal models for Coventry and later outcomes

### Model A — Countermeasure success

The conventional account is that British intelligence identified the new X-Gerät, BROMIDE was corrected after the Coventry failure, and German precision-pathfinder effectiveness declined. S4 supports this as the contemporary/post-Coventry British interpretation, but S5 qualifies it.

### Model B — Operational and environmental limits

Poor weather and lack of moonlight, small KGr 100 force size, limited marker tactics, target differences, ordinary air defences and loss of experienced crews could reduce German results even when X-Gerät was not effectively jammed. S5 is the main support; it is a later specialist analysis, not a complete statistical audit.

### Model C — Deception and distrust

Even when signals remained audible, jamming could reveal that Britain knew the beam’s existence and location, making crews fear that night fighters might follow the route. S3 and S5 support this as Price’s interpretation and post-war aircrew testimony. It should not be stated as a universally measured cause without German operational records.

### Model D — German adaptation and British code-name lag

Spoof beams and late activation of real X-Gerät beams may have caused British jammers to attack decoys. S5 attributes this to Hubert Langerfeld’s post-war account and Price’s reading of raid reports. This is a strong research lead but requires the German records and British 80 Wing reports.

## Research alternatives still open

- **Archive-first reconstruction:** inspect AIR 2/5238, AIR 20/1626–1627, AIR 20/1669–1671, AIR 41/46, HW 2/47 and WO 208/3506 before treating code words, dates or technical parameters as settled.
- **Equipment-first reconstruction:** locate photographs, receiver examinations and technical reports for Knickebein/X-Gerät receivers; distinguish equipment capability from actual raid performance.
- **Raid-report reconstruction:** compare KGr 100 operational reports with British 80 Wing assessments and raid damage, rather than inferring effectiveness from Coventry alone.
- **Intelligence-history reconstruction:** trace the June intercept, Cabinet discussion, Ultra handling and source-protection constraints to explain why later public accounts are compressed.
